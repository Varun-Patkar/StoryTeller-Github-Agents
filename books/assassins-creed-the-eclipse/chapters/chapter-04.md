# Chapter 4: The House of the First

The city had no walls, and somehow that was worse than if it had.

Cain lay in the reeds at the river's edge and looked at the thing the masters had built and understood, all over again, why his species had spent so long on its knees. Walls were what you built when you were afraid of something. The Isu had built no walls because, until very recently, there had been nothing on the earth they needed to fear.

The towers went up out of the plain like they'd grown there, pale and smooth and joined by spans of light that carried no one, because the ones who lived here didn't walk when they could simply decide to be elsewhere. There were no gates to storm. No line of defenders. Just the open, humming beauty of a place that had never once expected to be attacked.

"It's wrong," Nara said beside him, very quietly. She had her bow across her back and her hunter's stillness on, but her eyes kept flinching away from the towers as if looking too long might cost her something. "Nothing living made that."

"Something living made it," Cain said. "That's the problem."

Downriver, the rebellion was making its noise.

He'd given Adam a simple job, which was the only kind Adam liked: hit the southern approach, loud, and keep hitting it. Draw whatever thin garrison remained toward the gate that mattered, the one everyone could see. Bleed a little, make it look real, and under no circumstances break through until Cain sent word.

Adam thought he was buying time for a flanking force. There was no flanking force. There was Cain, and there was Nara, and there was the low black arch at the waterline that Cain had known would be there before he'd ever seen this place, because he had walked its map in a game, in a warm room, in a life that felt further away every day.

"That's our door," he said, and slid down into the water.

____

Inside, the city was empty in a way that made the back of his neck crawl.

Not abandoned. Empty. Everything worked. Light moved along the walls when they passed, following them, polite and attentive, and doors he had no name for folded open before he could wonder how. The air was clean and cool and smelled of nothing at all. It was a house with every lamp lit and every meal warm and no one home, and the wrongness of it sat heavier on him than any garrison would have.

He knew why. That was the part he couldn't say to Nara, or to Adam, or to anyone with a face that could still be read across a table.

They were all somewhere else. Every master who mattered, every mind in this whole shining anthill, was bent over the same problem, and the problem was the sky, and they were losing to it. The war with the humans had simply stopped being interesting. You didn't post guards on your grain when the whole barn was already burning.

"Where are the people," Nara whispered.

"Praying," Cain said. "In their way." He kept moving.

He led her down, always down, past chambers that opened onto drops he couldn't see the bottom of, past a hall where something vast and slow turned in the dark and he did not stop to learn what it was.

Once they passed a gallery lined with tall clear columns, and inside the columns were people. Humans, floating, eyes closed, whole and unmarked and perfectly still, dozens of them held in the pale light like insects caught in amber. Nara made a small sound and her hand went for an arrow before she caught it. Cain didn't slow. He knew what they were, or near enough. Spare parts. Study subjects. A larder of bodies the masters kept the way a careful cook keeps salt, against the day they needed one. He filed the gallery's place in his head, deep, next to the vessel and the map of his own forever. A man who was going to need bodies one day did not walk past a room full of them without taking a note.

Nara stayed a pace behind him, silent, trusting the strange man who moved through a god's house like he'd been given the deed. She didn't ask how he knew the way. She'd stopped asking that kind of question a long time ago. It was one of the things he valued most about her, and he'd started, lately, to notice that he valued it, which was its own small inconvenience.

He found the room he wanted the way you find a word you've been reaching for. It was simply there, at the bottom of everything, and he knew it on sight.

____

It was a small room, for the house of gods. That was the first surprise.

The second was that it was beautiful in a way the rest of the city wasn't, not grand, not vast, but careful, the way a thing is careful when it matters more than it looks. A ring of low pillars. A basin of still light in the center, no bigger than a cradle. And around the walls, faint and constant, a lattice of moving symbols that his eyes couldn't hold but his newer, stranger sense could, the sense the body had, the one the masters had built into their cattle and never expected the cattle to turn on them.

This was it. The thing under the city. The reason he'd marched four hundred people through a war to stand in this exact spot.

He put his hand near the basin, not touching, and the light leaned toward him like it was curious, and the knowing rose up to meet it and told him what he was looking at.

A vessel. A place to pour a mind.

The Isu did not die the way people did. They had learned, a long time ago, to take the thing that made them themselves and lift it out of the failing body and set it down somewhere that didn't fail. A network. A grey country under the world where a consciousness could go on thinking long after its bones were dust. They used it for their scholars, their judges, their dead they weren't finished with. They used it, he would learn much later, for worse things than that.

Right now, in this small careful room, they were using it to try to save themselves from the sky, and it was not going to be enough, and Cain was going to take it anyway.

"Watch the door," he told Nara.

Then he reached into the basin with everything the body had, and the Isu's whole terrible archive opened in front of him like a flower.

____

He had thought he understood what was coming. He'd been wrong. Knowing a thing from the outside, the way you know the ending of a story, was nothing like standing inside the masters' own record while they screamed.

The light showed him their work. Not gently. It simply gave him everything at once, the way the knowing always did, and he stood in the middle of it with blood starting again at his nose and made himself look.

He saw three of them. The ones the record kept returning to, the ones the rest deferred to. A great weary figure the others answered, running the numbers on the end of the world over and over as if this time the sum might come out different.

Cain lingered on him longer than he meant to. Here was the master of masters, the one the whole record bent around, and what came off him was not cruelty. It was exhaustion. He had tried everything a mind could try, and the sky did not care what he tried, and he kept trying anyway, because stopping was the same as agreeing to die. Cain understood him better than he wanted to. He looked, in the light, like what Cain was about to become. A thing that would not quit even after quitting became the sane choice.

Beside him, a woman with a voice the record treated like a bell, arguing for the future, for messages, for leaving warnings in stone for whatever crawled out of the ash. And a third.

The third stopped him.

She was beautiful the way all of them were beautiful, but there was something in the record of her that the others didn't have, a cold that went all the way down. He watched her look at the projections of the dying earth, at the count of how many humans would be left when it was over, and he heard her speak, and the knowing gave him the sense of it even though the sounds were nothing his mouth could make.

*Let it burn,* she was saying, more or less. *Let it take the herd. We rebuild from what's left, and this time we build the leash so deep they never learn the word for no.*

The others argued her down. The record showed that too. But Cain stood in the light and looked at the cold woman who wanted the sky to do her thinning for her, and something in him went very quiet and very certain, the way it had gone in the grass by his brother, the way it had gone in Eve's tent.

He didn't know her name yet. He would. He filed her face the way he filed everything, but this one he filed near the front, in the place he kept for problems that were going to outlive everyone else's.

"Later," he told the cold woman in the light, and she did not hear him, because she was seventy-five thousand years of trouble away and had not yet noticed she had an enemy.

He turned back to the work.

____

The tech itself was almost simple, once he stopped being afraid of it.

That was the thing no story had prepared him for. The masters were gods, and their god-machine, at its heart, did one clean thing: it read a mind, all of it, every weight and grudge and memory, and it wrote that reading somewhere a body wasn't. That was all. Lift, and set down. He turned it over in the light and understood it the way he understood the Apple now, not because he was cleverer than the ones who'd built it, but because he wanted it more, and want, he was learning, was its own kind of key.

He took it. Not the machine, he couldn't carry a room. He took the knowing of it, poured the whole shape of it into the space in his head where the future already lived, the map of how to build a vessel of his own, somewhere deep, somewhere no master and no man would ever think to dig. A country of one. His own grey.

And standing there with the end of the world in one hand and his own immortality in the other, Cain let himself want the thing he'd been refusing to say plainly since the grass.

He was not going to die.

Not in the sky's fire, not in a master's fist, not in any of the small ordinary ways that took everyone. The world had handed him one name and one fact, the man who killed his brother, the first of all the dead-makers, and the world had marked him for it and turned away. Fine. Let the mark stay. In the oldest version of his story, the one from the book, the killer had been cursed to wander and could not be killed, protected by the very thing that damned him. A deathless man with the worst name in the world.

He could think of nothing he wanted more.

One mind in the vessel. His. He'd already known, in some buried way, that the exit had room for exactly one, and now he understood that the one was not a limit he regretted. It was the whole point. Immortality shared was just a longer kind of company, and he had not clawed his way out of a murder and a war to spend forever holding hands. He would be the one who lasted. Everyone else would be seed, and stock, and memory, and he would carry them the way a man carries a debt, because they were useful and because, occasionally, he almost meant it.

"Cain." Nara's voice, sharp, from the door. "Something changed."

He felt it a half-second after she said it. The polite attentive light in the walls had stopped following them. It had gone still, all of it, the way a room goes still when someone in it stops pretending to read and starts, quietly, to listen.

Far off, through the vessel's fading glow, he felt the cold woman's attention turn.

Not toward him. Not quite. Toward the wrongness of a thing that should not be in this room, a heat where there should be none, a hand in the archive that had no right to be a hand. She was distracted, and dying, and drowning in the end of everything, and even so, some part of her had noticed the anomaly, the way you notice a draft from a door you were sure you'd shut.

"Time to go," Cain said, and pulled his hand out of the light.

____

They went up faster than they'd come down, and the city had stopped being a house and started being a throat.

Doors that had folded open now waited a beat too long. The moving spans of light overhead had gone the wrong color. Twice, something that wasn't a person crossed a hall ahead of them, tall and wrong and searching, and both times Cain put his back to a pillar and his hand near the Apple and did the arithmetic on whether he could take a fight in a god's own house, and both times the answer was no, and both times, thank whatever there was to thank, it moved on.

Once it didn't move on. One of the tall wrong shapes stopped at the mouth of the hall they were crossing and simply stood, turning its head in slow arcs. Cain pressed Nara flat against the cold pillar with one arm and did not breathe. He could feel its attention sweeping the dark like a hand running along a shelf, patient, thorough. The Apple was a coal against his ribs. If it found them, he'd have one bad second to decide whether to spend himself fighting a god on its own ground, and he already knew how that ended. Then, unhurried, incurious, the shape moved on. Cain let the air out of his lungs a little at a time, so it wouldn't hear the shake in it.

Nara didn't panic. He'd have kept her for that alone. She moved when he moved and froze when he froze and put an arrow to her string without being told, and when they finally spilled out through the black arch into the river with the cold water closing over the wrongness behind them, she came up gasping and grinning, alive in the way only people who've just cheated it can grin.

"We robbed them," she said, disbelieving. "We walked into the masters' own house and robbed them."

"We did," Cain said.

"They didn't even see us. The whole time. Not once."

"They had bigger things to look at," Cain said. "That's the only reason anyone ever gets away with anything. Someone bigger is always looking somewhere else."

Downriver, the city's southern gate was burning, and Adam's people were pouring through it at last, because a light garrison of terrified gods with their minds on the sky had finally broken, and the rebellion was about to win the greatest victory it had ever known. They would sing about tonight. The story would run down every river and into every leashed village, the masters bled, the masters ran, and the leashes would start to matter less, exactly as he'd promised. All of it true. All of it real.

None of it the reason he'd come.

He treaded water and watched the city fall and felt the vessel's knowing sitting warm and complete in his head, the map of his own forever, and for once he let himself simply have the moment, because he'd earned it and because there weren't going to be many more.

Then he looked at Nara. At the grin, and the water in her lashes, and the honest arithmetic loyalty that was the closest thing to trust he'd let himself keep.

The sky was coming. Weeks, now. Days, maybe. It would take the city and the camp and the singing and nearly everything that breathed, and he had always known that, and he had let himself learn her name anyway.

He wasn't going to upload her. That door had room for one, and the one was him, and he'd made his peace with that before he'd made it with anything.

But there were other doors. Deep places. Stone and stores and dark. A man building a country for one still had to build it somewhere, and there was no rule that said he couldn't put a few worth-keeping things in the walls on his way down.

"Come on," he said, and started for the far bank, toward his victory and his ending and the small ugly mercy he was already, against his own better judgment, deciding to allow himself. "We've got somewhere to be before the sky notices we're gone."

Nara followed, because she always did, because the math favored it, and because she had no idea yet that she was the one thing on his long cold list of the future that he'd chosen for a reason he couldn't quite make add up. He decided not to examine it too closely. Some accounts, he was learning, ran better when you didn't audit them.
