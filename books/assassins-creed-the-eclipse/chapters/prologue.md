# Prologue: The Cost of Living

### 2021. Malibu. Cain Mercer's villa.

The grunting carried through the whole house.

Anyone walking up the drive who didn't know better might have thought they'd wandered into one of Tony Stark's parties, the kind that ended with a wrecked car in the pool and a lawsuit in the morning. There was no party. There was one man, flat on his back on a leather couch the size of a small boat, a mouse and keyboard balanced on his stomach, staring up at a wall that wasn't a wall anymore.

The whole thing was a screen. Ninety inches of it, floor to ceiling, and right now it was running Assassin's Creed Valhalla at a resolution that cost more than most people's cars.

"So the whole pantheon was two dead scientists arguing," Cain Mercer said to no one. "Odin backs himself up inside the Yggdrasil, Loki does the same from the other side, and three thousand years of mythology is just their divorce. That's a sharper ending than this game earned."

He said it the way a man reads a stock report he already expected to be good. Mild approval. No fireworks.

Cain was thirty-four and looked younger, which he'd paid for, and richer, which he hadn't needed to pay for. The face on the screen glare was sharp, symmetrical, the kind of handsome that opened doors before he said a word. He knew exactly how much that face was worth. He'd been keeping the books on it since he was six.

That was around the age the orphanage stopped being a place he lived and started being a place that taught him things. Nobody sat him down for lessons. He learned by watching. He learned that the kid who cried loudest got moved to the back of the line, and the kid who smiled at the right adult got the last cup of pudding. He learned that the system, the one with the caseworkers and the clipboards and the state seal on the letterhead, did not love you. It processed you. If you wanted something out of it, you figured out which lever it responded to and you pulled that one, over and over, until the machine gave.

He'd been left there as a baby with nothing but a blanket and a folded note. The note gave him a name. Cain. Whoever wrote it either had a cruel sense of humor or no sense of one at all. First murderer in the book, and they'd stapled it to an infant. He'd hated it for years. Then, somewhere around twelve, he'd stopped hating it and started thinking it fit.

The foster homes came and went. Three of them, then a fourth. He was never a bad kid in any way you could write down. He did his chores. He got the grades. What the families couldn't stand was the thing behind his eyes, the part of him that was always measuring. One foster sister told her mother he looked at people like they were furniture he was deciding whether to keep. She wasn't wrong. She just said it out loud, which was rude.

The couple who finally kept him were the Mercers. Rich, older, kind in the distracted way of people who had money and no children to spend it on. They couldn't have kids of their own, and by the time they found him they'd stopped trying to. He took their surname without any particular sentiment. It was a good name. It came with a good tax bracket. He kept the Cain because by then he'd decided he liked being underestimated by anyone who thought a name meant anything.

The Mercers taught him the second half of the lesson the orphanage started. The orphanage taught him that the world took from the weak. The Mercers, without ever meaning to, taught him how the world worked once you had leverage. He watched his adoptive father run a room without raising his voice. He watched a handshake move more money than most people saw in a lifetime. He watched how a smile, deployed at the right second, could make a man sign something he'd swore he never would.

So Cain built a smile. He practiced it the way other kids practiced free throws.

By the time he was seventeen he understood the one rule that ran underneath all the others, the rule nobody put on a poster because it didn't sound nice. What you are doesn't matter. What people think you are is everything. A saint the world believes is a fraud dies poor. A monster the world believes is a saint gets a wing of the hospital named after him.

Cain chose to be believed.

Through school he was the sunny one. The boy who tutored the kid failing chemistry, who organized the food drive, who remembered your birthday and your dog's name. Teachers wrote him letters. Classmates would have voted him most likely to save the world. Not one of them ever got close enough to see the man behind the smile, because the man behind the smile had decided, very early, that letting people close was a cost with no return.

He was cold. He knew he was cold. He'd made his peace with it before most people learned to drive.

But cold wasn't the same as cruel, and Cain drew that line in a place other people found confusing. He wasn't a man who hurt for pleasure. He didn't kick down for fun. He simply divided the world into two columns, and once you were sorted, you stayed sorted.

His people, the ones who were his, got the best of him. Loyalty like a vault. He paid them more than he had to. He covered their debts before they asked. He remembered which one had a sick mother and which one was terrified of losing the house, and he made those problems quietly disappear, and he never mentioned it again. Cross him and you'd find out he could ruin your life with the patience of a man watering a plant. But stand at his back, and there was nothing he wouldn't spend to keep you standing.

Everyone else was weather. Something to be read, used, and walked around. He'd take advantage of a stranger without losing a minute of sleep, because a stranger would have done the same to him, and at least Cain was honest with himself about it.

He made his money young and made it fast.

It started with a piece of software he wrote at nineteen, a small ugly thing that solved a boring problem for logistics companies, the kind of problem nobody wanted to look at because there was no glory in it. Cain had never cared about glory. He cared about the invoice. The tool worked, the contracts stacked up, and by twenty-three he'd folded it into a company with his name over the door and a valuation that made the business press write flattering things about a young man who was, they all agreed, going to change the world.

He didn't change the world. He acquired pieces of it. A fintech outfit here, a data firm there, each one bought at the exact moment the seller was too scared to hold and too tired to fight. He was very good at finding that moment. By thirty he ran a holding company the analysts couldn't quite classify, and the number next to his name had nine zeros behind it and was reaching, slowly and surely, for a tenth.

He was, by every metric the world used to measure a man, a success.

He was also, by any metric that mattered to a person and not a spreadsheet, alone.

He had ten thousand contacts and no friends. He had a phone that never stopped and no one on it he could call at three in the morning just to talk. His galas were full. His weddings-and-funerals list was long. Board members loved him. Journalists loved him. Women who liked the shape of his bank account loved a version of him he was careful to keep shallow.

Not one of them knew him. That was by design. Cain had run the numbers on friendship the same way he ran the numbers on everything, and friendship kept coming back a loss. People who knew you could hurt you. People who loved you could leave. The math was ugly, so he stopped doing it, and he built a life where every relationship had an exit and a price tag, and he told himself the quiet was the sound of winning.

Most nights he believed it.

The nights he didn't, he had games.

That was the one indulgence he never audited. Everything else in his life had a purpose, an angle, a return. The games were just his. He'd drop into someone else's world and be someone with no board meeting in the morning, no image to protect, no smile to hold. He'd played hundreds of them. He finished most. A few he came back to like a man visiting a grave.

His favorite wasn't the pretty one currently glowing on his wall. His favorite was old, ugly, half-broken on release and better for it. Vampire: The Masquerade, Bloodlines. He'd played it more times than he'd admit to a therapist he'd never hire.

He didn't love it for the combat, which was bad, or the levels, which were worse. He loved it for the thing sitting behind all of it. The oldest one. The first. Caine, the man the whole hidden world traced itself back to, who killed his brother in the second story humanity ever told and got, for his trouble, a curse that wouldn't let him die.

Cain used to think that was the trick of it. Everyone read the curse as punishment. He read it as a promotion.

Imagine it. Not the fangs, not the blood, the other part. To simply not end. To stand at the edge of every empire while it rose and screamed and fell, and to still be there, quiet, unbothered, when the last brick came down. To watch every war and take a cut of both sides. To outlast every man who ever underestimated the orphan with the cursed name, and to be there, unchanged, to watch them turn to dust.

He didn't want a throne. Thrones were targets, and targets got knives in them. He wanted the thing the throne could never buy. Time. All of it. He wanted to be the one who was still there.

"I want to be Cain," he'd said once, out loud, to an empty apartment years ago, and he'd laughed at himself and meant every word.

He glanced at the clock in the corner of the wall. Almost eleven. The gala was at three, which in his world meant a car at two-thirty, which meant he had a handful of hours and a body that wanted none of them.

The gala. He rubbed his eyes and felt the grit in them.

This was the part of the empire nobody warned you about. The image had to be fed. A man worth what Cain was worth couldn't simply vanish into a dark room and play dead games until his eyes bled. He had to be seen. Photographed shaking the right hands, laughing at the right jokes, writing the right check to the right cause with a journalist positioned to catch him doing it. The philanthropist. The one who gives back. It was the most valuable performance he did all year, and it exhausted a part of him that his money had never learned how to refill.

He set the mouse and keyboard on the floor and lay there a minute longer than he should have. His chest felt tight in a way he was getting used to and had decided to ignore. His heart did a thing, a small stumble, a skipped beat that it had started doing a few months back.

He'd meant to see someone about it. There was proof he'd meant to. A cardiology consult sat in his calendar, booked by an assistant back when the skipping first started, and he had dragged it forward so many times the reminder had become a private joke. It surfaced every couple of weeks. Cardiology, 9 a.m., and a little arrow he tapped to push it into some future month that was always going to be less busy than this one. The future was never less busy. He'd moved that appointment eleven times. He knew the number because the calendar kept count, and the calendar was the only thing in his life that ever told him the truth.

Doctors' appointments were three hours he could spend closing a deal, and the body, unlike a deal, had always just kept working.

He got maybe four hours of sleep a night. Sometimes three. He wore it like a medal. Sleep was time, and time was the only thing on earth Cain refused to waste, which was funny, when he thought about it later, in the last clear moment he'd get. He'd spent his whole life terrified of wasting time and had never once considered that the body doing the spending had a budget of its own.

He closed his eyes. Just for a minute. The gala could wait for a minute.

Sleep took him faster than he expected.

...

He woke in the dark, and something was wrong.

Not a sound. Not an intruder. His body. It had stopped feeling like his. His left arm was a stranger's arm, heavy and far away, and the tightness in his chest had climbed up his neck and set its teeth in his jaw. He tried to sit up and the room tilted and did not come back level.

The heart that had been skipping beats for months finally lost the rhythm entirely.

Cain had never panicked in his life. He didn't panic now. He did the thing he always did, the thing that had made him rich and kept him alone. He assessed.

He was dying. He knew it the way he knew a bad quarter from a good one, immediately and without argument. Something in the machinery had given out, and no amount of leverage was going to renegotiate this.

And in the strange, wide calm that comes when there's no deal left to close, Cain understood exactly why.

He'd spent his whole life measuring what things were worth. He'd built an empire out of buying assets other people were too scared or too tired to hold. And the one asset he'd owned outright since the day he was born, the one nobody could buy out from under him, the one thing that was actually, finally his, he had run into the ground for thirty-four years and never spent a cent maintaining.

The body. He'd starved it of sleep and called it discipline. He'd dragged the same warning forward eleven times and called it focus. He'd treated the one thing keeping him in the game like it was free, like it was furniture, like it was weather. He, who never let a stranger take advantage of him, had let his own flesh subsidize every deal he ever made, and now the note had come due all at once, in the dark, with no one in the house to hear it.

A billion dollars in the bank. Nine zeros reaching for the tenth. And not one of them could buy him the one thing he'd never bothered to protect.

He wanted to laugh. He didn't have the air for it.

His thoughts were going soft at the edges now, the room draining of color. And in that last narrowing space, one thought stayed sharp, the way one light stays on in a house going dark. It wasn't fear. Cain didn't do fear. It was the oldest want he had, the one he'd said out loud to an empty apartment years ago, and it rose up now clear and complete and absolutely certain.

He didn't want more money. Money had failed him. He didn't want another year, because a year was just a longer version of the trap he was already in, a body that spent itself down to nothing while he wasn't looking.

He wanted out of the trap entirely. He wanted the one thing the curse in his favorite game had really offered, under all the blood and the fangs. A body that didn't run out. Time that didn't have a budget. To not be a man betting everything on a heart that could quit in the dark. To simply, finally, not end.

"I wish," Cain thought, and his lips moved without sound, "I could be Cain."

The dark was almost total now. Somewhere very far away, in a place that was neither the room nor his skull, a voice answered him. It was patient. It was old. It did not sound surprised.

"So be it."

Cain Mercer stopped.

### The next morning.

"...billionaire and philanthropist Cain Mercer was found dead in his Malibu home early this morning by his household staff. He was thirty-four."

The anchor wore the face they all learned somewhere, the one shaped like grief for a man he'd never met.

"Doctors are calling it heart failure, brought on by years of exhaustion and overwork. Colleagues describe a man who slept three hours a night and called rest a waste of time. An orphan who built one of the most watched firms in the country from nothing, and gave a great deal of it away."

A photograph filled the corner of the screen. The sharp face. The practiced smile. The one everyone believed.

"He leaves no children and no family. A man who had everything, gone at thirty-four." A beat, the practiced pause. "We'll have more as the story develops."

### 75,000 years earlier.

A man stood over a corpse.

The ground under him was red dirt and dry scrub, and the sky was a color no camera would ever get right, and the air smelled of copper and dust and something burning far off. At his feet lay another man, younger, his skull caved in on one side, a shepherd's blood soaking into soil that had never in its short history known what a murder was.

The man doing the standing held something in his hand. Not an apple, whatever the stories would later decide to call it. A sphere, smooth and pale, sitting in his palm like it had been made to. A moment ago it had been dark and quiet. Then his blood touched it, and it woke. The smear on its surface pulled the light in and gave it back brighter, gold threads turning under the skin of the thing, sorting themselves into lines and symbols that no tongue on Earth had been invented yet to read. It had tasted him. Now it was awake, and it was listening.

And under the copper smell and the far-off burning, in a place that was neither the plain nor the inside of his skull, the last thing he'd heard came back to him one more time, patient and old and already going quiet.

_So be it._

He looked down at the body. He looked at his own hands, at the blood drying in the lines of a palm he'd never once seen before and had somehow just used to kill.

"Huh," said Cain.
