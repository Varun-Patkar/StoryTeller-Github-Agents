# Chapter 1: The Wrong Cain

### 75,000 BCE

"Huh," I said.

It was the only word I had, and it wasn't even mine. It came out of a throat that had never spoken English, in a voice a full octave lower than the one I'd used for thirty-four years. I felt the shape of it leave my mouth and I thought, distantly, that was interesting, and then the sphere in my left hand lit up like someone had struck a match inside it, and the whole world folded in half.

The pain went in through my eyes.

I have had migraines. I have had the specific hell of a hangover in a boardroom under fluorescent lights while a man three times divorced explained synergy to me. None of that came close. This was two people trying to fit into one skull, and the skull losing.

For a second I was in a leather chair in Malibu with my chest seizing. For a second I was here, wherever here was, with the sun on the back of my neck. Both were true. Both were me. The two of them ground against each other like tectonic plates, and somewhere in the grinding they stopped being two things and started being one.

Then it eased, the way a cramp eases, all at once and leaving an ache behind.

I opened my eyes.

I had a second set of memories now, sitting under mine like sediment. Not a stranger's. Mine. That was the strange part. I reached for them and they came, easy and familiar, the way you remember your own childhood.

The body I was standing in belonged to a young man named Cain. Not the name off a letter in an orphanage. The real one, the first one, the one all the others were named after eventually. Son of two people this world called Adam and Eve. He had a brother.

I looked down.

The brother was at my feet. Younger, a little softer in the face, laid out flat in the red dirt with a lump already swelling purple above his ear where something hard had caught him. He was breathing. Slow and even, the breathing of the unconscious, not the dead.

I knew what had caught him, because it was still in my right hand. A blade. If you were generous. A palm-length shard of stone, knapped down one edge until it was mean, lashed to a stub of bone with dried sinew. Primitive. Effective. I had used the flat of it to put my brother on the ground.

And then, going by the way I was crouched over him with his head hauled back by the hair and the sharp edge already resting against the soft part of his throat, I had been about half a second from opening it.

I held very still.

The other Cain's memory supplied the reason, helpfully, without any shame about it at all. Jealousy. Plain and hot and old. Abel was better. Abel was faster with the work, gentler with the human herds, easier for their parents to love. And that morning the two of us had been doing what we did when the adults were away at the fighting, which was pass the shining thing back and forth and dare each other to make it do tricks. And I had watched the light bend to Abel's hand better than it bent to mine, one time too many, and something in me had simply decided that the problem was Abel.

So I'd taken the shining thing. And I'd taken the rock. And I'd have taken the rest, if a dead billionaire hadn't fallen out of the sky into my head at exactly the wrong moment.

I looked at the shining thing in my left hand.

An Apple of Eden. I knew it the way I knew my own name, which is to say two ways at once. This Cain knew it as the thing his parents had stolen, the reason the sky over the far hills sometimes lit up at night. The other me, the one who'd spent too many years and too much money on a projector wall, knew it as a Piece of Eden. Isu technology. A control node. Hold it, and every plain human within reach becomes something you can steer by the back of the skull, because that is what the Isu built humans to be. Steerable.

It didn't work on me. It didn't work on Abel either. That was the whole point of us. Our parents had been made a particular way, a designer batch, hybrids grown with enough of the old blood to be immune to the leash. They'd passed it down. Adam and Eve, the first two the Apples couldn't touch, had looked at the thing that was supposed to own them, found it slid right off, and decided to find out what else they could get away with.

What they'd gotten away with, so far, was a war.

I could hear it, now that I was listening. A long way off, past the ridge, the low thud of something that wasn't thunder. The rebellion. Humans and the handful of hybrids who led them, throwing themselves at gods. And winning, my newer memories insisted, actually winning, the herds turning on the herders faster than anyone upstairs had planned for.

Good for them.

It wasn't going to matter.

I knew that the way I knew everything useful, which was from having played the games and read the wikis and watched the lore videos at two in the morning instead of sleeping, back when not sleeping was a personality I was proud of. I knew how this specific story ended, because it was the oldest chapter of the only franchise I'd ever actually loved.

The humans were going to win the war. And then the sky was going to kill everyone who won it.

I stayed crouched there over my unconscious brother, a knife at his throat and a god's leash in my other hand, and I did the math, because doing the math was the one thing that had followed me across the whole distance of death.

I had wished to be Cain.

I want to be very clear that I knew exactly which Cain I meant. I meant Caine, the one from the ugly beautiful vampire game I'd worn out three times over. The deathless one. The first one. The man who killed his brother and got a curse for it that turned out to be the best deal anyone ever signed, an eternity of standing at the edge of every war and every empire, watching, outlasting, never once having to worry about a heart giving out in the dark.

That was the wish. That was the exact and specific thing I'd asked the ceiling for as I died.

And something had listened. Something old and patient that hadn't sounded surprised. So be it, it had said, and it had dropped me into a Cain. It had even given me the fratricide, right on schedule, the knife already in my hand.

It had just skipped the part where it makes you immortal.

"But I didn't mean this Cain," I said out loud, to the dirt, to the corpse-that-wasn't, to whatever was still listening if anything was. My voice came out flat. I wasn't panicking. I have never panicked. I was doing the thing I do instead, which is add up the ways I've been cheated. "This Cain is weak. This Cain is mortal. This Cain bleeds."

There was no god here waiting to bless a murder. That was the whole cosmic joke of the place I'd landed in. In the story I remembered from Sunday mornings, blurry as it was, Cain killed Abel and God cursed him and marked him and made him a wanderer, and the curse was a kind of terrible attention, proof that Someone was keeping score. Here there was no Someone. Here the closest things to gods were the ones losing a war over the ridge, and they didn't care if two of their tools killed each other in the dirt. They encouraged it. A tool that's afraid of dying works harder. Two ants fighting was less than nothing to them.

I could cut Abel's throat right now and the universe would not so much as clear its own.

I would just be a mortal man with a dead brother and a headache.

That was when the Apple decided it wasn't done with me.

It flared, a second time, gentler, and this time the light didn't go in through my eyes. It went in behind them, and it showed me things.

I want to describe it as a dream but it was too clean for a dream. It was more like the loading screen lore of my whole misspent adult life, except I was inside it, and it was real, and it was coming.

I saw the sun.

I saw it do something suns are not supposed to do. A great arc of fire tore loose from the surface of it and reached, and the reaching had a direction, and the direction was here. I saw it hit. I saw the sky over the whole world go the color of a struck match. I saw the cities of the Isu, the real ones, the towers that made this stolen Apple look like a toy, go dark and then go to ash. I saw the humans who had just won their war fall down in the fields where they stood. I saw the number, somehow, felt it more than counted it. When the light was done, fewer than ten thousand people were left alive on the entire Earth. The winners and the losers, burned down to the same handful together.

The Great Catastrophe. That was the tag on the file. I'd known the date in the vague way you know a fact for a quiz. I had never once stood inside it and felt the heat.

And then, because the Apple apparently believed in being thorough, it showed me the part that mattered more.

It showed me that the gods didn't actually die.

Oh, the bodies died. The tall ones with the old blood, they burned like everyone else. But some of them had seen the fire coming, and some of them had been clever, and cleverness is a thing that outlives meat. I saw them pour themselves out of their dying bodies and into their machines, into the deep networks under the ground, into a grey country of pure data where a mind could sit and wait and scheme for as long as the lights stayed on.

And they waited. God, the way they waited.

I saw a woman full of contempt for the whole human project, sealed in the dark under what would someday be a city on the far side of an ocean, spending seventy-five thousand years working out how to get her hands back. Juno. I saw a man who kept coming back, over and over, the same face and the same grief walking out of different mothers across the centuries, unable to die properly and unwilling to stop. Aita, and the sad broken lineage of him. I saw one who wore a king's shape and fell in a marsh in a year my other memory labeled eight hundred seventy-seven, and I saw the trickster who mirrored him lie quiet in the grey for over a thousand more and come walking back out into a world of glass towers in a year I remembered as 2020. Loki. I even caught the edge of others, a woman's voice, a name like Persephone, all of them dead and none of them gone, all of them still with their fingers in the clay of the human race long, long after the world agreed they were myths.

And I saw, at the very end of the long dark corridor of all of it, one modern man in a hood put his hand on a glowing pedestal and die to keep the same fire from finishing the job a second time. Desmond. 2012. He saves everyone, and in the saving he lets the woman in the dark walk free.

Then the Apple went quiet, and I was just a man again, kneeling in the dirt with my ears ringing.

I stayed down for a long moment.

Here is what I understood, once the ringing stopped.

The Isu were going to lose their war and then cheat their own extinction and then spend the entire length of human history reaching up through the floorboards to move us around. Every disaster in the story I loved, every cult and every apple and every tyrant with a glowing rock, traced back to one of these patient dead things refusing to actually be dead. They had done it by becoming information. By outliving their bodies. By turning themselves into something the fire couldn't reach.

Which was, when you stripped the mysticism off it, exactly the thing I had wished for as I died.

I had asked to be the one who stands at the edge of every war and outlasts everyone. I'd been thinking of a vampire. But the vampire was just a story humans told because they'd forgotten the real version, and the real version was standing right in front of me, wearing the future like a coat. You didn't need a curse. You didn't need fangs or a god keeping score. You needed to stop being meat before the meat ran out, and you needed a machine deep enough to hide in.

The Isu had built exactly that machine. Several of them. And they were about to leave it lying around in a burned and empty world with almost nobody left alive who knew how it worked.

Almost nobody.

I looked down at Abel again.

The knife was still at his throat. My hand had never moved. Some part of the old Cain was still standing there with its blood up, still wanting this, and I noticed that part of me with a kind of cold recognition, because it wasn't a stranger. I knew that appetite. I'd had a tidy grown-up version of it my whole first life, the part that sorted every person I met into an asset or an obstacle and felt nothing much either way.

But I ran the numbers, the way I always ran the numbers, and killing my brother came back a loss.

Dead, Abel was one less person the Apple didn't control. One less hybrid. One less pair of hands that could carry something heavy across a burning continent in the small handful of years I had before the sky came down. There was no reward on the other side of this knife. No curse, no mark, no immortality. Just me, alone, one asset lighter, in a world that was about to end.

And there was the other thing, the thing I turned over more carefully because it didn't fit the spreadsheet.

I didn't want to.

Not out of goodness. Let's not insult either of us. I had known this boy for exactly four minutes and half of that was through borrowed memory. But I'd spent a whole life with ten thousand contacts and no one to call at three in the morning, and I had died in an empty house, and something about waking up with a family in my head, an actual mother and father coming home over that ridge, two people who would be glad to see this face, landed somewhere the numbers didn't reach.

His people, I thought, in the old private language I'd built for myself. He always took care of his people.

I took the knife away from my brother's throat.

I sat back on my heels in the dirt and I breathed, and the sun was warm, and somewhere past the hills the last war of the old world went on without me, and my brother's chest went up and down, up and down, alive.

"Okay," I said, to no one, to the quiet, to the Apple gone dark in my hand.

I knew the ending. That was the one thing I had that nobody else on this doomed planet had, the thing that had cost me a whole life to acquire. I knew the sky was going to open. I knew the gods were going to cheat death by becoming ghosts in a machine. And I knew, now, that a man could do the exact same trick, if he was fast, and cold, and willing to do the ugly work in the small window he had left.

I wasn't going to save the war. The war was already lost, it just didn't know it yet.

I was going to save myself. And, because the numbers said so, and maybe a little because of the way that mother-and-father memory sat in my chest, I was going to save the three people who shared my blood and my strange immunity and my one chance of getting out the far side of the fire.

I would become what they were going to become. A mind without a body. A thing the sun couldn't burn. I'd go into the dark ahead of the Catastrophe, and I'd wait it out, and I'd come up on the other side into an empty world with seventy-five thousand years of interfering dead gods ahead of me and the only living map of every move they were going to make.

And every time one of them reached up through the floor, I would be there to cut the hand off.

It was, I had to admit, a lot more ambitious than a gala.

Behind me, past the ridge, the thudding stopped.

That was worse than the thudding. Thudding meant the fighting was over there. Silence meant it was ending, or moving. I turned my head and looked at the far hills, at a thin smudge of dust rising off the trail that came down from them toward our little cluster of huts, and my newer memories told me, with the flat certainty of a boy who had watched for this exact dust his whole short life, that it was them.

Mother and Father. Coming home from the war.

At my feet, Abel made a small sound and his eyelids twitched.

"Right," I said, and I looked at the knife in one hand, and the god's leash in the other, and my brother starting to wake up with a lump on his head, and the dust of my dead parents-who-weren't rolling down toward all of it. "Let's see how good a liar this Cain is."

I got up, and I went to hide the knife.
