# Chapter 1: The Weight of the Apple

The stone was still in his hand.

That was the first thing he understood. Not the sky, not his name, not the ring of white towers on the plain behind him. Just the weight of it, rough and wet, his fingers locked so hard around the rock that they had stopped feeling like fingers.

The second thing he understood was the body.

A young man lay in the trampled grass, turned half onto his side, one arm flung out as if he had reached for something and not been given the time to close his hand. The back of his head was wrong. The grass under it was dark and getting darker.

Cain looked at the body, and then he looked at the stone, and the arithmetic between the two of them arrived all at once.

He dropped the rock. It hit the ground and rolled and he was already stumbling back from it, from the body, from the whole shape of what his arms remembered doing. His heel caught and he went down hard on the dirt, and that small ordinary pain, the jolt of it up his spine, was the thing that finally shoved the rest of the world into focus.

He knew this place.

He should not have known this place. He had never been here. And yet the knowing came up through him like water finding the low ground, filling in everything at once until there was no part of his skull it hadn't touched.

He knew the towers. Pale and seamless and taller than anything human hands would build for another seventy thousand years, they stood on the far side of the river with the early light sliding off their curves. He knew the color of them. He had seen that exact color rendered on a screen, once, in a menu, in a memory that felt like it belonged to a different person entirely.

Because it did.

____

There had been a man.

The man had a small apartment and a job he didn't love and a shelf of games he loved too much, and among all of them there had been one series he came back to the way some people come back to a hometown. He had known its maps. He had known its lore the way a priest knows scripture, the whole tangled history of it, the ones who came before and the ones who came after, the golden fruit that made men into puppets, the war that no one truly won.

He had known all of it as a story. Something that happened to characters. Something you could pause.

He had argued about this world with strangers over the internet. He'd had theories about it, favorites, complaints. Somewhere in a life that was already starting to feel like a book he had read instead of a thing he had lived, there was a chipped mug with a small white logo on it, and he had drunk bad coffee out of that mug while reading about exactly these towers, in a warm room, safe, bored, alive.

The distance between that room and this field was so large that his mind kept sliding off it and landing back in the grass, next to the body, every single time.

The man had gone to sleep in that apartment. Cain was fairly sure of that, in the same distant way he was sure of a dream. There had been a bad feeling in his chest, and then there had been nothing, and now there was this.

Now there was a body in the grass with the back of its head caved in, and hands that had done it, and the two of them were the same hands.

"No," Cain said. His voice came out cracked and unfamiliar, lower than the one he remembered. "No, no. Get up. Get up, please."

He crawled to the body. He turned it. He had already known the face before he saw it, the way you know the last stair in the dark, but knowing did not help.

Abel. His brother. The name sat in his mouth like it had always been there, because for this body it always had.

Abel's eyes were open and there was nothing behind them. He looked surprised. That was the detail that lodged, right then, before anything else. Not the blood, not the wound. The surprise. Abel had not understood that his own brother was going to do this, right up until his brother did.

Cain pressed his hand to the ruined skull as if he could hold the pieces of it in place, as if that was a thing that worked, and it was not, and he knew it was not, and he did it anyway.

"I didn't," he said. "I wasn't. That wasn't me."

But it had been these hands.

____

The knowing kept coming, and it did not care that his hands were still shaking.

It told him where he was on the long line of everything. It told him the pale towers belonged to the ones who had made him, the ones who called themselves the first and everyone else second. It told him what he was to them. Not a son. Not even a servant, really. A tool that walked and talked and could be pointed.

It told him about the fruit.

His free hand, the one not slick with his brother's blood, was closed around something. He had not noticed it. His body had been holding it this whole time the way you hold the one thing you were sent to bring back, carefully, close to the ribs, so that nothing could take it.

He opened his fingers.

The sphere sat in his palm and turned itself over with a slow inner light, patterns folding and unfolding across its surface that his eyes could not quite follow and his mind refused to name. It was warm. It was heavier than a thing that size should be. It was the most beautiful object he had ever held and he understood, with the cold flat certainty of a man reading a caption under a photograph, exactly what it was.

An Apple of Eden.

Abel had carried it. That was the whole of it, laid out plain and ugly. The masters had wanted the fruit. Abel had been holding it, and a command had come down through the leash they had built into every human skull, and the leash had reached into Cain and closed his hand around a stone and walked him across the grass to his brother.

He had done it smiling. Some buried part of the body remembered that, and offered it up, helpfully, and Cain leaned over and was sick into the grass.

When he was done he stayed there on his hands and knees for a while, breathing, the Apple glowing patiently beside his brother's outflung arm. Somewhere across the river a bird was making an ordinary morning sound. The sun was coming up the way it did on a hundred thousand mornings that meant nothing. The world had not stopped for this. The world did not know.

He was the first. The thought arrived quiet and terrible. Whatever came after, however many there would be, all the murders in all the centuries stacked up behind this one moment in the grass. He was the door they would all walk through.

History remembered him. He knew that too. That was the joke of it, the part that would have made the man in the apartment laugh if he weren't currently that man. Of everything Cain would ever be, the world had kept exactly one fact. The first man to kill. That was his whole entry. That was the sum of him.

They had not kept his brother's kindness. They had not kept whatever Abel had been going to do with his one short life. They had kept the killing, because the killing was useful, because it was a thing you could build a warning around.

"I'm sorry," Cain said to the body. The words came out on their own, some reflex the body still had loaded. He listened to himself say them and felt how little they weighed. The man who should have meant them, the brother who had stood here a minute ago, had died before Cain ever woke up. What was left was a stranger apologizing to a corpse he'd been handed, and even the stranger could tell it changed nothing.

____

The leash pulled.

He felt it before he understood it, a tightening behind his eyes, gentle at first, the way a hand rests on your shoulder before it starts to steer. There were words underneath it that were not quite words. *Bring it. Bring the fruit. Come home, good and faithful thing, come home and be praised.*

His knees straightened without his asking. His body turned toward the river, toward the white towers and the ones inside them, and it did this the way your foot finds the floor when you stand, without consultation, without doubt.

Cain stopped.

He should not have been able to stop. That was the second impossible thing of the morning, and it frightened him almost as much as the first. The command was in his blood, keyed to the very shape of him, and every other human in every field along this river was bending to some version of it right now without ever knowing they had bent. He could feel the wrongness of resisting it, a pressure building behind his sternum, a nausea that had nothing to do with grief.

But the hand steering him was reaching for a mind that was no longer entirely the mind it had been built to steer. It closed on a stranger. And the stranger, shaking, teeth gritted, planted his heel in the dirt and did not walk to the river.

The pressure spiked. He tasted metal. For a moment the light went strange at the edges and he thought he might fall.

Then it eased, and he was still standing where he chose to stand, and he was breathing hard, and he was more afraid than he had ever been in either of his lives.

"Okay," he whispered. "Okay. That's new. That's very new."

Across the water, at the base of the nearest tower, something moved.

He looked, and he saw one of them.

He would never afterward be able to describe it cleanly, and he would try, over long years, to people who deserved to know. Tall past the point where tall stopped meaning anything. Beautiful in a way that skipped past admiration and went straight to fear, the way a cliff edge is beautiful. It stood at the tower's foot and it was looking across the river at him, and even at that distance he felt the attention of it land on him like a weight.

It was waiting for its fruit. It had sent a tool to fetch a thing, and the tool had stopped moving, and now it was curious.

Curiosity, in one of them, was the most dangerous thing in the world. He knew that too.

Cain made himself move. Not toward the tower. He bent, slow and careful, keeping his body loose, and he picked up the Apple, and he saw the far figure go still with a kind of satisfaction, *yes, good, now come*, and he turned the other way and he ran.

He ran badly. His legs were not his legs yet. He tripped twice in the first thirty strides and the second time he nearly went into the river and he kept his fist closed around the fruit the whole way down, because whatever else happened, they were not getting it back. He did not have a plan. He had a body he barely knew and a dead brother behind him and a head full of a future that had not happened yet, and he ran into the long grass with all of it.

Behind him, faint and enormous and inside his skull rather than his ears, the leash yanked once, hard, an animal jerk on a rope, and found nothing to hold.

He felt the exact moment they realized. Not that a tool had wandered. That a tool had refused.

____

He did not stop until the towers were gone behind a rise and his legs simply quit, folding him down into the grass where no one could see him from the river. He lay on his back with the Apple on his chest, rising and falling with his breath, and he stared up at a sky that was the same blue it would be in every century he had memorized and never lived.

The man in the apartment had known how this story went.

That was the thing Cain kept circling, lying there, waiting for his heart to slow. He knew what these creatures were and what they had built and how it ended. He knew about the two who had already run, the ones born wrong by the masters' measure, the ones the fruit could not reach. Adam. Eve. He knew there was a war coming that the histories would call a rebellion, and he knew, with the sick certainty of a man who had read the last page first, how that war ended.

It ended with the sky.

Not soon. Not tomorrow. But it was coming, the thing that would burn the towers and the fields and nearly everything that walked or crawled, and there was no version of the story where anyone stopped it. He had watched people better than him try, in the tale, and fail. It was not a battle you could win. It was weather. It was the sun deciding, once, to lean a little closer, and everything under it paying for the lean.

He could not save his brother. He could not save the world that was coming apart. He was one confused man in a body that had just committed the first murder in the history of his species, hunted, already, before he had drawn twenty free breaths.

He turned his head and was quietly, thoroughly sick again in the grass. Then he wiped his mouth and lay back and let himself, for one held moment, simply be wrecked about it. Abel. The surprise on his face. The hands. He gave himself that. He suspected it might be a while before he could afford it again.

For one shining, ugly second, lying there with the fruit on his chest, he let himself want it.

The Apple did impossible things. He had held that fact at arm's length since the grass, and now he took it down and looked straight at it. The thing could reach into a living skull and rewrite what it found. It had walked him across a field and closed his fingers on a stone against his own will. If it could do that, some desperate corner of him reasoned, then maybe it could do the other thing. Reach back. Undo. Put the surprise back onto his brother's face and then smooth even that away.

The knowing answered before the hope could finish standing up, flat and without any mercy in it at all. No. The fruit commanded the living. It bent minds that were still there to be bent. It had nothing to say to the stillness he had left in the grass, because there was nothing in that body left to command. Death was the one door the golden light could not open. He turned the thought over twice, hunting for a seam in it, a loophole, a rule he had misremembered. There was no seam. There never would be. He understood, distantly, that he would keep checking anyway, in one form or another, for a very long time, and that the answer would be the same every time he asked.

Abel was gone. Not hidden, not sleeping, not waiting behind some cleverness he had not thought of yet. Gone, in the one way that stayed.

When it passed, something small was left behind where it had been. Not a plan. He was nowhere near a plan. Just a fixed point, hard and cold and his, the first thing in this second life he had actually chosen.

He was not going to carry the fruit to the river.

Whatever they had made him for, he was going to be bad at it. He was going to be the worst tool they had ever built. They had reached into a human skull expecting a servant and they had gotten a man who knew how their story ended, and if he could not stop the ending, then he was going to spend whatever was left of him making sure that when it came, it did not take everyone.

He knew, too, that saying it and doing it were separated by a distance he could not yet measure. He was one man. He had no allies, no plan, no place to sleep, and a hunt already sniffing along his trail. The creatures across the river had built the whole world he stood in, down to the bones inside his arms, and he had defied them by standing still for the length of a single breath. That was the sum of his rebellion so far. One refused errand and a lot of running.

It was not much. Held up against the size of what he knew was coming, it was almost nothing.

He looked at the Apple glowing on his chest, this thing his brother had died holding, and he closed his hand over its light until the glow leaked out red between his fingers.

"All right," he said, to no one, to Abel, to the towers he'd left behind and the two exiles he had not yet found. "All right. Let's see how far off the rails I can throw this."

He got up. His legs held, this time. To the north, past the grass, the ground broke into low hills, and somewhere out past the hills were the only two people in the world who had ever told these creatures no and lived.

Cain started walking toward them, the first murderer in history, carrying stolen light in a closed fist, leaving one set of footprints in the wet grass and no name that anyone would ever think to write down.
