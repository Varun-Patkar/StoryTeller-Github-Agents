# Chapter 2: A Better Class of Master

Three days in, Cain had worked out the rules of his new life, and he did not think much of the management.

Rule one: everything wanted to eat him. The world before the world he remembered was all teeth. He had thrown rocks at two different animals he had no name for and lost both fights on points.

Rule two: the body was strong and stupid and knew things he didn't. It found water. It knew which roots to dig and which to leave. He had learned to stop arguing with it and just let it walk while he did the thinking.

Rule three, and this was the one that mattered, was that he was not going to make it on his own. Not out here. Not with what was coming.

He sat on his heels at the top of a rise as the light went orange, chewing something bitter the body had approved of, and he took stock the way he'd once taken stock of a bad quarter. Assets. Liabilities. He had a body that wasn't his, a head full of a future nobody else had read, and a glowing golden ball that let him reach into the skull of anyone the Isu had built a leash into.

That last one was the whole account. Everything else was overhead.

He turned the Apple over in his hand. It had stopped frightening him somewhere around the second night, which probably said something unflattering about him. He was choosing not to look at it too closely.

He should have felt worse about Abel. He was aware of the gap where the feeling was supposed to go, the way you're aware of a tooth that's been pulled. He kept checking it with his tongue and finding nothing there.

The truth, which he examined once and then filed away, was that he had never met Abel. He'd inherited a corpse and a legend and a name in a book, and the part of him that had been Cain, the part that was still in here with him, had killed for that golden ball without a flicker of regret and would happily have done it again. The two of them, the man from the apartment and the first murderer, had turned out to agree on an uncomfortable number of things.

Chief among them: survive. Everything else was negotiable.

So. He needed people. Specifically, he needed the only two people in the world who had ever told the masters no and were still breathing to talk about it.

He knew where they were. Roughly. History was generous with the myth and stingy with the map, but a rebellion left marks, and he'd been reading the ground for three days. Burned fields the Isu hadn't bothered to reclaim. Bodies left as lessons. A direction the lessons all pointed away from.

North. He'd been walking north.

____

They found him before he found them, which he should have expected, and didn't, and was annoyed about.

The body felt it first. A prickle up the back of the neck, the animal part of him going still. Cain crouched in the brush and made himself small and watched three figures come down the slope on the far side of the stream, moving in the loose spread of people who were hunting something.

He knew what they were before they got close. He could see it in the walk. Too even. Too patient. No one moved like that on their own, all the small human fidgets sanded off. These were people with a hand on the back of their necks, and the hand was not in the field with them.

Thralls. Enslaved humans, keyed and pointed and sent, their own minds sitting quiet in the dark while something across a river drove them like a cart.

The Isu had not forgotten the tool that refused to come home. Of course they hadn't. He'd embarrassed them, and things that thought of themselves as gods did not enjoy being embarrassed by the cutlery.

The lead thrall stopped at the stream and turned its head, slow, scanning, and Cain felt the attention behind its eyes brush across the brush he was hiding in. Not the thrall's attention. The other one. The one holding the leash, looking out through borrowed eyes from somewhere safe.

He had two choices, and he made the ugly one without much of a debate, because the pretty one got him killed.

He stood up.

Three heads turned toward him at once, in perfect time, and that synchronization was the most horrible thing he'd seen since the grass, worse than teeth, because it was people wearing their own faces with nobody home.

"Hi," Cain said, and lifted the Apple.

He had not practiced this. There had been no manual. But the fruit had walked him across a field to kill a man he'd never met, and he had spent three days deciding that if it could push, it could pull, and that he was done being on the wrong end of it.

He reached, the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and the Apple opened like a hand.

He felt the leashes. Three of them, running back across the water to the thing that held them, taut and humming and utterly indifferent to the people strung along their length. And running alongside them, thin and new and his, three fresh lines he had not known he could cast until he cast them.

He did not try to be gentle. He didn't have the skill for gentle and he didn't have the time and, if he was honest, he didn't have the inclination. He took hold of all three at once and he pulled, hard, the way you'd rip a splinter, and the Isu's grip tore loose with a wrench he felt in his own back teeth.

The three thralls staggered. For half a breath they were nobody's, just three terrified strangers waking up in the wrong field.

Then they were his.

"Sit down," he said.

They sat.

Across the stream, very far away and suddenly very interested, the thing that had been driving them went still with a kind of shock he could taste. He'd felt that flavor once before, three days ago, across a different river. The precise moment one of them realized a tool had turned in its hand.

Cain smiled at the empty air where the attention was, and let it see him do it.

"Not yours anymore," he said.

The leash lashed back, trying to reclaim what he'd taken, and it met his lines and could not get past them, and he held on and hated how much it cost him. His nose was bleeding. The light had gone strange at the edges again. Whatever the Apple was doing to make this happen, it was billing his own skull for the service, and the account was not deep.

He couldn't win a long fight. So he didn't have one.

He let two of the thralls go. Simply opened his hand and released them, and they collapsed where they sat, breathing, alive, free, useless to everyone. The third he kept, because he was greedy and because a man walking into a rebellion could do worse than arrive with a gift.

The Isu across the water gave up the two broken ones and pulled back to lick its pride. It would tell the others. That was fine. Let them learn his name. Let them learn there was exactly one leash in the world they couldn't yank, and it was walking north.

Cain wiped the blood off his lip with the back of his hand and looked at the thrall still sitting obediently at his feet, a woman, older, with a hunter's build and eyes that were slowly filling back up with a person.

"Congratulations," he told her. "You've been promoted. You work for me now. It pays better, mostly in staying alive."

She stared at him.

"You'll come around," he said, and started walking, and she got up and followed, because he made her, and because out here that was the only kind of loyalty he could afford to buy on credit.

____

The rebellion took him at spearpoint the next morning, which he also should have expected.

He'd let them. That was the part they didn't know. He'd seen the sentries an hour before they saw him, and he had walked into the ring of them with his hands open and the Apple tucked away and the freed thrall a pace behind, because getting captured by the people you wanted to join was, on balance, more efficient than trying to find them politely.

They took the Apple off him fast and rough. That stung. He let it.

They marched him through a camp that was less than he'd hoped and exactly what he'd expected. A few hundred, maybe. Lean, scarred, watchful. Fields of people who'd woken up, one way or another, and decided being cattle was worse than being dead. There were more children than he'd thought there would be. There was a smell of woodsmoke and unwashed bodies and, under it, fear worn so long it had gone stale.

These were the ancestors of every Assassin who would ever draw a blade. Right now they were farmers with sharp sticks, and they were losing.

They brought him to the middle of it, to the two people the rest of the camp arranged itself around without seeming to, and Cain got his first look at Adam and Eve.

He'd expected them to glow. Idiotic, but there it was. The stories built them so tall that the reality took a second to fit. Adam was broad and still, a man who'd learned to hold power in reserve, with a scar that had taken part of one eyebrow and old blood under his nails. He looked at Cain the way you look at a snake you haven't decided is dead.

Eve was smaller and worse. She had eyes that did what the Isu's did, that landed on you with weight, and Cain understood at once that she was reading him and that she was good at it. Phanes' daughter. There was a mind behind that face that had been built out of the same stuff as the ones across the river, minus the leash they'd never managed to fit her with.

"He carried this," said the woman who'd taken the Apple, and held it out.

Adam didn't touch it. Eve did. She turned it over the way Cain had, and the light of it slid over her face and told him nothing, because she gave it nothing.

"Where did you get it," Eve said. Not to the guard. To Cain.

"Off my brother," Cain said. "You've probably heard. It's the one thing anyone's going to remember about me."

Something moved through the camp at that. A word, passing back through the crowd, the way a word does. He watched it go and did not enjoy it as much as he'd told himself he would.

"You're the one from the river," Adam said. Flat. "The tool that turned. Their hunters have been screaming about you for three days. A thing they can't hold." He said thing the way the Isu might have. Old habit, maybe. Or a test.

"That's me," Cain said. "The unholdable thing. I've come to be unholdable at you instead, if the position's open."

"It isn't a joke," Eve said.

"No," Cain agreed. "It's a job interview. I'm aware which one of us needs the other more, so I'm going to be honest with you, which I understand is unusual in a first meeting." He looked around the camp, slow, letting them see him count it. "You're losing. You've got a stolen Apple you can barely use, a few hundred people who are brave and starving, and an enemy that reaches into your own ranks and turns your friends into puppets whenever it feels like it. You're going to keep losing. And then something is going to happen that makes all of this look like a good year."

"And you know all this," Eve said, "how."

Here was the edge of it. The one thing he had that no one else in the world had, and he was not about to spend it in the first hour for a warm meal and a spear pointed somewhere else.

"I know things," he said. "That's my whole value, and I'm not going to lay it all on a table for people who might still gut me before lunch. What I'll give you for free is this. Your enemy's real weapon isn't the war. It's the leash. And I'm the only person you have ever met who can cut it."

Adam's jaw worked. "Words."

"Sure," Cain said. "Give me back my ball and point me at a problem, then."

____

The problem, as it turned out, was already among them.

He felt it before the shout went up, the same prickle, the same wrongness, and he turned toward it a half-second before the young man three fires over stopped being a young man.

It was a clean job. The Isu had gotten a leash into someone inside the camp, some poor tracker they'd taken and turned and sent home smiling, and they'd been sitting quiet in his skull waiting for exactly this, for the moment the rebels clustered around something new and interesting and stopped watching their own. The thrall came up out of the crowd with a blade already moving, and it wasn't going for Cain. It was going for Eve.

Of course it was. Kill the immune ones. Kill the ones you can't wear.

People screamed. Adam moved, fast for his size, but he was on the wrong side of the fire and he wasn't going to make it, and the blade was coming down toward the one mind in this camp the Isu wanted stopped most.

"The Apple," Cain said. "Now."

Later he would wonder why the guard threw it to him instead of running. Panic, probably. A body looking for anyone who sounded like they had a plan. It didn't matter. The Apple left her hand and Cain caught it and it opened for him before it had finished touching his palm, and he was already reaching across the camp for a leash he could feel humming in the dark.

He didn't try to free the thrall. Freeing took time he didn't have. He took the leash the Isu was pulling and he seized it, mid-motion, and he stopped the man's arm as if he'd grabbed the wrist himself.

The blade hung a hand's width from Eve's throat.

The thrall shook. Two forces had hold of him now, the god across the river and the man across the fire, and the poor bastard in the middle was just meat being argued over. Cain felt the Isu heave, trying to force the arm down through him, and he set his feet in his own skull and heaved back, and his nose opened up again and the world went white at the corners.

"Let. Go," he said, through his teeth, and he wasn't sure which of them he was talking to.

He won. Not because he was stronger. He was fairly sure he wasn't. He won because the thing across the river was doing this from a distance through a tool, and Cain was doing it from six feet away with everything he had and nothing held back, and at that range, for that one second, he simply wanted it more.

He tore the leash out of the Isu's grip, and this time he didn't set the man free and he didn't keep him. He turned him. Just for a moment. Just long enough to march the blade around, away from Eve, and to make the thrall look across the fire at Cain with borrowed, terrified eyes.

"Tell them," Cain said to the empty air behind those eyes, to the thing that was still watching, furious, from far away, "that the cutlery's got opinions now."

Then he let go, gently this time, and the young man dropped the knife and folded to the ground and started to cry, himself again, hollowed out and whole.

The camp was silent. Three hundred people staring at the stranger with blood down his chin and a golden ball in his fist, standing between their matriarch and a knife that had already been stopped.

Cain wiped his face. His hands were shaking and he hid it by tucking the Apple against his ribs, the way the body had held it that first morning, close, where nothing could take it.

Eve had not moved. She looked at him across the fire, and now, finally, there was something in her face he could read.

She did not look grateful. She looked like a woman who had just watched a wolf drive off a lion in her own camp and was doing the sum on what came next.

"You saved my life," she said, "and you enjoyed it."

"I enjoyed winning," Cain said. "You were just standing where the winning was." He managed a smile, and it came out more honest than he meant it to. "Don't mistake me for one of yours, Eve. I'm not here for your free will or your rebellion or your grand cause. I'm here because the sky is going to try to kill everything I intend to own one day, and I'd like some help making sure it doesn't."

Adam had come around the fire at last, spear low, uncertain now whether to point it or lower it.

"And what do we call you," he said.

Cain thought about the name in the book. The one fact the world had decided to keep. He turned it over, this small ugly legend, and found that it would do. It would do very well. A name that made people flinch and look away and never, ever look closer.

"Cain," he said. "You've heard of me. Try to keep it to yourselves."
