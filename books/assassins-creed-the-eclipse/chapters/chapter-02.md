# Chapter 2: The Hardest Pitch of Two Lives

### 75,000 BCE

I had four minutes to become a good son, and I'd never been one in either life.

The dust on the trail resolved into two people while I was still deciding how to stand. That was the tell that the boy I was wearing had noticed a hundred times and I was only borrowing now: you could read the whole war in how they came down off the ridge. Fast meant a rout, ours or theirs. Slow meant dead friends carried home. They were coming at a walk. Not slow. Not fast. The walk of people who had won something today and paid for it.

My mother came first.

I want to be careful about that word, because I'd had a mother in the other life, a distracted kind woman named Mercer who signed my report cards and never once looked at me the way this one did. Eve came down the trail with a spear she hadn't cleaned yet and eyes that found me before they found anything else, and the borrowed part of me lit up like a dog hearing a key in a lock, and the new part of me, the part that had died alone in a leather chair, felt something it did not have a word for and decided not to look at it directly.

Behind her, Adam. Bigger, slower, a cut over one eyebrow held shut with pitch and someone's hair. He carried himself like a man who had spent years learning exactly how much space he took up and had decided to take up more. He looked at the huts first, then the treeline, then the herds, then us. Threats, then assets, then children. I knew that order. I'd run meetings in that order.

"They're back," Abel said, from the dirt, in a voice full of gravel and hurt.

Right. Abel was awake.

He'd come up while I was watching the ridge, one hand pressed to the lump above his ear, blinking at a world that had apparently gone sideways on him. His eyes landed on me and there was no fear in them, which told me he didn't remember the knife. Just the blow. Good. That was the one mercy in this whole scene and I hadn't even earned it, the timing had.

"You hit me," Abel said. He said it the way a younger brother says everything, half accusation and half genuine bafflement that the world contained a person who would do that to him. "We were playing and you took the light and you hit me with the rock."

"I did," I said.

I have found, across two lifetimes, that people expect you to deny things, and that admitting them immediately breaks something open in the conversation you can then climb through. Abel's mouth was already shaped around the next part of the accusation and my agreeing with him left it hanging there with nowhere to go.

Eve crossed the last of the ground and went to one knee in front of Abel and tilted his head with two fingers under his chin, checking the lump the way you check fruit for rot. Gentle and fast. Her spear never left her other hand.

"He'll live," she said. Not to me. To Adam. Then, to Abel, softer, "Sit in the shade. Drink. You're not dying, you're just going to wish you were by tonight."

Abel went, throwing me one last wounded look, and I let him have it because he'd earned it and because a man who's been hit wants the person who hit him to see that he saw.

Then it was the three of us, and Adam was looking at me, and I understood that the actual conversation was about to start.

Here is a thing I knew from the other life. When a man like Adam looks at you and goes quiet, the worst move you can make is to fill the quiet. Everyone fills the quiet. That's the mistake. The quiet is a test and the answer is to be comfortable inside it, so I stood in it, and I looked back at him, and I let him find whatever he was looking for.

He didn't like what he found.

"You're standing wrong," Adam said finally.

"Am I."

"He hits his brother, my son does. He's done it before, he'll do it again, they're boys." He came closer. The cut over his eye had bled down into the crease beside his nose and dried there. "But after, he sulks. Or he lies. Or he runs to your mother before Abel can. He does not stand in the yard like a man three moves into a game of stones, waiting to see what I do." His head tilted, exactly the way Eve's had over Abel's skull, checking me for rot. "Who taught you to stand like that?"

And there it was. The whole problem of my new life, delivered inside the first four minutes, by the one person in the world equipped to notice.

I could lie. I was good at lying. The boy's memories were right there, I could reach into them and pull out the exact sulk Adam expected, wear it, defuse this. Part of me wanted to, out of pure habit, because hiding was what I did, it was the reflex I'd built a whole first life on.

But I ran the numbers, fast, the way I always do, and the lie came back a loss.

Because I didn't need these two to think I was a normal boy. I needed them to move a family across the world in the next handful of days on the strength of my word. You don't get that from a boy who sulks. You get that from something they're a little afraid of. And Adam was already most of the way to afraid. The trick, the same trick it's always been, was to give the fear a shape they could use instead of one they'd have to kill.

"Something happened when I held the light," I said.

Eve stood up.

I felt the attention change. It's a physical thing, real attention, it has weight, and I'd spent a career learning to feel the moment a room gives it to you. I had it now. Two people who had been half-listening the way tired parents half-listen were suddenly all the way here.

"The Apple," Eve said. She used a different word for it, older, but that's the one that came through the merge, so that's the one I'll write. "You held it and something happened."

"It showed me things."

Adam made a sound in his chest, not quite a laugh. "It doesn't show. It tells. It reaches into the dirt-people and it tells them what to do, that's all it does, that's all it's for. We took it off a dead god and it is a leash and nothing more." He said it fast and certain, and under the certainty I heard the thing I was listening for, which was that he needed it to be true. "It doesn't show us anything. It can't. We're not the ones it talks to."

"That's how you use it," I said. "It's not all it is."

"And a boy would know that. A boy who has held it twice."

"A boy who saw the end of the world when he held it the second time. Yes."

I let that sit.

This is the part people get wrong about persuasion. They think it's about the force of what you say. It isn't. It's about who has to talk next. I'd put a thing in the air that was either the most important sentence they would ever hear or the raving of a boy with a knock coming to his own head, and the rule of it was that whoever spoke next had to choose which. I wasn't going to choose for them. I waited.

Adam chose wrong first, the way strong men do.

"He's got sun in him," he said to Eve, turning half away, wanting it settled. "Or the blow he took, maybe he gave himself one back. We don't have time for this. The push north is in three days and the boys are old enough now to hold a line behind the wall, both of them, and after we're gone they'll have to hold more than that. The god-things live a long time, longer than us, this war doesn't end when we do. I won't have you filling his head with visions when he needs to be learning the wall."

It was, I noticed, a good speech. Adam had clearly given it before, some version of it, to himself, on long nights. The war outlives us so the sons take it up. It was the most human thing in the world and it was completely, catastrophically beside the point, and I felt a strange tenderness toward him for not knowing that yet.

"There isn't going to be a wall," I said.

"Cain."

"There isn't going to be a north to push into, or a line to hold, or a war for me and Abel to inherit, because in less than one turn of the seasons the sky is going to open and burn all of it. That's what it showed me. Not a dream. Not sun sickness." I kept my voice flat. I have learned that the flatter you say the enormous thing, the harder it lands, because horror performed sounds like performance and horror stated sounds like fact. "A fire comes off the sun. It reaches the whole world at once. It burns the god-things in their towers and it burns us in our fields, the winners and the losers together, and when it's done there are fewer people left alive on the entire earth than we've got fighting in the north. That's what the Apple showed me. It showed me the day everyone dies."

Nobody said anything.

Abel, in the shade, had stopped nursing his head and was listening.

Eve was the one watching my face, and I let her. I'd learned long ago how to arrange a face into whatever I wanted a person to find on it, and the strangest part of this whole new life was that I didn't have to. I wasn't lying. For the first time in longer than I could measure, I was standing in front of people I needed to convince and simply telling them the truth, and I didn't know how to do that any other way than the way I did everything, cold and clear and certain, so that's how it came out.

"Why," Eve said slowly, "would it show you that. A leash. Why would a thing that only points at humans and says 'obey' show my son the end of the world."

"I don't know," I said, which was almost true. "You don't know either. That's the point, isn't it. You can make it steer the dirt-people. That's the one trick you've found. But you took it off a dead god and you don't know what else is in it, because you're not the ones who built it, you just stole it and learned the shallow end." I watched that land on Adam, because it was aimed at him. "You're about to tell me to ignore a warning because it came out of a thing you don't understand. But that's exactly backwards. A thing you don't understand does something you didn't know it could do, that's not the moment you laugh it off. That's the moment you go very still and pay attention."

Adam had turned back around somewhere in the middle of that.

The cut over his eye had started bleeding again, a thin line, and he didn't wipe it. He was looking at me the way you look at a tool that has just moved on its own in your hand.

"You don't talk like him," he said quietly. "Like Cain. You look at me out of his face and you don't talk like my son at all."

And this was the knife-edge of the whole thing, the exact moment the pitch lived or died, because he was right, and we both knew he was right, and if I let him keep pulling that thread he would unravel the whole impossible truth of me and there was no version of that conversation that ended with them believing anything I said.

So I gave him something true to hold that wasn't the truth.

"I held the end of the world in my hand," I said. "Then I put a rock into my brother's skull over a toy, and I stood here in the dirt with both of those things in me at the same time, and something in me changed. Yes. You're not wrong. Something is different. I can't make it un-different for you." I let my voice drop, just slightly, the way you do when you want a man to lean in. "But you can use the boy who sulks, who'll learn your wall and die on it in a year when the sky comes down. Or you can use the one standing in front of you, who knows what's coming and is already thinking about how to walk out the far side of it. You don't have to understand me, Adam. You have exactly one thing to decide, and it's whether I'm lying."

Eve answered before Adam could. She'd been quiet the way the dangerous ones are always quiet, and when she spoke it wasn't to me.

"He's not lying," she said. "I've watched him lie his whole life, he's good at it, he gets it from you. This isn't that." She looked at me and something moved behind her eyes that I would spend a long time afterward trying not to think about, because it was aimed at a son she thought she had and I wasn't quite him. "I don't know what my boy became this morning. But he believes every word of that, down to the floor. And I have never once, in all this war, been sorry I trusted the thing that scared me over the thing that comforted me."

Adam looked at her. Something passed between them, the shorthand of two people who had led a rebellion out of nothing but nerve and each other, and I watched a decision get made in a language I wasn't part of.

"Say it, then," Adam said, turning back to me, and his voice had changed, gone flat and working, the voice of a man who has decided to spend belief and wants his money's worth. "The sky burns. Fine. Say I believe you. You don't warn a man about a flood you can't stop just to watch him drown. You've been standing there three moves ahead since we came down that hill. So tell me the other three. What do we do."

And there it was. The room, turned. The hardest pitch of two lives, and I'd closed it, and now they were asking me for the plan, which was the one thing in this entire doomed valley I actually had.

I almost smiled. I didn't, because Abel was watching and a boy remembers the day his brother hit him and then smiled, but God, I wanted to.

"We don't fight the fire," I said. "You can't. Nobody can, not you, not the god-things, and believe me, some of them are going to try." I crouched, and I picked up a stick, and I did the thing I'd done in a hundred boardrooms, which is take the worst news anyone has ever heard and turn it into a diagram. "We do what the smart god-things are about to do to save themselves. We just do it first, and we do it better, and we don't tell them we did it at all."

I scratched a line in the dirt. The sun. A second line, curving off it. The fire, reaching.

"The god-things don't really die when their bodies burn," I said. "The clever ones have machines. Deep places, under the ground, where the fire can't reach. And they've learned how to pour themselves into those machines and keep going after the meat is gone. A person, but made of the same stuff the Apple's made of. Light and numbers instead of blood. They'll sit in the dark for a very long time and wait for the world to grow back, and then they'll start reaching up out of it to move us around like they always have."

Eve had crouched too, now, across the little diagram. Adam stayed standing, but he was looking at the dirt.

"You want us to become god-things," Adam said.

"I want us to steal the one good idea they ever had before they finish having it," I said. "There are two places that matter. One is far to the north, across the cold water." I stabbed the stick into the dirt, up and to the side, roughly where Norway sat under seventy-five thousand years I hadn't lived yet. "There's a machine there that does the hard part, the part even the god-things can barely do. It takes one of those poured-out light-people and puts them back into a body. A real body, warm, breathing. Without it, you save yourself and you're a ghost forever. With it, a ghost can come home to flesh."

"And the other place."

"West. Across the great water, a long way." Another stab, down and across, where a Temple sat under a city that wouldn't have a name for a very long time. "That's where you do the pouring. It's the deepest, safest hole in the world, and it has the machine that can take a whole person and turn them entirely into light. That's where I go. That's where I put myself into the dark, and where I hide all four of our bodies so cold and so deep the fire goes right over the top of us."

"All four," Eve said.

"You, him, Abel, me. We all go on ice, deep down where it's safe. And a copy of us, or of me first, goes into the machine, so there's something awake in the dark keeping the doors shut while our bodies wait out the burn." I looked up at both of them. "You don't lose me. You don't lose Abel. We don't lose each other. That's the whole point of doing it this way instead of just dying like everyone else is about to. Everyone else on this earth is going to spend their last year fighting a war that's already lost. We're going to spend ours climbing down a hole together and pulling it shut behind us."

The valley was quiet except for Abel, in the shade, who had come to the edge of the shadow to hear better and didn't seem to remember he was supposed to be angry at me.

"It splits us up," Eve said. She'd gone right to the one flaw, the way she'd gone right to Abel's lump. "The cold water is north. The deep hole is west. We can't do both in the time you say we have and stay together."

"No," I said. "We can't. You three go north for the machine that brings a ghost home. I go west and start digging us the grave that isn't a grave." I kept my eyes on her, because Adam would follow her and not the other way around. "I know. Believe me, I know what I'm asking. But there's stolen god-craft that moves you fast across the water, the kind of thing you won this war to get your hands on, and it's the only reason any of this fits inside the time we have. We split now, or we all burn together in a year holding the line on a wall that doesn't matter."

Adam crouched at last. He picked up the stick I'd been drawing with, turned it over, looked at the crude little map of the end of the world scratched into his own front yard.

"My son," he said, not quite to me, "walked out into the sun this morning a jealous boy with a rock." He set the stick down. "And he's going to lead his family into a hole in the ground on the far side of the sea to hide from the sky, and I'm going to let him, because his mother says he isn't lying and because I have never once known her to be wrong about that." He stood, and the war-leader was back in him, the man who moved people. "Three days. We were going to spend them getting ready for the wall. We'll spend them getting ready for your hole instead."

He walked off toward the huts to start giving orders to people who didn't yet know their war had just ended, and Eve stayed crouched across from me a moment longer, looking at the little sun and the little fire in the dirt, and then at me.

"When we're in the dark," she said quietly, so Adam wouldn't hear, "and it's just us and the cold and the waiting. I'm going to ask you again who you are. And you're going to tell me the true thing, not the true thing you gave your father."

I looked at my mother, who was not my mother, who had seen straight through me inside of ten minutes when a whole first life full of people never had.

"Yeah," I said. "Okay."

It was the first promise I made in the new world, and it was the only one, for a very long time, that I actually meant to keep.
