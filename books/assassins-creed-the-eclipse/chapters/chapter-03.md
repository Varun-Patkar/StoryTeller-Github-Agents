# Chapter 3: The Knife

By the time the leaves turned, they were calling him the Knife.

Cain would have preferred almost anything else. The name was lazy. It described what he did to other people and said nothing about what he was, which, on reflection, was probably why the rebellion liked it. People wanted their weapons simple.

He let them have it. A man arguing about his own legend was a man drawing eyes, and he had spent a season teaching himself to be looked at in exactly one way and no other. The Knife. The strange quiet one who walked into a field of the enemy's puppets and walked out with the field empty. That was all they needed to see.

What they did not see was the accounting.

He kept it in his head, the only ledger in the world written in a language no one else could read. Weeks. He was counting down in weeks now, and the number was smaller than it had been, and none of the people cheering his name had any idea there was a number at all.

The war was going well. That was the joke that kept him warm at night. The war was going wonderfully, and it did not matter even slightly, and he was the only one at the fire who knew both halves of that.

____

The masters came for the ford at the end of the dry season, and they came the only way they had left, which was to send people to do it.

Cain watched them come from the ridge, and he did the sum, and the sum was ugly. Two hundred thralls, maybe more, walking up the valley in that horrible even step, farmers and mothers and children old enough to hold a spear, every one of them driven from somewhere safe by a hand on the back of the neck. The Isu had stopped pretending to fight their own wars a long time ago. Why spend a god when you could spend a village.

Adam crouched beside him, still as stone, reading the same field and hating it for different reasons. "That's Yset's people," he said. "The eastern camp. They were taken in the spring." His jaw moved. "My cousin is down there."

"Probably," Cain said.

Adam looked at him.

"You want me to tell you a comfortable thing," Cain said. "I don't have one. Your cousin is a leash with a body on the end of it, and in about an hour that body is going to try very hard to kill yours. So." He stood, and rolled the ache out of his shoulders, and took the Apple out from against his ribs where he kept it always now, warm as a held coal. "Let's see if I can hand him back to you."

He did not enjoy this part. He wanted to. He'd told Eve he enjoyed winning, and he did, but the winning was after. This part, the reaching, was just cost.

He opened the Apple and he opened himself and the valley lit up in his head, two hundred leashes strung back across the hills to the handful of Isu sitting safe and far and bored, running an army the way a child runs ants with a stick.

He couldn't take two hundred. He'd learned his ceiling the hard way, in a smaller fight, with a worse headache. He could take a piece. He could take the front of it, the wedge, the part that mattered.

So he took the wedge.

He didn't try to free them. Freeing was slow and kind and he had no time for either. He seized forty leashes at once, ripped them out of the Isu's distant grip, and turned them, and forty thralls in the front rank stopped walking up the valley and turned around and began, at his direction, to walk back down it. Into the rest.

The masters felt it go. He tasted their shock, that flavor he'd come to know, sharper now because there were more of them sharing it. They yanked back, all of them at once, trying to reclaim the wedge, and the pain of holding against that many was a white wire behind his eyes and blood already sheeting down over his mouth.

He'd tried once to describe to himself what this felt like, holding a leash against the thing that made it. The closest he'd gotten was a rope with a bull on the far end, in the dark, gripped with nothing but the muscle behind his own eyes. The bull never tired. He did. Every second was a negotiation with a part of his own skull that wanted to switch off rather than keep paying.

He held for six seconds. That was all he needed.

Six seconds of forty puppets swinging their spears into the ranks behind them, of an army suddenly at war with its own front, of that perfect terrible even step dissolving into a mess of bodies that no longer moved as one. Then the rebels hit the confusion from the flank, Adam at the point of them roaring something old and wordless, and the thing that had been an army came apart.

Cain let the forty go. He had to. His ceiling was a cliff and he was already over the edge of it, and the last thing he saw clearly before he sat down hard in the dirt was the wedge collapsing, freed men falling to their knees mid-swing, some of them cut down by rebels who couldn't tell in the press who'd just been saved and who was still a weapon.

That was the cost, too. He'd freed forty and watched a third of them die in the ten seconds it took to free them, killed by the friends he'd freed them for. He filed it. He was getting a large collection of things filed.

"You're bleeding," said a voice above him. Nara. Of course. She had a talent for stating the roof was on fire while it burned.

"I noticed," Cain said, and let her haul him upright.

____

They won the ford. They won most things, that season.

Nara walked him back to the camp with his arm over her shoulder, which he permitted because his legs had opinions and none of them were load-bearing. She was the hunter he'd taken at the stream, the first leash he'd ever cut on purpose, and she had every reason in the world to have run the moment he let go of her mind and no reason at all that he could find for why she hadn't.

He'd asked her once. Early. He'd released the Apple's hold on her completely, made a point of it, told her she was free and could go, and watched to see what a person did with that.

She'd stayed.

"Where would I go," she'd said, like he was slow. "You're the thing they can't hold. Everyone near you lives longer. That's the whole world now. You, or the leash." She'd looked at him with those flat hunter's eyes. "I pick you."

He respected that. It was the most honest loyalty he'd ever been offered, loyalty as arithmetic, a woman choosing the winning animal. He could trust arithmetic. It was love and belief he didn't know what to do with, and the rebellion was drowning in both.

"You keep almost dying," Nara observed now, dumping him against a log near the fire. "For their cousins. Their villages." She crouched, studying him. "You don't care about their cousins."

"No," Cain agreed.

"So why."

He closed his eyes. The headache was a living thing. "Because the Knife that saves your cousin gets to stand at the front and point at maps," he said. "And I need to be the one pointing at maps. That's all. The cousins are just where the pointing happens to land."

Nara was quiet for a moment. "That's ugly," she said, without heat.

"It's honest," Cain said. "You of all people should like it."

She snorted, and got up to find him water, and did not disagree.

The days after a win had their own weather. The camp ate a little better, laughed a little louder, and told the story of the ford back to itself until the Knife in it stood taller than the man against the log. Children who had watched forty puppets turn on their own line now played at it in the dirt, one of them always cast as him, waving a stick and shouting until the others fell down. Cain watched that once, from a distance, and felt nothing he could name. A tool being polished into a legend. It was useful. He let it happen and did not join in.

____

The council met three nights later, when he could stand without the world tilting.

They met in Eve's tent, which was to say the largest of the miserable shelters, and the leadership of the rebellion crowded into it and argued in the low urgent way of people who had learned that being loud drew the sky. Adam. Eve. Six or seven others, the ones who'd survived long enough to matter.

Cain waited. He'd learned that too. Let them exhaust the obvious first, and then be the one holding the thing they hadn't thought of.

"They're pulling back," Adam said. "Everywhere. The eastern watchtowers are empty. The mines at the black river, empty. Ground they killed us over a year ago, and now they just... leave it." He shook his head. "It doesn't make sense. We're not strong enough to push them back. They're choosing to go."

"A trap," said one of the others.

"For what," Eve said. "They don't need traps. They need to point a finger and half our people fall down worshipping." She hadn't looked at Cain once. She was very good at not looking at him, and he'd come to understand that her not-looking was a kind of watching. "They're afraid of something. And it isn't us."

There it was. She was the sharpest thing in the tent, always, and it made her dangerous and it made her useful and one day it was going to make her a problem.

"They're afraid of something," Cain agreed, and every head turned, because the Knife rarely spoke in council and they'd all learned it meant something when he did. "And you're right, Eve. It isn't us."

"Then what," Adam said.

And here was the needle he had to thread. He knew exactly what the Isu were afraid of. He knew it was coming out of the sky in a matter of weeks, that it would not care which side of this war anyone had been on, that the towers and the tents would burn the same. He knew, and he could not say it, because a man who knew the precise shape of the end of the world was a man who got very interesting questions asked of him, and interesting was the one thing he could not afford to be.

So he told them a true thing that was not the truth.

"The masters are dying," he said. "Not from us. From each other, from something in their own house. You've all seen it. The thralls come worse-handled every month. Sloppier. Like whoever's holding the leash keeps getting called away to a bigger fire." He let that sit. "They're not retreating from the war. They're abandoning it. The war stopped being their most important problem, and if their most important problem isn't us, then it's inside those walls." He looked around the tent, slow, landing on each of them. "Which means for the first time, their cities are looking the wrong way."

The silence after that had a shape he liked.

"You want to march on a city," Adam said slowly.

"I want to march on the right one." Cain crouched and drew in the dirt, a river, a bend, a mark. He'd been drawing this map in his head for a season. "Here. Upriver. They call it a minor holding, they garrison it light, and right now everyone inside it is staring at the sky and not at their own gate." He tapped the mark. "We take that, we don't win the war. We prove the war is already over. Every village still wearing a leash hears that the masters bled, and the leashes start to matter less. That's how this ends. Not a battle. A story."

It was a good pitch. It was even mostly true. The city was lightly held, the Isu were distracted, taking it would break morale across the whole enslaved world. All of that was real.

None of it was why he'd chosen that city. He'd chosen that city because he knew what was buried under it, and because in a matter of weeks he was going to need what was buried under it more than he'd ever needed anything, and he was not going to be able to walk in and ask.

Adam was nodding, slow, convinced. The others were leaning in. He had them.

He had all of them except one.

"You're very sure," Eve said quietly, "for a man who came to us off a road with a dead brother and no past." She was looking at him now. Full weight. "You're always very sure, Cain. About which fields to raid, which nights to move, which way the masters will jump. As if you've read it somewhere." A pause. "What are we marching toward. Really."

The tent had gone still. This was the moment, and he'd known it was coming from the night he walked in, the night Eve's eyes had landed on him and started keeping their patient account.

He met her look and gave her the only thing that would hold. More honesty. It was the only currency she couldn't counterfeit.

"You want to know if I'm steering you," he said. "I am. I've been steering you since the ford, since before the ford. I'm marching you toward that city because it serves me, and because it also happens to serve you, and I've spent my whole short life making sure those two things point the same direction so that people like you keep following people like me." He didn't blink. "I've never once pretended to be here for your cause, Eve. I'm here for me. The difference between me and the masters is that when I win, you live. That's the entire difference. It's also the only one that's ever mattered to anybody."

For a long moment nobody breathed.

Then Adam laughed, short and rough, the laugh of a man who'd rather have an honest wolf than a lying lamb. "At least he says it to our faces," he said, and the tension broke, and the council moved on to the shape of the march.

Eve didn't laugh. She held Cain's eyes a beat longer, and what was in her face wasn't anger. It was worse. It was a kind of recognition, the look of someone filing a thing away for later, in a ledger of her own.

He knew that look. He'd invented it. And he understood, watching her look away at last, that she had believed every word he'd said and drawn a conclusion from it he hadn't offered: that a man who steered you toward his own gain would keep steering long after the masters were bones, and that someone, someday, would have to decide what to do about him. She wasn't wrong. He just didn't intend to be anywhere she could reach when the day came.

____

Later, when the tent had emptied, Nara found him at the edge of the camp looking upriver at nothing, at the dark where the city was.

"You told the truth in there," she said. "Adam thinks it makes you trustworthy."

"Adam's a good man," Cain said. "Good men think honesty and goodness are the same thing." He watched the water. "I told them the truth about being selfish. I didn't tell them the other one."

"There's another one?"

There was. There was the number in weeks, and the fire coming down out of the sky, and the thing under the city that could hold a mind after the body it belonged to was ash. There was the plan he'd been building since the grass, the exit that had room in it for exactly one person, and the cold flat fact that every face at that fire, Adam's, Eve's, hers, was almost certainly going to be gone before the next year turned, and that he'd known it the whole time he was learning their names.

He could save some of them. Not from the sky. Nobody was saving anybody from the sky. But he was a man who intended to still be here in ten thousand years, and a man like that thought about seed the way a farmer did. Who was worth keeping. Whose knowledge, whose blood, whose stubborn immune line might matter to a future he was the only one planning for.

He was already making that list, too. He had a lot of lists.

The immune ones went at the top. Eve's line first of all, that stubborn hybrid blood the masters had never once managed to leash, worth more to a future than every spear in the camp. Adam's nerve. A handful of others he'd marked quietly over the season, the clever ones, the ones who asked the second question after everyone else had stopped satisfied at the first. Not because he loved them. Because a future needed stock, and he was the only man alive shopping for it this early, while it was still cheap.

"There's always another one," he told her. "That's the job."

He looked at the dark upriver a while longer, at the place where his real war was, the one that had nothing to do with masters or leashes and everything to do with a sky that didn't take sides.

"Get some sleep," he said. "We march in three days. We're going to take a city."

He did not add the rest of it, the part only he carried. That he wasn't going to that city to win a war.

He was going there to disappear.
