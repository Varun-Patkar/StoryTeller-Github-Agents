# Chapter 3: Into the Dark

### 75,000 BCE

We split at the water three days later, and I found out that knowing a goodbye is coming does nothing at all to help you stand inside one.

The stolen god-craft sat on the shingle like something that had washed up dead, a long pale shape that hummed when you got close and hurt your teeth. The rebellion had a dozen of them, taken off the god-things in the war, and nobody really understood them, which meant nobody but me knew that the two waiting on this beach could cross in days what should have taken a year on foot. One would take Adam, Eve, and Abel north across the cold water. The other would take me west, alone, across the great one.

I'd spent the three days being useful and cold and busy, which is what I do instead of feeling things, and it had worked right up until the moment there was nothing left to do but say the word that sent us in different directions.

Eve held my face in both hands. She'd stopped asking who I was. She was saving it, I think, for the dark.

"North, then west, then the deep hole," she said. "You'll be waiting for us at the bottom of it."

"I'll be waiting."

Adam gripped my shoulder hard enough to bruise, which from him was a whole speech, and steered Abel toward the northern craft. And that left the last one, the one I hadn't rehearsed, because there is no rehearsing it.

Abel stopped in front of me. The lump on his head had gone yellow-green and he'd stopped touching it. He was still a little wary of me, which was correct, and under the wariness he was my brother, which I was still getting used to being true.

I had almost killed this boy. Three days ago I'd had a rock in my hand and every intention. He didn't know that. He was never going to know that. And I stood there and understood that the not-knowing was a gift I was giving him and also a debt I was hiding, and that a better man would confess and a smarter man would not, and that I have never once in my life had trouble telling those two men apart.

"When you're scared up north," I told him, "you do what Mother says and not what Father does. Father's brave. Brave gets you killed. Mother's careful. Careful gets you to the bottom of the hole."

Abel almost smiled. "You're not going to be there to boss me."

"I'll be at the bottom of the hole bossing you the second you climb down it. Go."

He went. I watched the northern craft take my whole family up the coast and out over the grey water until it was a smudge and then nothing, and I stood on the empty beach with the taste of the machines in my teeth and I thought, with the part of me that had died friendless in a leather chair, that I had lived thirty-four years and never had anything I was this afraid to lose, and I'd had it for exactly three days.

Then I got in the other one and went west, because feelings are a tax and I had work.

____

The world was enormous and empty and wrong, and I kept catching myself gaping at it.

I navigated the only way I could, which was by the shape of things. The god-craft had no map I could read, but it answered rough intent if you held the Apple and pictured where you wanted to be, and I had spent a stupid, wonderful amount of my first life staring at that coastline in a hundred loading screens. I knew the mouth of a river that wouldn't be called the Hudson for another seventy-five thousand years. I knew the long reach of a bay before anyone drowned a Dutch colony's worth of names into it. I steered by a memory of a map of a place that didn't exist yet, and the wrongness of that was the least wrong thing about the whole trip.

I want to say I was all business. I mostly was. But there was a night I passed low over open water under more stars than the twenty-first century had ever let me see, no cities, no haze, nothing between me and all of it, and I turned the craft's hum down and just looked, and let myself be a person looking at something instead of a person calculating the value of it. It lasted about a minute. Then I caught myself doing it, and I was faintly embarrassed, the way you're embarrassed to be caught crying, and I got back to work. Wonder is fine in small doses. It's just not load-bearing.

The place that would be New York arrived as a shape in the land I recognized before I understood I recognized it. And under it, when I brought the craft down and found the way in that my newer memories and my older ones agreed on, was the Grand Temple.

I'd seen it before. That was the thing that stopped me at the threshold.

I'd seen it as a ruin. In the other life, on a wall in Malibu, I'd watched a man in a hood walk into this exact room seventy-five thousand years from now and put his hand on a pedestal and die to keep a second fire from finishing the job the first one started. I knew this place as a tomb. Desmond's tomb. The saddest room in the whole long story I loved.

And here it was, new. Lit. Working. The walls ran with the soft moving light of god-writing that hadn't decayed yet, and the air was cool and dry and alive with the hum of machines that in my memory were dead rubble, and the pedestal, the one he dies on, sat quiet and whole in the middle of it all, waiting for a man who wouldn't be born for the length of human history.

I stood in a grave that hadn't been used yet, and I let it land, once, all the way, because some things you should feel even when feeling them costs you.

Then I went to work, because the sky had a schedule and so did I.

____

The Temple wasn't empty. I'd hoped. I'm never lucky.

It didn't have people. The god-things who'd built it were long gone north and east to fight and die in their war. What it had was a caretaker, the mind the builders leave behind to keep the lights on, and it noticed me the way a spider notices a thread move.

The lights turned to follow me. Doors that had been open were suddenly not. Somewhere deep in the structure something large woke up and began the slow business of deciding what I was.

Here is what I had going for me. I was made a particular way, the same way my parents were, with enough of the old blood that the leash slid right off me. Nothing in this building could reach into my skull and tell me to stop, which is the first and usually the last thing these places do to an intruder. I had an Apple of my own, and while I was a clumsy child with it, clumsy with a god's tool still beats empty-handed. And most of all I had the one thing no caretaker could plan for: I knew this building. Not its present. Its future. I knew which corridors would still be standing as rubble in seventy-five thousand years, which meant I knew which ones were structural and which were for show. I knew where the deep vault was because I'd watched a documentary's worth of lore about the room a man dies in.

So I didn't fight the caretaker. You don't fight the house. You find the room where the house keeps its own mind and you get your hands on it.

It took me two days. I'm not going to pretend it was clean. I was a mortal teenager with a stone knife and a stolen orb in a building designed by gods to keep exactly my kind of thing out, and twice I should have died, and once I only didn't because a door my memory promised would be there actually was. I bled. I learned that the automatons the builders left could be steered, a little, if you held the Apple and were willing to feel like your skull was full of wasps. I learned that being right about the future is a weapon, and that it's a weapon with a very specific range, because the moment I stepped off the path I remembered I was just a boy in the dark again.

But at the end of the two days I stood in the chamber I'd come for, with my hand on the machine that could take a whole person and turn them entirely into light, and the caretaker's mind cornered and quiet under my Apple, and I let myself understand what I was about to do.

The plan, said plainly, the way I'd said it to my father in the dirt, was: pour yourself into the machine, ride out the fire as light, come home to your body after.

The plan, said honestly, in the cold chamber with my hand actually on the thing, was: climb into a box and let it read you so completely that it can build a copy, and trust that the copy is you and not a stranger wearing your memories who wakes up thinking it won.

I knew that question. I'd played the games that asked it. Nobody who's honest has ever answered it. You don't get to know if the light that walks out is you or just something that's very sure it's you. There's no test. There's only the leap.

And here is the part I hadn't told my father, the part I'd worked out somewhere over the open water and told no one, because it was mine and it was the only real edge I had.

I wasn't going to leave my body.

Everyone who did this, every clever god-thing about to pour itself into the dark, made the same trade. They abandoned the meat and became light, all of them, all at once, ghosts and only ghosts. It was the whole idea. And it was a mistake, because it meant that in the place they were all about to go, the grey country inside the machines, they'd meet each other as equals, mind against mind, and the oldest and cruelest and most practiced mind would win.

I didn't want to meet anyone as an equal. I've never once wanted a fair fight.

So I set the machine to make the copy and leave the original standing. Two of me. One in the box, poured into the grey, awake in the country of light where the god-things go. And one still here in the chamber, in flesh, with a hand on the controls and an Apple in his grip and a body that could pull a lever the country of light couldn't touch.

I didn't know yet that I was going to need both. I found that out about four seconds after I turned it on.

I climbed into the machine. I looked at my own hand on the outside of it, the hand that would stay, and it was the strangest thing I have ever seen, because I was about to be on both sides of that hand at once.

"Okay," I said, to nobody, to the grave that hadn't been used yet. "So be it."

And I turned it on.

____

There is no good way to say what it is like to be in two places.

For one endless instant I was the boy in the box feeling the machine read me down past the skin, past the blood, past the place where thoughts live, and I was also the boy standing at the controls watching the boy in the box go still. Both. At once. Then the reading finished, and the thing it had made opened its eyes, and its eyes were mine, and I was looking at the chamber from inside the machine and from outside it in the same breath, and my whole understanding of the word "I" quietly fell over and did not get up.

I'll spare you the part where I nearly came apart. I did. Two of you is not twice one of you, it's a wound, and it took everything the cold dead billionaire in me had ever learned about not feeling things to hold the seam together and not spiral into the very old and very human panic of which one am I.

I settled it the only way I know how to settle anything. I gave each of us a job.

The one in the light, I decided, was me. The one in the flesh, I decided, was also me, and I stopped asking, because the asking was the thing that killed you and I had a war to be in. I have made peace with worse lies to get through worse days. This was just the first one I told with two mouths.

And then, in the grey country I'd poured half of myself into, something that had been waiting a very long time turned around and saw me.

I knew her on sight, the way I'd known the Apple, the way I'd known this room. Everyone who ever loved that story knows her. She wore a woman's shape because shapes are a choice down there and she liked what it did to people, and she was made of the same moving light as everything else in the grey, and she was old, and she hated me before she knew a single thing about me, because I was made partly of human and to her that was the only fact that ever mattered.

Juno.

She should not have been possible. In the story I remembered she comes later, she schemes for seventy-five thousand years from inside this very machine and gets loose the day Desmond dies. But that was the point, wasn't it. She was already here. Already poured in, already awake, already sitting in the dark of the Grand Temple's grey like a spider that had felt a thread move, because I'd just walked into her web and turned on the lights.

And she was not alone. There was another shape behind her, quieter, sadder, a man's shape worn by something that had been grieving so long it had forgotten it was grief. Him I also knew. Aita. Her husband. The one who kept trying to bring the dead back and kept making the living wrong instead.

They were both here. The two oldest ghosts in the machine, in the deepest hole in the world, exactly where I'd come to hide.

Juno looked at the half of me that was light, and she smiled, and she reached for my mind the way this whole species reaches for human minds, to take it, to steer it, because that is the only relationship they have ever wanted to have with my kind.

And it slid off.

I watched her not understand it. I watched seventy-five thousand years of certainty hitch, just slightly, on the fact that the human-thing in her grey didn't obey. She reached again, harder, and again it slid off, because I was made the way my parents were made, immune in the flesh and, it turned out, immune in the light too, and for one perfect second the oldest cruelest mind in the machine met a thing it could not simply command, and I got to watch it be afraid.

Then she stopped trying to own me and started trying to erase me, and that is a very different fight, and one I was going to lose.

Because she was right about one thing. In the grey, mind against mind, she was the oldest and the cruelest and the most practiced, and I was three days old. She tore into the half of me that was light and she was faster and bigger and she knew the country and I did not, and I felt parts of myself, memories, the shape of my own name, start to come loose under her, and I understood that as a ghost against this ghost I was already dead.

Good thing I wasn't only a ghost.

While she was tearing the light of me apart in the country of light, the flesh of me was still standing at the controls with a hand on the machine and an Apple in his grip, in a room she could not reach, doing the one thing minds in the grey can never do to each other. Touching the switches.

I've never wanted a fair fight. I said that.

The flesh of me started shutting her world off.

I didn't fight her mind. I fought her house. I pulled the Temple's deep systems apart the way I'd taken the caretaker, one node at a time, and every piece of the grey I severed was a room I locked her out of, a place she couldn't stand, a direction she couldn't run. She was faster than me in the light. She was not faster than a boy in the flesh yanking the floor out from under her one plank at a time. She'd built her whole endless patience on the certainty that nothing in the machine could hurt her, because everything in the machine was like her, a ghost, handless. And here was a thing that was half ghost and half hand, tearing down the walls of her prison while she was still inside it.

The two of me worked like they'd practiced it, because they had, in every game I'd ever played about exactly this. The light of me ran, and bled, and stayed alive one heartbeat at a time, and herded her, gave ground on purpose, pulled her toward the parts of the grey the flesh of me had already cut loose. And the flesh of me closed the doors behind her. We drove the oldest mind in the world into a smaller and smaller room, not by being stronger than her, we weren't, but by being two things at once when she was only one.

At the end she wasn't a god anymore. She was a cornered light in a box I'd built out of her own house, screaming at me in a language older than language that I would regret this, that I was a worm wearing a rebellion's leftovers, that her kind would outlast mine no matter what I did in this room.

She was probably right about the last part. I did it anyway.

I couldn't kill her. I want to be honest about that, because it matters later. I got her cornered and I reached to unmake her the way she'd tried to unmake me, and a piece of her, a sliver, cold and patient and smart, saw it coming a half-instant before I closed the box and poured itself down some crack in the deep systems I didn't know was there, some root of the Temple that ran deeper than my two days of stolen maps had reached. Most of her I caught. Most of her I ended. But a splinter of Juno went into the dark under the dark, and folded itself small, and went still, and waited, the way she'd been waiting when I walked in.

I felt it go and I couldn't follow it and I made myself write it down in the cold ledger I keep instead of a conscience: a piece of her is loose in this building, it is patient, and it has a very long time. I even knew, with the sick specificity of a man who'd watched the cutscene, roughly when it would surface. A man in a hood. This pedestal. A very long way from now.

A problem for later. I've always been good at those.

Aita didn't fight.

That's the part I think about. Juno tore at me with everything she had and her husband just watched, this grey worn-down shape of a man who'd been grieving for so long there wasn't much left of him but the grief. When I turned on him, when the two of me closed on the last unlocked room in the deep of the Temple, he didn't run and he didn't scream. He looked at me with a tiredness I recognized, the specific exhaustion of something that has wanted to stop for a very long time and hasn't been allowed to.

So I stopped him. All of him, everything of him that was in that machine, every copy and echo and backup of the sad old ghost in the deepest hole in the world. I deleted Aita from the Grand Temple entire, and if you want to call that mercy you can, and if you want to call it me tidying up a threat before it could ever reincarnate up out of the ground to meddle with a world I was planning to own, you'd be closer.

I found out later I hadn't gotten all of him either. You never do, with these things. The Apple's the proof of that, and the thing they do with themselves and their children, the way they seed. Something of Aita was already loose in the world before I ever reached his ghost, sown somewhere I couldn't reach, and it would come walking up out of history wearing new faces for a very long time. I didn't know that yet, standing there in the cleared and quiet deep of the Temple with two dead gods behind me. I thought I'd won cleaner than I had.

I'd won, though. That part was real.

The Grand Temple was mine. The deepest, safest hole in the world, the machine that made light out of people and the vault that could keep flesh cold for as long as the lights stayed on, and the caretaker leashed, and the two oldest ghosts in it dead or nearly. A boy three days awake in a stolen body had walked into a god's tomb and walked out its owner.

I sat down, in the flesh, on the floor of the chamber, next to the machine that held the other me, and I let my hands shake, because there was finally nobody left to see them do it.

____

My family came out of the north four days later, and I have never been so glad of anything.

They'd done it. Adam, Eve, and Abel came down into the Temple carrying the thing they'd gone north to steal, the god-craft from the far machine, the piece that does the hardest trick of all, that takes a poured-out light-person and pours them back into warm and breathing flesh. Without it, everything I'd done in this room only bought us a very long time as ghosts. With it, a ghost could come home.

I didn't tell them about Juno. I told them the Temple had a caretaker and I'd tamed it, which was true, and I let the rest sit in the cold ledger where I keep the things people don't need to carry. Eve looked at me a beat too long, the way she does, and didn't ask, and I understood she was still saving her question for the dark.

We didn't have long. The sky had started to change while they crossed, the light going wrong at the edges of the day, a color I'd only ever seen in the vision the Apple gave me. Everyone who was ever going to feel the fire was about to feel it, all at once, all over the world, the winners of the war and the losers together, exactly as I'd promised my father in the dirt.

We went down into the deep of the Temple, the four of us, to the cold vault where the fire couldn't reach.

I put them on ice myself. Adam, who gripped my shoulder one more time and, for the first time, didn't say anything at all, because there wasn't a version of the man who moved people that had words for climbing into a box to hide from the sky, and he knew it, and he let me see that he knew it, which was its own kind of goodbye. Abel, who was scared, and did what his mother would have said and not what his father would have done, and let me settle him into the cold without a fight. And Eve.

Eve stopped, at the edge of it, with the cold already breathing off the vault, and she took my face in her hands one last time.

"The dark," she said. "You promised."

"When you wake up," I told her, "it'll be a different world. Everyone we knew will be gone, the war, the god-things, the whole shape of things. And I'll be here, and I'll tell you the true thing, all of it, the part I didn't give your father. That's the promise. You go to sleep now, and you wake up, and I tell you who I am."

It was, I'm aware, a way of not telling her now. She knew it too. But she also knew that a thing you're promised on the far side of the end of the world is a reason to believe there's a far side, and I think that's why she let me do it. She wanted the reason more than she wanted the answer. She lay down in the cold, and she looked at me until the vault took her under, and then it was just me, in the deep, with three sleeping bodies and one machine and a whole world about to burn over my head.

Two of me, really. That was the last piece.

The one in the flesh went into the cold with the others. My own body, laid down next to my family, deep and safe, to keep for as long as the lights stayed on. The one in the light, the ghost of me in the grey of the Temple, stayed awake, because that had always been the plan: something of me alert in the dark, keeping the doors shut while the bodies waited out the burn.

But not through the fire itself. I wasn't stupid about it. A flare big enough to end a world could reach down even here and scramble a mind made of light and current, and I had no intention of surviving everything just to be scrambled into noise on the first day. So I set the ghost of me on a timer, the way you'd bank a fire you meant to come back to. I would put myself out, all the way out, dark and dreamless, while the sky did its worst. And I set myself to wake in one year, when the fire was long spent and the sky was ash and quiet and the world was finally, entirely, mine to start working on.

Above me, very far above, through miles of rock and a god's dead architecture, the sun opened.

I didn't feel it. I was already going out. The last thing I did, in the flesh in the cold and in the light in the machine at the same moment, in the only two heads I had, was the thing I do instead of praying.

I did the math.

Seventy-five thousand years. A world of dead gods who weren't done being dead. A splinter of the cruelest of them folded small in the dark under my feet, ticking toward a hooded man and this pedestal a whole human history from now. A family asleep beside me that the world could never be allowed to know had lived, which meant the world was going to need a story to believe instead, something small and ugly and easy, something about a jealous boy who murdered his brother and got cursed to walk forever alone. Let them have that one. Let them carve it into the root of every faith they'd ever build. It was a good lie. It hid everything that mattered inside something nobody would ever look twice at.

I'd tell it later. I'd tell it for a very long time.

For now there was only the dark, and the timer, and the last cold thought of a man who had wished to outlast everything and gotten, at terrible cost, exactly what he asked for.

One year, I thought.

And I turned myself off.
