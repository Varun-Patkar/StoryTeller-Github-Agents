# Chapter 5: The Deathless

The sky started lying three days before it killed everyone.

It began at the edges. A wrongness in the color of the evening that only Cain knew to read, a green that had no business being up there, a shimmer along the north horizon like heat coming off a stone that wasn't warm. The rebels saw it and thought it was beautiful. Some of them took it for an omen of victory, the masters' own heavens turning strange in the season the masters fell.

They were half right. It was an omen. It just wasn't theirs.

"That's not weather," Nara said, the first night it showed. She stood at the mouth of the pit with her arms crossed, watching the green crawl. "You've been watching the north for a week. You knew this was coming."

"I did," Cain said.

"Since when."

"Since before I met you." He didn't look up from his work. "Since the grass."

She was quiet a while. Behind them, deep in the pit, the thing he'd been building for a season made its low patient sound in the dark, and the light of it moved on the stone walls.

"What is it," she said finally. "Really. Not the war story. The other one."

He'd told her, at the city, that there was always another one. He supposed she'd earned this piece of it.

"The sky is going to open," he said. "In three days, maybe four. Not rain. Fire, of a kind, more than a kind, poured down out of the sun all at once. It won't care who won the war. It won't care about leashes or masters or brave farmers with sharp sticks. It will burn the towers and the tents the same, and when it's done there will be almost nothing left that breathes." He set down the tool he'd been holding. "That's what the masters were afraid of. That's what they were all looking at while we robbed them. They ran the numbers on it for a hundred years and the numbers never once came out kind."

Nara took that the way she took everything, flat, without flinching, because flinching cost energy and she was a woman who spent nothing she didn't have to.

"And this," she said, nodding at the pit, at the humming light. "This is how you live through it."

"This is how I live through everything," Cain said. "Forever, if I've built it right."

____

He had not built it to be understood, and Nara didn't try. She just looked at the vessel for a long moment, the basin of stolen light at the bottom of the pit, the cradle of it, the small careful machine he'd copied out of a god's own house.

"There's room for one," she said. Not a question. She'd always been quick.

"There's room for one."

"You."

"Me."

She nodded slowly. She didn't argue. That was the thing about arithmetic loyalty. It cut both ways, and she'd always known the sum as well as he did.

"You could have told the camp," she said. "Adam. Eve. All of them. You could have told them what's coming."

"I'm going to tell them enough," Cain said. "Not the truth. A man who knows the exact hour the world ends is a man people start to wonder about, and I have spent a great deal of effort being a man nobody wonders about." He looked at her. "But I'm going to save them anyway. Some of them. Enough."

"Why. You don't care about them."

"No." He picked the tool back up. "But a world with no people in it is worth nothing to a man who plans to be here after. I need seed, Nara. I need there to still be a herd when I come back up, or all of this is just an expensive way to be alone in the dark." He said it plainly, because she was the one person he never bothered lying to, and because it was true. "I'm not saving them. I'm saving my own future. They just happen to be standing in it."

"That's ugly," Nara said.

"It keeps them alive," Cain said. "You of all people should like it."

She almost smiled. She didn't, but it was close.

____

He went to Adam and Eve on the second night, when the green in the north had spread to a bruise across half the sky and even the bravest of the rebels had stopped calling it beautiful.

He'd expected it to be hard. It wasn't. That was the strange mercy of the last days: the whole camp could feel that something enormous was wrong, and they were looking for someone to tell them what to do, and the Knife had never once been wrong about which way the masters would jump.

"The sky is sick," he told them, in Eve's tent, with the bruise-light leaking through the seams. "I don't know all of it. I know it's coming from where the masters have been staring, and I know they were terrified, and I know terror in a god means the rest of us should already be running." He crouched and drew, the way he always drew. "There are deep caves in the western hills. Old ones. Below the stone, below the reach of weather and light and whatever the sky is about to do. We take everyone we can carry food and water for, and we go down, and we stay down until it's over."

"For how long," Adam said.

"Longer than you'll want to. Weeks. Maybe more. However long the sky takes to stop being a weapon." He met the big man's eyes. "Bring the children. Bring the old ones who can still walk. Bring the stores, all of them. And Adam, bring your immune. You and Eve and yours. That blood matters more than you know. If anything crawls out of those hills after, it should be yours."

Adam looked to Eve. He always did, at the turns that mattered.

And Eve, who trusted almost no one and Cain least of all, sat with her sharp eyes on him for a long moment, and Cain watched her mind do the same work his did, weigh the man against the sky, weigh the cost of believing him against the cost of not.

"You're not coming," she said.

It wasn't a guess. He'd known she'd get there.

"No," he said.

"Why."

"Because I have something to finish here, and because a man like me doesn't do well packed into a cave with three hundred frightened people for a month." He gave her the small true smile he saved for her, the one that admitted the joke. "You've wanted me gone since the night I walked in. Here's your chance to get it without doing the work yourself. Take the people down. Live. That's the whole of what I'm asking."

Eve stood. For a moment he thought she'd say the other thing, the one that had been in her face since the tent a season ago, the one about what a man who steers you for his own gain does after the masters are bones.

Instead she said, quietly, "Whatever you are, you led them out of the worst year they've ever had. I don't understand you and I don't trust you and I'm not sure you're on anyone's side but your own." A pause. "But you're right about the caves. I felt it before you said it. So we'll go down." Her jaw tightened. "Don't imagine that makes us even."

"I'd never insult you like that," Cain said, and meant it.

They took the people down the next morning. Three hundred and some, roped together, carrying everything they could lift, filing into the western hills under a sky the color of a wound. The seed of everything that would ever come after, Adam and Eve at the front of it, and not one of them knowing that the strange quiet man who'd pointed the way had done it to keep his own investments alive.

He watched them go from the ridge. He felt something, watching. He declined to name it. There would be time to name things later. He had nothing but time coming.

____

Nara wouldn't go.

He'd known that would be the hard one. He'd left it for last, the way you leave the worst chore, and now the third day was dying and the north was on fire in slow motion and there was no more last left to leave it for.

"Go with them," he said. "The caves. Adam's already asking where you are."

"I pick you," Nara said. "I've always picked you. Why would that change now."

"Because the thing I'm about to do has room for one, and it isn't a cave, and you can't follow me into it." He kept his voice even. "You'll die up here. Not in a month. In about a day. The caves are the only door that stays open for you, and it's closing."

"Then I'll die up here."

"No," Cain said, "you won't," and he heard his own voice go somewhere he hadn't sent it.

She stopped. She looked at him, and for once the flat hunter's calm cracked, just a hairline, just enough for him to see the person underneath the arithmetic.

"You're not coming out of that pit," she said slowly. "Are you. Whatever you do down there. The camp's going to say the Knife died in the cataclysm. And it'll be true, near enough. Won't it."

He could have lied. He was good at lying. He looked at her, at the first leash he'd ever cut on purpose, the one loyalty in the world he'd never had to buy with anything but staying alive, and he found that he didn't want to spend one of his lies on her. Not this one.

"Something like that," he said. "The man you followed is going to end in that pit. What comes out, if anything ever does, won't be him. Not the way you knew him." He held her eyes. "You've spent a year betting on the animal most likely to live. So bet right one last time. The caves live. I don't, not in any way that's any use to you. Go where the living is."

For a long moment she didn't move. The sky burned quietly behind her.

"You saved the immune because their blood matters," she said. "You saved the camp because you need a herd. You've got a reason filed for every person you've ever kept." Her voice was rough. "What's my reason, Cain. What ledger am I in."

And there it was. The one sum he'd been refusing to work since the city, the account he'd told himself not to audit.

"I don't have one for you," he said. "That's the truth. You're the one entry I can't make balance. I keep you because I keep you." He almost stopped there. He didn't. "That's as close to a reason as you're going to get from a man like me, and it's more than I've given anyone. Now go live, so that somewhere up there, in the world I'm doing all this to save, there's one person who was never on a list."

Nara stared at him. Then she did the thing he would carry down into the dark with him, the one image he chose to keep out of all of it. She stepped in, and put her rough hunter's hand flat against the side of his face, once, hard, the way you'd check that a thing was real.

"Idiot," she said. "You could have just said that a year ago."

Then she turned and walked toward the western hills, toward the living, and she did not look back, because looking back cost something and she never spent what she didn't have to.

He watched until the dark and the distance took her. Then he went down into the pit to end.

____

The sky opened on the fourth day, exactly as the masters' numbers had promised.

He felt it before he saw it, deep as he was. A pressure, a wrongness, the whole world drawing a breath it wouldn't let out. Then the light came down.

Even underground, even at the bottom of everything, he knew when it hit. The stone above him lit at its edges. The air in the pit went strange and charged. Far up the shaft, the darkness turned the color of a forge, and stayed that color, and he understood that above him the whole of the sky had become fire, that the towers were going, that the tents and the fields and everything that had ever stood in the open was going, that the world was being unmade in silence over his head while he stood in the one hole deep enough to be spared.

He did not watch. There was nothing to watch. There was only the work, and the work was the last thing, and he turned to it.

The vessel was ready. He'd made it ready. The basin of stolen light waited, patient as the machine it was copied from, and beside it the cradle he'd built for the body, the stone womb where his own flesh would lie while the world above burned and healed and forgot, for however many thousands of years it took.

He'd already seen to the rest. The tools that could have copied what he was about to do, broken. The few marks on the stone that a clever eye might one day read, scoured smooth. When the sky was finished with the world, there would be nothing up top to say the Knife had done anything but die with everyone else, and nothing down here for anyone to find, because he'd built the pit to close behind him like a wound sealing over. History would get its one line about Cain and not a word more. He'd made certain of it. Anonymity was the second half of immortality. A deathless man the world remembered was just a target that lasted longer.

He lay down in the light.

He had thought, at the end, he might be afraid. He wasn't. The man from the apartment and the first murderer had agreed on very little in their short shared life, but they agreed on this, on the wanting of it, on the refusal to simply stop the way everything else stopped. He reached into the vessel the way he'd reached into the Apple, the way he'd reached into every leash he'd ever cut, with everything he had and nothing held back.

And the vessel reached back, and began, gently, to lift.

It did not hurt. That surprised him. It felt like being read. Every weight and grudge and memory of him, the grass and the Apple and the ford and the god's cold house and a rough hand against his face, all of it lifted out of the failing meat and carried down into the dark country he had built to hold it. It did not feel like copying and it did not feel like leaving. It felt like being poured, slow and complete, from a cup that was cracking into one that would not. The body on the stone went still, and slept, and would keep sleeping. The self went elsewhere.

The last thing he felt through the body's fading senses was the heat coming down the shaft, the world's fire reaching for him and finding him already gone. The masters had marked him, and history had marked him, the first of all the dead-makers, the man with the worst name there was.

And now the sky itself had come for him, the way it came for everyone.

And, alone of everyone, he had refused it.

____

There was dark, and then there was the Core.

He came into it slowly, the way you surface from deep water, and it was not like waking and it was not like dreaming. He simply was, in a place that had no light because it needed none, no body because it had left that behind on a stone above, no sky to burn him and no ground to hold him. There was only the vast patient architecture he had stolen and rebuilt, humming in the dark under a dead world, and him, poured into it, whole.

He reached out, carefully, and felt the shape of what he'd made. The country of one. His own grey. It went further than he'd dared hope, dark and deep and utterly his, and it would grow. He would make it grow. He had, after all, nothing but time.

Far above, the world was ash and fire and would be for a long while yet. Somewhere in the western hills, under the stone, three hundred frightened people were still breathing in the dark, Adam and Eve among them, the seed intact. And one hunter who was never on a list. They would climb out, eventually, into a scoured and empty world, and they would think the Knife had died in the cataclysm, and they would be right, and they would be wrong, and there would be no one left alive who could tell the difference.

Cain settled into the dark of the thing he'd become, and did the only sum that mattered now, the one that stretched out ahead of him past the ash, past the healing, past the slow return of green and grain and the first stammering rediscovery of everything humanity had just lost.

Tens of thousands of years. That was the number now. Not weeks. Not days. Ages.

He had wanted to be deathless, like the Cain in the oldest story, the killer the world could not kill. He had gotten his wish, entire, and the price of it was this: to be the one thing that lasted, in the dark, alone, while everything he had ever touched turned to dust and myth above him.

He found, examining it, that he could live with the price.

He had always been able to live with the price. That was rather the whole point of him.

Above, the sky burned itself out. Below, in the dark, the deathless man began, patiently, to plan.
