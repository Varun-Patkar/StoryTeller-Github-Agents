## Outline

1. The morning after the river, Coen gives his family a simple account that hides the warning and his restored memories.
2. He decides future knowledge must remain secret because even people who love him could expose it through fear, faith, or careless words.
3. Coen visits Anca under the ordinary excuse of thanking her for helping Alin and asks her to teach him to read.
4. Anca tests whether his interest will survive dull work and chores rather than showing him magic.
5. Coen begins with letters and Latin, privately accepting that reaching witchcraft will take years.

**Arc beats advanced:** Coen adopts permanent secrecy and begins his credible path toward Anca's witchcraft.
**Foreshadowing planted:** Anca's covered rune scars; forbidden books require literacy; Coen's preparation will be slow and physical.

## Chapter Text

Alin woke hungry.

That was the first good thing Coen heard in the morning.

His brother sat close to the stove with both blankets still around him and tore bread into a bowl of hot milk. Esme watched every bite. Whenever Alin paused, her hand moved toward him before she stopped herself.

Pieter sat across the table.

"Tell me again," he said.

Coen had expected this. He had spent half the night deciding what to say.

"The ice cracked. I took a branch and pulled him out."

Pieter looked at Alin. "That what happened?"

Alin swallowed. "Yes."

"And you went out there after I told you to gather wood."

Alin looked down at his bowl.

"He won't do it again," Coen said.

"Did I ask you?"

"No."

Pieter held out his hand. "Knife."

Alin slowly took the little eating knife from his belt and placed it in their father's palm. Pieter had given it to him in autumn after Alin spent weeks asking for one of his own.

"You get it back when I trust you near the river."

Alin's face crumpled. "But I need it."

"You need sense more. Eat."

Esme put another piece of bread in Alin's bowl. She had been quiet since the boys returned, but not empty or distant. When Mirto reached for the hot pot, she caught him by the wrist without looking and moved him back onto the bench. Lunka sat beside her, too small to understand the argument and happy to steal crumbs from her mother's plate.

They were all here.

Coen had to look away for a moment.

"What?" Pieter asked.

"Nothing."

His father watched him. Coen kept his face still and drank his milk.

He would keep the rest to himself.

He trusted his family. That was not the problem. Yanna could let something slip while angry. Alin could boast. Pieter might take a warning to the priest or the village headman. Esme would worry until she asked the wrong person for help.

The truth was too strange. A dead man from another world. A game that knew their names. Plague, vampires, and deaths that had not happened yet.

Even if they believed him, nothing good would follow.

He would tell no one.

Not about the other life. Not about the future. Not about the lights between worlds.

He could still use what he knew. He only needed ordinary reasons for the things he did.

Pieter pushed back from the table. "You lost the wood."

"I left it by the river."

"Then fetch more. Stay off the ice."

"I will."

"Take Yanna."

Yanna made a face but did not argue. Pieter left for the yard, taking Alin's knife with him.

Coen waited until the door closed.

"Mama, can I go see Anca after the wood?"

Esme looked over. "Why?"

"She gave us the warming herbs. I should tell her Alin is well."

That part was true. One of the neighbours had fetched Anca during the night, and she had brought herbs for the bath and stayed until Alin stopped shaking.

Esme touched Coen's forehead. "You were sick too."

"I'm better."

"Be back before dark."

"I will."

Anca's hut stood beyond the last cluster of village homes, close enough for people to find her and far enough that the smell did not bother anyone. Bundles of drying plants hung beneath the roof. Even in winter, the place smelled of smoke, bitter leaves, and damp earth.

Coen knocked.

"Come."

Anca sat at a table sorting roots into small clay bowls. A knife lay beside her hand. Books and loose pages occupied the dry end of the table, safely away from the herbs.

Coen noticed the books first.

Yesterday, they would have been useless marks to him. Now he recognized the alphabet, though the words themselves appeared to be Latin. His old life had given him English and fragments of other modern languages, not enough Latin to read the page.

Anca looked up from the roots. "How is Alin? Did he sleep?"

"He woke twice. Mama kept him by the stove. He ate bread and milk this morning."

"Good. If he can eat, he should be all right."

"Papa took his knife."

"That might keep him off the river."

"Mama told me to thank you for coming last night."

"She thanked me herself."

"I know. I wanted to as well."

Anca studied him for a moment. "You walked all this way to say that?"

He had prepared three different answers. All of them sounded false now that she was looking at him.

"No. I wanted to ask you something. I want to learn to read."

Anca glanced at the books. "Why now?"

"I don't want to spend my life in the mine."

It was the best excuse because it was also true. In the game, Coen had learned Latin while dreaming of a life beyond Laslea. He did not need future knowledge to want more than years underground breathing silver dust.

"Most boys don't want the mine," Anca said. "They still go."

"I want to go somewhere else."

"Where?"

"I don't know."

Anca returned to the roots. "Reading will not give you a horse."

"I know."

"It will not fill your purse."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Coen said nothing.

Anca picked up a dark root and cut away a soft patch. "Books and ink cost money. Lessons take time too. If I teach you, I need to know you will keep coming when it stops being new."

"I'll learn."

"We will see."

"I won't stop."

Her knife stopped.

Coen worried that he had sounded too old. Anca pushed one of the clay bowls toward him.

"Separate those. Long roots here. Split ones there. Rotten ones in the fire."

"Then you'll teach me?"

"Help me with these first. I can show you the letters while we work."

Coen sat.

There were more roots than the bowl had seemed able to hold. Some were straight, some forked near the end, and several differed only by a soft brown patch close to the stem. His hands soon smelled bitter.

Anca showed him how the long roots narrowed near the tip and how rot softened beneath the dirt. When Coen placed one in the wrong bowl, she held it beside a good root and asked him what was different. He found the soft patch himself.

The task took most of the afternoon.

When the last root was sorted, Anca checked every bowl. She moved one split root from the long pile but found nothing else wrong.

"Tomorrow," she said.

Coen blinked. "Tomorrow?"

"After your chores. If your parents agree."

"For reading?"

"For letters. The sweeping is because my floor needs it."

He nodded quickly.

Anca wiped her knife and reached for a bundle above the table. Her sleeve pulled back.

Several old scars crossed the inside of her forearm. They were too straight to be accidents. Thin lines joined at sharp corners, forming part of a shape Coen remembered from the game.

A rune.

Anca lowered her arm, and the sleeve covered it again.

Coen looked back at the roots before she could notice.

He had found the right person to teach him.

That did not mean he could ask for witchcraft. Not yet. Anca guarded those scars in a village where the wrong priest could have her dragged away. A child who somehow knew what they meant would frighten her.

So he would sweep. He would learn letters he already half recognized. He would study Latin, herbs, and whatever else she placed in front of him.

When Anca finally trusted him with the marks that opened the way to witchcraft, he would be ready.

"Before dark," Anca said.

Coen looked through the window. The sun had dropped behind the trees.

He jumped from the stool.

Anca laughed as he ran for the door.

"Tomorrow!" he called.

"Chores first."

Coen ran home with empty hands and the first part of a plan.
