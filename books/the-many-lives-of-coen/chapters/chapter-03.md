## Chapter Text

By spring, Coen could read the labels on Anca's jars.

Most were only one or two words: names of roots, leaves, dried flowers, and powders. Anca wrote each name in charcoal on a piece of scrap wood and tied it to the jar with string. Coen still made mistakes.

One afternoon, Anca asked for ground comfrey. He brought her dried nettle instead.

She held up the tag. "Read it."

Coen stared at the letters. "Nettle."

"And what did I ask for?"

"Comfrey."

Anca placed the nettle beside the comfrey. "Look at the leaves. How can you tell them apart without the tags?"

Coen rubbed one leaf between his fingers and smelled it. "This one is sharper."

"Good. Now you will remember with more than your eyes."

She sent him back to the shelf.

Anca praised him when he found an answer, then asked how he had reached it. When he guessed wrong, she usually gave him another piece and let him try again.

The work suited him. His old memories had not given him Latin, but they had taught him how to sit still, repeat a task, and keep going after the first excitement wore off. He learned the local letters quickly. Latin was slower. The endings of words kept changing, so Anca taught him to use the rest of the sentence.

"Start with what you know," she told him. "Who is doing something? What are they doing? Then make your best guess at the rest."

At home, nobody questioned the time he spent with her as long as his other work was finished. Pieter checked the woodpile before letting him leave. Esme found more uses for him once he could recognize Anca's labels. Yanna made him read the same instructions twice whenever medicine was involved.

Alin was the only one who complained.

"You never come to the hill now."

"I went yesterday."

"You left early."

"Papa called me."

"You always leave."

Coen stopped tying his boots. Alin stood by the door with a stick over one shoulder. Since the river, he had stayed away from deep water, but nowhere else had become safer. He still climbed trees, jumped from roofs, and fought boys larger than him.

"I'll come after my lesson," Coen said.

"You'll be late."

"Then wait."

Alin scowled. "Bring your sword."

It was a wooden practice blade with a split near the handle. Calling it a sword was generous.

"All right."

"Promise?"

"Promise."

Alin left before Coen could change his mind.

Esme watched from beside the stove. She looked tired, but she was on her feet, kneading dough with both hands. Mirto sat under the table with two carved animals. Lunka had fallen asleep in a basket lined with old wool.

"Do not make him wait," Esme said.

"I won't."

"You said that last time."

Coen finished tying his boots. "Anca kept me."

"Anca would have let you leave if you told her Alin was waiting."

Coen had no answer for that.

Esme dusted flour from her hands and gave him a small cloth bundle. Inside were two rolls with onions baked into the top.

"Take these to Anca. She will say no if I ask first."

Coen tucked the bundle inside his coat and left.

---

Anca accepted the rolls without arguing.

She ate one while Coen read from a thin page covered in cramped Latin. The writing concerned a saint, a fever, and a village whose name he could not pronounce. At least, he thought it did.

He reached the final line and stopped.

"Again," Anca said.

"I read it."

"You guessed without using the sentence. Try again. Who is the subject?"

Coen looked down at the page. "The saint."

"Good. Now find the verb."

He went back through the sentence. One word had the same root as the Latin word for water, but the ending was different. Another word looked close to something Anca had taught him last week.

"He washed them?"

"He washed their wounds."

Anca pointed to the final word. "This tells you what he washed. You were close."

Coen read the line again.

Anca reached across the table and pointed to the changed ending. Her sleeve slid up her forearm. The old scars appeared again.

Coen had seen them many times over the past months. Anca covered them quickly whenever patients visited. When they were alone, she sometimes forgot.

Now that he knew more letters, he could see that the scars were not letters at all. The lines formed a precise shape with smaller cuts branching away from it. It looked simple until he tried to follow the whole pattern.

Anca pulled her hand back. "The next line."

Coen kept his eyes on the page. "What are those marks?"

The room went quiet.

Outside, someone led a mule along the road. Its bell faded toward the village.

"Scars," Anca said.

"I know. Who made them?"

"I did."

Coen looked up. "Why?"

Anca folded her sleeve down over her wrist. "Finish the page."

"Do the marks mean something?"

Anca watched him for long enough that Coen wished he had waited another month.

"Lock the door," she said.

He did.

"Now the shutters."

Coen closed those too. The hut became dim except for the fire and one small oil lamp.

Anca took the page away and set it beneath the other books.

"It is witchcraft," she said. "These marks open the way for it. Once the gate is made, a witch can learn different hexes and prepare the ones she means to use."

Coen waited. He wanted to ask everything at once, but that would be the quickest way to end the lesson.

"Does it hurt?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Every time?"

"The first carving hurts, and it must be exact. After that, each hex takes blood when it is used. A careless witch can kill herself without an enemy touching her."

Anca rested both arms on the table.

"It is forbidden. If the wrong priest sees the marks, I hang. If the Inquisition comes, hanging may be the kind end. You will not speak of this outside the hut."

"I won't."

"Not to Alin. Not to your mother. Not because you are frightened for me, and not because someone promises they only want to help."

"I won't tell."

Anca knew nothing about his other secret. She had still given him the same rule.

"Teach me," Coen said.

"No."

The answer came so quickly that he almost asked again.

"Why?"

"You are a child. You have read jar labels for one season. Witchcraft begins with cutting the gate into your skin, and the scars stay where people can find them. That is enough reason."

Coen looked at her covered arm. "Will you teach me when I'm older?"

"Maybe."

"What do I have to do?"

Anca sighed and leaned back.

"First, keep learning your letters and Latin. They will not teach you witchcraft, but they will teach you how to study without rushing. The hexes use older glyphs. Much of that knowledge came from the Uriashi, and you do not know their signs yet."

"Will you teach me?"

"When you can read a full page without turning a saint into a fish. Then we begin the glyphs."

Coen nodded.

"Second, learn which herbs stop bleeding and which ones only hide the pain. You will know them by sight, smell, and touch. No labels."

"All right."

"Third, keep my secret."

"I can do that."

"You have not had time to prove that."

Anca pinched the cuff of her sleeve and pulled it farther over her wrist. "And think about this. Once the gate is on your arm, you hide it every day. Long sleeves in summer. Cloth wraps while you work. Gloves when you can get them. One careless moment can be enough for someone to call you a witch."

Coen closed his mouth.

Anca pushed the Latin page back toward him. "Ask me again when you can do all three."

Coen bent over the page.

"The saint washed their wounds," he read.

"Better."

"Then the fever broke."

"Good."

He finished the rest of the passage with Anca giving him a hint twice.

When he left the hut, the sun was still above the trees. Alin would be waiting on the hill with two sticks and several complaints.

Coen started running.
