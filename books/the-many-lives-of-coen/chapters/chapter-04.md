## Chapter Text

Three years passed before Anca let Coen ask again.

During that time, he studied at Anca's hut, finished his work at home, and trained whenever Pieter or Alin was willing.

Latin became easier once he stopped trying to match every word to one from his old life. Anca taught him to find the subject, the verb, and the ending that changed who did what. When he guessed, she asked him how he reached the answer. Sometimes his reasoning was enough. Sometimes she gave him another clue.

The magical glyphs were harder. They were older than the books in Anca's hut and did not behave like ordinary writing. One line could mean heat when it bent inward and hunger when it bent out. A circle might close a working, contain it, or turn it back on the person using it. Anca said much of the craft came from the Uriashi and their skin-signs, though human witches had copied and changed the practice over generations.

Coen learned herbs beside the glyphs. He gathered comfrey, yarrow, nettle, and plants whose names had no meaning in his old world. Anca made him identify them without labels. Later she made him do it by smell. By the third year, he could clean a wound, stop ordinary bleeding, and tell when a cut needed more help than he could give.

He kept training with Pieter too. Sword practice in the yard became harder drills, long walks with loaded baskets, and wrestling with Alin whenever his brother decided lessons had stolen enough of Coen's time.

Alin won more often than he used to.

Alin never learned why Coen visited Anca so much. No one did. Coen had kept her secret for three years.

---

Anca tested him at the end of spring.

She placed six dried plants on the table. No tags. Two were harmless, two stopped bleeding, one dulled pain, and one made a wound bleed harder.

"Show me what you would use after a deep cut," she said.

Coen picked up the first bundle and rubbed a leaf between his fingers. It smelled bitter but clean.

"Yarrow."

"Yes."

The second had thicker leaves and a sweet smell underneath the dust.

"Comfrey."

Anca waited.

"For closing the wound after it is clean," Coen added. "Not inside a deep wound."

"Good. What do you avoid?"

He pointed to a narrow red stem. "That one. It keeps blood moving."

"And this?"

Coen smelled the final bundle. "For pain."

"Would you use it?"

"Not before I know how bad the cut is."

Anca gathered the plants and returned them to their jars.

Next came a piece of slate marked with three glyphs. Coen named the open line, the binding turn, and the sign that directed the working through the body instead of away from it. Anca changed one short stroke and asked what happened.

"It turns back."

"Where?"

He followed the lines with one finger. "Into the arm."

"How much?"

"I don't know."

"Neither do I. That is why we do not guess."

Her voice came out clipped. She sounded like the abbey teacher she had described more than once.

Anca noticed it too. She rubbed the bridge of her nose.

"My teacher at the abbey would have made me copy the whole glyph a hundred times," she said. "I hated her for it. Tell me what part you do understand."

Coen pointed to the outer line. "This sends the working back. The inner marks decide how strongly, but I don't know those yet."

"Exactly. You know enough to stop instead of pretending."

She took the slate away.

"Last question. Who have you told?"

"No one."

"Alin?"

"No."

"Your mother?"

"No."

"Has anyone asked about my arms?"

"Yanna asked why you wear sleeves while gathering in summer. I said the plants scratch."

Anca raised an eyebrow. "Do they?"

"Some do."

"A useful answer."

She opened the chest beneath her table.

Inside lay folded cloth, a narrow knife, a small bottle of strong spirits, and a pair of dark leather gloves. The gloves were worn but carefully mended.

Coen stared at them.

"You are still young," Anca said. "I may be making a mistake."

"You said to ask again."

"I did. I did not promise to say yes."

"Are you saying yes?"

Anca looked at him for a long moment. "I am saying that if we do this, you follow every instruction. If you feel faint, you tell me. If the wound grows hot or begins to smell, you come here. You do not try a hex alone until I allow it."

"I understand."

"Repeat it."

Coen did, using his own words. Anca made him include the part about not hiding an infection.

Only then did she set his left arm on the table.

She washed it, shaved a strip of hair, and drew the gate in dark ink from below his elbow toward his wrist. The pattern was larger than he expected. It followed the inside of his forearm and split into narrow branches near the base of his palm.

"This is the gate," Anca said. "Not a hex. The hexes come later. They pass through this."

Coen studied the lines until he could follow them without losing his place.

"Ready?"

He was not.

"Yes."

The knife was sharper than any blade in his father's house. The first cut burned. The second hurt worse because Coen knew what was coming.

Anca worked slowly. She spoke whenever she changed direction and warned him before the deeper turns. Coen bit down on folded leather and kept his arm still.

Blood filled the lines. Anca wiped it away, checked the pattern, and continued.

By the time she finished, sweat had soaked Coen's shirt. His arm throbbed from elbow to palm.

Anca covered the cuts with a thin layer of paste and wrapped them in clean cloth.

"No magic today," she said.

Coen spat out the leather. "I wasn't going to ask."

"You looked ready to ask."

She slid the gloves across the table.

"Wear the left one whenever you leave home. When anyone asks, the wrap protects a burn from the forge. Once it heals, use a cloth bracer under the glove. Never trust a sleeve by itself. Sleeves pull back."

Coen put the gloves into his coat.

"Thank you."

"Wait until tomorrow before thanking me."

---

The gate healed into dark red scars over the next two weeks.

Anca checked it every day. She made Coen wash it, change the wrap, and describe every patch of heat or itching. Only when the swelling disappeared did she bring out a small slate bearing a new glyph.

"Mercurial Fervour," she said. "It makes the body faster. We begin with a short distance because your legs will not know what to do with that speed."

They walked beyond the village to a level stretch of grass between two stands of pine. Anca had chosen ground without rocks, trees, or a cliff nearby.

Coen wore a cloth bracer over the gate and the old glove over that. Anca made him remove both.

She taught him the glyph in pieces. He traced each line on the slate until he could reproduce the whole sign from memory. Then Anca showed him how to prepare it through the gate. No new scar appeared, but the old pattern tightened beneath his skin.

Coen felt a faint pull beneath his skin.

"That is enough," Anca said. "You have prepared the hex. Open the gate here when you cast. Only a small cut."

She touched a short line near his wrist.

Coen used a lancet. A bead of blood rose from the scar.

The world tightened around him.

"Run to the stump," Anca said. "Then stop."

The stump stood perhaps thirty paces away.

Coen ran.

His first step carried him too far. His second came before he had recovered from the first. The grass blurred beneath his boots, and the stump rushed toward him.

He tried to stop.

His feet tangled. Coen hit the ground shoulder-first, rolled once, and ended on his back with the sky spinning above him.

Anca reached him at an ordinary run.

"Anything broken?"

Coen moved his fingers, then his legs. "No."

"Head?"

"Still there."

She helped him sit. Blood continued to bead from the short cut. More than he expected.

Anca pressed folded cloth against it. "Hold this."

Coen obeyed. His face felt cold, and the trees tilted when he looked at them.

"You are pale," she said.

"I feel pale."

"Lie back."

He did.

Anca raised his arm and waited until the bleeding slowed.

"The hex worked," Coen said.

"It did. You lost control before you reached the stump."

"I couldn't stop."

"I saw. Next time, ten paces."

Coen closed his eyes until the spinning stopped. His arm hurt, his shoulder would bruise, and he had used more blood than Anca intended.

He had still crossed thirty paces in a few heartbeats. On the next attempt, he meant to remain on his feet.
