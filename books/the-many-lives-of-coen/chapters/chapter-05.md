## Chapter Text

By the next harvest, Pieter decided Coen was old enough for the mine.

He said it after supper, while Esme was still cutting onions into the pot. Alin had been arguing about who had carried more wood. Mirto was under the table again. Lunka sat on the bench with her heels hooked on the rung, waiting for bread.

"Tomorrow you come with me," Pieter said.

Coen had known the words were coming. Knowing did not make them easier.

"I can still help Anca."

"On rest days. The mine pays. Reading does not."

Alin looked up at once. "What about me?"

"You take the wood and the garden. When I say you are ready, you will know."

Esme did not argue. She wiped her hands and packed a second cloth of bread for the morning. "Eat before the climb. The air down there takes it out of you."

Yanna snorted. "He already eats enough for two."

"Then he can work for two," Pieter said.

Coen slept badly. Not because the mine was a surprise. Because once he went down there, he would not be a child the village could overlook.

In the morning Esme checked the wrap on his left forearm before he pulled his sleeve down. She thought it was for an old scrape. Coen let her think that.

"Keep it covered," she said. "Rock dust gets into everything."

"I will."

Alin followed them as far as the yard. "If you find silver, bring me some."

"It does not come out in pieces you can put in a pocket."

"Then bring me a rock."

"Go inside," Pieter said.

Alin went, but he watched them down the path until the houses hid him.

---

The mine sat above Laslea, a dark mouth cut into the hill. Men were already there with lamps, ropes, and tools worn smooth by other hands. The air smelled of wet stone and old sweat.

Pieter handed Coen a cloth. "Tie that over your mouth when the dust is bad. You will still taste it."

He did.

The first day was hauling. Coen carried broken rock to the carts until his shoulders burned. The wrap under his sleeve rubbed raw. He checked it whenever he had a moment alone and pushed the cloth back down before anyone could ask.

Pieter watched him work without much talk. When Coen swung too wide, his father caught the handle.

"Short. You are not chopping wood. Hit the rock in front of you, not your own legs."

By the fourth day Coen stopped asking when they would go back down. They went. They came up grey. Esme made him wash in the yard before he entered the house.

The dust did not come off all at once. It clung to his hair and the lines of his palms. After a month, his spit had a metal taste that water did not take away.

He still went to Anca on rest days.

She made him sit by the window and unroll the wrap. The scars had settled into dark lines. Mine work had not opened them, but the skin around them stayed angry where the cloth rubbed.

"You are keeping it covered," Anca said.

"Esme thinks it is an old cut."

"Let her. If a priest sees these, reading will not save you."

She did not ask whether he regretted the mine. She already knew the answer he would give in public. Boys from Laslea went below. Wanting a different life had never been enough.

When the arm looked clean enough, they walked beyond the village, and she let him use Mercurial Fervour again. Ten paces. Then twenty. He learned to stop before the hex spent more blood than he could spare.

"Better," Anca said, after he halted on his feet instead of his shoulder. "Do not use it where men can see you run."

"I know."

"Knowing and doing are different. You will be tired from the mine. Tired men get careless."

He was tired. He went anyway.

Years passed that way.

Coen's hands thickened. His back learned the bend of low tunnels. The metal taste stayed. A faint grey gathered at the roots of his nails and in the skin beside them, too light for a stranger to name and too stubborn for soap.

Alin grew tall enough to wrestle him without looking like a child doing it. He still lost more often than he liked. Yanna started speaking to other girls as if the rest of the house could not hear. Mirto stopped hiding under the table. Lunka learned to fetch water without spilling half of it.

Esme stayed on her feet. She scolded, cooked, and pulled Lunka's hair into order every morning. The river had frightened her, but it had not emptied her.

Pieter started taking Alin up to the mine entrance on short days, then sending him home with the tools. "Not yet," he said, whenever Alin asked. "You still bounce when you walk."

Coen did not tell them that the work was doing what he had feared. It was also doing what he needed. His body was no longer a boy's. The hex no longer threw him on his back. He could keep a secret in a place where men stood close enough to smell the blood on his wrap if he was stupid.

He was not strong enough for Brencis. He knew that too. The remembered future had never given him a year written on a wall. Only the shape of what was coming: plague, the castle, the night his family would be taken if he stayed as weak as he had been beside the river.

So he hauled rock, hid his arm, and learned to stop.

---

The first talk of fever came with a cart from down the valley.

The driver wanted salt and nails. He paid with a story instead of extra coin. People were sick in the lower village. A woman had died after three days of heat. The priest there had closed two houses.

Esme sent Coen to Anca with dried mint and a question. Anca was already boiling water. Her table was covered in cloths.

"It is not only the lower village," she said. "I had two children here this morning with the same heat."

"Will herbs stop it?"

"They help some. They do not stop a thing that has already decided to move."

Coen stood in the doorway longer than he should have. In the other life, plague had come before the vampires finished taking Vale Sangora. He could not say that. He could not even say he had been waiting for it.

"What should we do at home?"

"Keep Lunka and Mirto away from sick houses. Wash your hands. If someone in your family burns with fever, you come to me before you listen to anyone selling charms."

"I will."

She looked at his sleeve. "And you do not bleed for magic because you are frightened. Frightened work is sloppy work."

He nodded and went home.

That week Laslea spoke of fever the way it had once spoken of early frost. Carefully, and then all at once. A neighbor closed his shutters. The priest told people to pray and stay out of one another's rooms. Esme boiled extra water and kept the little ones inside unless they were fetching for her.

Alin hated the indoor days.

"I am not sick."

"You will stay in until I say," Esme said.

She said it while kneading dough, not from bed. Coen watched her and held onto that. The river had not taken Alin. His mother was still herself. The rest of the remembered damage had not arrived yet.

It was close enough to taste, like silver.

Two days later, riders came up from the castle road.

They did not enter every house. They stopped in the square, drank, and told the village headman that Greifberg had a new master. The mine would send more silver before winter. Anyone who argued could explain it to the men who would come back with lists.

Pieter heard it at the mine before Coen did. He repeated it at supper without raising his voice.

"A lord named Brencis," he said. "That is what they told Martin."

Coen kept his eyes on his bowl.

The name fit the space he had been keeping empty for years. He had hoped, stupidly, that changing Alin's death might have changed this too. It had not.

"What kind of lord?" Yanna asked.

"The kind who wants more silver," Pieter said. "Eat. We will do the work. We always do the work."

Esme set a hand on Lunka's hair, then on Mirto's, counting them the way she did when she was thinking about winter stores. "If they take more from the mine, they will still need people to dig it. That is something."

Alin made a face. "They could need me."

"They could need you to sit down," Pieter said.

Lunka laughed. She was well. Her spoon tapped the bowl, and bread crumbs stuck to her chin. Coen reached over and brushed them off because that was ordinary, and ordinary still belonged to them.

After the meal he went out to the yard and sat on the chopping block until the light went.

He could not tell them why the name mattered. He could not tell them that plague and this lord arrived together in the story he remembered, or that waiting until the castle finished taking people would be too late.

He could try to move them. He could invent a reason a miner might give: work elsewhere, a cousin, a winter away from sick houses. He would need more than a feeling they could not see.

Behind him the door opened. Alin came out with a leftover heel of bread and sat on the ground.

"You look like you swallowed a rock."

"I am tired."

"You are always tired now."

Coen tore the bread and gave him half. "Do not go near the closed houses."

"Mama already said."

"Then listen to her."

Alin chewed. "If the new lord pays better, maybe Father will let me come down with you."

"That is not what this is."

"You don't know."

Coen almost answered. He stopped himself and looked back at the house instead. Smoke rose from the chimney. Esme's voice reached the yard, telling Mirto to leave the fire alone. Lunka answered, bright and annoyed, still healthy.

The mine had taken his childhood. The fever was already in the valley. Brencis had a name in Laslea now.

Coen wiped the grey dust from his nails, failed, and went inside before the dark made him sit there too long.
