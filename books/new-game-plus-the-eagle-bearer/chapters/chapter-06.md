# Chapter 6: A Fate Worse Than Death

They gave her the general's tent at dusk, and they gave it to her alone.

That was the arrangement. Stentor had said it flatly, the way he said everything, that the Wolf would see the misthios and no one else, and Kassandra had agreed before Theron could decide whether to argue. So he stood outside in the blue evening with two Spartan guards who did not like him, and he listened to the low voices through the canvas, and he did the hardest work of his life, which was to stay where he was and let it happen.

Inside, Nikolaos looked older than the stories.

Kassandra had built him in her memory as a giant, the way children build the people who hurt them, all shoulders and shadow and a hand that opened over a cliff. The man by the brazier was tall and gray and tired, a soldier who had spent thirty years being hard because Sparta asked it and had forgotten, somewhere in the asking, how to be anything else. He looked at her for a long time before he spoke, and when he did his voice was rough.

"They told me the misthios with the broken spear had come for my head," he said. "They did not tell me she had my daughter's face."

"I am not your daughter." The words came out of Kassandra flat and fast, rehearsed across three decades. "You made sure of that. On a mountain. When I was seven."

Nikolaos closed his eyes. "Kassandra."

"You held my hand." She was very still, the way she went still before violence, and Theron, outside, heard the stillness in her voice and pressed his palm flat against the tent pole and did not move. "You held my hand, pater, and then you opened it, and you let me fall. Do you know what it is to be a child, and to look up, and to see your father's face while the ground comes up to meet you? I do. I have looked at it every night for thirty years."

"Every night," Nikolaos said. "For me as well."

"Do not."

"You want me to deny it. I cannot." He did not step toward her. He seemed to understand that if he stepped toward her she would kill him, and that he might deserve it. "The ephors decreed it. The oracle at Delphi named your brother a curse on Sparta, and named you a traitor for trying to save him, and the law of Sparta gave me a choice between my children and my city, and I was a coward, and I chose the law. I told myself it was duty. It was fear. I have had thirty years to learn the difference."

Kassandra's hand was on the spear. Theron knew the shape of that grip. He had seen it end men.

"Give me one reason," she said.

"I will not give you one." Nikolaos opened his hands, empty, at his sides. "You came here to kill the Wolf of Sparta. He is standing in front of you. If your knife is the price of what I did, then take it, and I will not raise a hand, because there is no honor left for me to protect. I spent it all on that mountain."

And this was the moment. Theron, outside, with his eyes shut, knew it to the breath. This was the hinge the whole story turned on, and he had spent six years making sure he would not be the one to push it. He had brought her here. He had kept her alive to reach this tent. He had done everything except the one thing that mattered, which was hers, which had always been hers.

He heard her draw a breath. He heard the spear shift.

"No," Kassandra said.

The word came out cracked and furious and grieving all at once. "No. You do not get to make it easy. You do not get to kneel down and hand me your throat and call it justice and be finished. You threw a child off a mountain and you have slept every night since in a warm tent with a war to keep you busy. Death is not the fate you have earned." Her voice broke and held. "Live. Live, and carry every one of those nights. Look for the ghosts of what you did in every face you pass. That is worse than any knife. That is what you deserve, pater. That is what I am giving you."

There was a long silence. Theron realized his own eyes were wet, and let them be, out there in the dark where no one was keeping score of him.

"You are more than Sparta ever deserved," Nikolaos said at last, quietly. "You always were." A pause, and then, heavier: "There is a thing you must know, and it will not be a kindness. I am not your father. Not by blood. Your mother carried you before she came to me. I raised you as my own because I loved her, and because you were worth loving, and none of that gives me any right to the word. But it is the truth, and you have earned the truth if nothing else."

Outside, Theron heard the small terrible sound of a person absorbing a thing that rearranges the floor under them.

"Then who," Kassandra said.

"That is your mother's to tell. Myrrine lives. I do not know where, only that she is beyond the reach of the men who did this to us, and that if anyone can find her, it is a woman who walked out of the sea and made herself into a legend." A rustle, the old man moving. "Find her, Kassandra. Ask her the questions you cannot ask me. And when you find your brother, because I think you will, tell him what I could not. That I am sorry. That it was never his curse. It was my cowardice, wearing a prophecy for a mask."

When Kassandra came out of the tent, the guards stepped back from her without being told, because there are some expressions that clear a path on their own. Theron pushed off the tent pole and fell into step beside her, saying nothing, because six years had taught him when nothing was the only honest thing to say.

They walked out past the fires to the dark edge of the camp before she stopped. She stood with her back to him, looking at nothing, the broken spear loose in her hand.

"He's alive," she said. Testing it. "I let him live."

"You did."

"I don't know if it was mercy or cruelty."

"It was both," Theron said. "That's why it was right."

She turned then, and looked at him, and whatever storm was in her had not passed, but it had a shape now, a direction. "My mother is alive. My father is a stranger. My brother is a weapon somewhere in the dark, and a dead man on a mountain was neither of those things." She laughed, once, wet and disbelieving. "By the gods, Theron. What kind of story am I in?"

He looked at her in the low firelight, this girl he had crossed two lives to keep, standing at the start of the long road back to everyone she had lost, not one of them as gone as she had believed.

"The kind where you get them back," he said. "One at a time. If you're stubborn enough."

"I am very stubborn."

"I know," he said. "It's the best thing about you."

She snorted, and scrubbed a hand across her face, and for a moment she let herself lean, just slightly, her shoulder against his, the way she had on the ridge above Sami a lifetime ago. Then she straightened, and the misthios came back into her spine, and she looked out toward the sea and the dark islands and the mother waiting somewhere past them.

"Then we find Myrrine," she said. "Wake Barnabas. We sail at first light."
