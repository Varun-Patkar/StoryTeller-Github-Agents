# Chapter 3: The Man With One Eye

Stealing from a man who calls himself the Cyclops turned out to be easier than stealing from Markos, and Markos was on their side.

The estate sat above the water on the north end of the island, all whitewashed walls and hired muscle and a gate that its owner clearly believed made a statement. Theron studied it from the treeline in the last of the light and found it saying exactly what he remembered it saying, which was that a frightened man had spent a great deal of another man's money trying to look unafraid.

"Two on the gate," Kassandra said beside him, low. "Four in the yard. More inside."

"Six in the yard by the time the moon's up. They rotate." He had watched this from a couch, once, at three in the morning, eating cold noodles. He did not say that part. "The eye's in the main room, on a little shrine he built for it, because of course he did. We don't fight our way in. We wait for the watch to change, and there's a gap, one man's walk, on the seaward wall. We go over there."

She gave him the look, the one that meant she was deciding whether to ask how he knew and had concluded, again, that she would rather not. It was a good look. He had built his whole life on the other side of it.

They went over the seaward wall in the gap between one bored guard and the next, and Theron's hands did not shake, because a boy who had climbed every cliff on Kephallonia for six years had the fingers for it now, and because he had bought a small pot of pine pitch that morning and worked it into his palms so the old rope would not slip. That was the shape of most of his cleverness. Not the leap. The pitch.

The eye sat where he knew it would, black and polished and faintly ridiculous, on a shrine lit by a single lamp. Kassandra lifted it, weighed it in her palm, and looked at it with the flat contempt of a woman who had never once needed a stone to feel like more than she was.

"He worships this," she said.

"He lost a fight and needed to be a god about it. Men do that." Theron held out the small pouch he had brought for exactly this. "Come on. The longer we admire it, the more likely someone admires us."

They were over the wall and into the trees before the lamp in the shrine even guttered.

The trouble, as it always did, came the next day, and it came the way Theron had known it would, which did not make it easier to watch.

Markos had a plan, and his plan was Theron's plan wearing a wider grin, and Theron let him have it. But before they could send word to the Cyclops that his precious eye was for sale, the man moved first. He was not, in the end, a patient god. Word came up from Kleptous Bay that the Cyclops had come back to the island himself, in a stolen ship, and that he had a man down there with him, and that the man was screaming.

Kassandra was already reaching for her spear.

"You know who it is," Theron said. It was not quite a question. He did know, and he had learned that the fastest way to steer her was to arrive a half-step ahead and let her think they had gotten there together.

"I know it's a trap," she said. "He wants me to come."

"He does. He also wants to feel like the one holding the knife, which means he'll talk before he uses it, which means we have time, which means we do this the smart way and not the way where you charge down a beach again." He was already picking their route in his head, the rocks, the boats, the angle of the sun that would be in the guards' eyes at that hour. "He's not a fighter. He's a bully with money and one working eye. Take the eye's men off him and he folds."

She looked at him a moment longer than usual. "You talk like a man twice your age."

"I feel like one," he said, honestly, and she snorted and let it go.

Kleptous Bay was a scatter of wrecked boats and bad smells, and the Cyclops was exactly where the screaming said he would be, a big soft man gone loud with fear, standing over another man lashed to a mast with a knife too close to his remaining eye. The prisoner was older, weathered, one socket already long empty, and even trussed and bleeding he was talking, a low steady stream of curses and philosophy that suggested a man who had decided fear was simply beneath him.

"You want your eye," the Cyclops was shouting at the water, at the island, at no one. "You want it? Come and get it, misthios, come and see what I do to people who steal from me."

"Behind you," Kassandra said, pleasantly, and then it was quick.

Theron took the two men on the left flank the only way he ever took anyone, which was to already be standing where they needed to fall. He put a boat's beached hull between himself and the biggest of them, let the man commit to the swing, and used the rope, always the rope, to turn a charge into a sprawl. Kassandra did the rest, the way a storm does the rest. The Cyclops discovered, in the space of about a minute, that being a god of one small island did not carry over to a beach where the Eagle Bearer had decided to be annoyed.

By the end he was on his knees in the surf, weeping about his eye.

Kassandra held it up where he could see it, this black stone he had built a shrine to, and considered him for a long moment. Then she tossed it to a goat that had wandered down to watch, and the goat, being a goat, ate it, and the Cyclops made a sound Theron would remember fondly for two thousand years.

They cut the prisoner loose. He rolled his shoulders, felt his one good eye with two fingers to be sure it was still there, and grinned at them through the blood like a man greeting old friends.

"Barnabas," he said, as if that explained everything. "Captain of the Adrestia, which that pig stole from me, which I would very much like back. You have the look of people going somewhere. I have a ship and a debt to repay. The gods clearly arranged this. They do that."

Theron looked at the old sailor, and at the trireme riding at anchor past the wrecks, and felt the board tilt under his feet the way it always did when the story took its next breath. The Adrestia. The sea road off this island and onto the mainland, toward a man writing a letter, toward a general called the Wolf, toward all of it.

"We're going to need a ship," he said.

"You are going to need many things," Barnabas said cheerfully, hauling himself upright. "A ship is a good start. Come. Let me show you the finest vessel in the Greek world, and I will tell you how the gods spoke to me about your arrival. It is a long story. It has a storm in it."

Kassandra caught Theron's eye over the old man's head, one eyebrow raised, half a laugh in it. Theron just shrugged. Some things you could not have planned for even with two lifetimes of reading ahead, and a talking sea captain who thanked the gods for his own kidnapping was, he decided, one of the better ones.

They followed Barnabas down to the water, and the island that had raised them fell quiet behind, and somewhere across the strait the real game was already dealing its first cards.
