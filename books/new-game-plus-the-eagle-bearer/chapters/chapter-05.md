# Chapter 5: Earning the Wolf

Megaris was on fire in the slow way a country burns when two larger countries have decided to fight their war on top of it.

The Adrestia came in past an Athenian blockade at dawn, Barnabas threading the ship through a gap in the patrol boats with the calm of a man who had done more dangerous things for worse reasons. By the time the Athenians understood there was a Spartan-flagged trireme in their water, it was ashore and empty and the people who had been on it were already walking inland through a landscape of burned olive groves and nervous soldiers.

The soldiers belonged to Sparta, and the man who commanded them found Theron and Kassandra before they had gone half a mile.

"You're the misthios," he said. Not a question. He was young, hard-faced, armored like a man who slept in it, and he looked at Kassandra with the particular contempt Spartans kept for people who fought for coin. "The one with the broken spear. We've heard. Stentor." He offered the name like a challenge rather than an introduction.

Theron watched Kassandra's face do nothing at all, which was how he knew it had done something. Stentor. The adopted son. The other child Nikolaos had raised, the one who had stayed, the one who did not know he was standing three feet from the sister he had never met. Theron filed the ache of it away and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would not be a lie or a landmine.

"I'm looking for your general," Kassandra said.

"Everyone is looking for the general. The general is busy winning a war." Stentor's mouth thinned. "If you want to reach him, you earn it. There are Athenians in these hills making my life difficult. Make them stop, and maybe I let you breathe the same air as Nikolaos. Maybe."

The purse from Elpenor was still heavy at Kassandra's hip. She did not touch it. "Fine," she said.

So they earned it.

It took the better part of two months, and Theron spent those months being useful in the quiet ways he had made his specialty. He was not the strongest soldier in any Spartan camp, and he made sure no one ever thought he was trying to be. What he was, was the person who noticed things. He noticed that the Spartans kept losing supply trains they should not have been losing, and that the losses were blamed on Athenian raiders who left no tracks, which was strange, because Athenian raiders were many things but they were not ghosts.

"Grain doesn't vanish," he told Kassandra one night, the two of them on watch at the edge of a camp that smelled of cold iron and colder men. "It gets eaten, or it gets sold. If the Athenians took it, we'd find it in an Athenian belly or an Athenian market. We haven't. So it's still here, and someone with a Spartan face is selling it and calling the empty carts a raid."

Kassandra looked at him sidelong in the dark. "You are certain."

"I'm certain grain doesn't vanish."

It was Hyrkanos, in the end, a rival mercenary who had wormed his way into Spartan trust and was bleeding the army from the inside for coin. Theron did not expose him with a grand accusation. He simply followed the logic to a warehouse, and let Kassandra walk into it, and let the evidence be the kind that even a proud Spartan officer could not argue with. Stentor's face when he understood how close the rot had come was worth watching, in a grim way. It was the first time the young man looked at Kassandra as something other than a hired sword.

"You could have taken the coin and stayed quiet," Stentor said. "Sold it back to us like he did. Most would have."

"I'm not most," Kassandra said.

"No." He studied her a moment longer, as if a thought had brushed him and moved on before he could catch it. "No, you're not."

Theron watched the two of them stand there, the sister and the brother, neither one knowing, and thought about how much of loving people was standing very still and letting the slow machine of the truth do its own work at its own pace. He could have told them. He could tell them a hundred things. Every one of them would cost more than it bought.

Later, when the camp was asleep and the fires were low, Kassandra found him where he sat with his back against a supply cart, awake, because he was often awake.

"You knew about Hyrkanos before we found the warehouse," she said. She said it plainly, the way she said everything, laying it on the ground between them to see what he did with it. "You always know. You knew about the goat path. You knew to hurry to Phoibe. You know things a boy raised by Markos has no way to know."

Here it was. Theron kept his breathing even and his face open and let the pause be exactly as long as an honest one would be.

"I pay attention," he said. "That's all it is. I watch, and I remember, and I put things next to other things until they make a shape. You do it too, with a blade. I do it with everything else." He shrugged, and let the shrug be tired rather than careful. "Somebody in this pair has to be the one who thinks three steps ahead. You're too busy being the one who wins the fight."

She was quiet for a long moment. He could not tell, in the dark, whether she believed him or had simply decided that believing him was easier than the alternative. With Kassandra those two things had always lived close together, and he had built six years on the narrow ground between them.

"Three steps ahead," she said at last. "Then tell me, Theron. This man. My father." Her voice did not change, which was how he knew what it cost. "When I stand in front of him, what am I going to do?"

And that, at least, he could answer without a single lie, because it was the one thing he had refused to decide for her.

"I don't know," he said. "That's yours. I'll be standing next to you either way."

She looked at him. Then she nodded, once, and sat down against the cart beside him, close, her shoulder against his, and neither of them said anything else. In the morning a runner came from Stentor. The Athenians were broken, the general was pleased, and Kassandra of the broken spear had earned what she came for.

The Wolf of Sparta would see her.
