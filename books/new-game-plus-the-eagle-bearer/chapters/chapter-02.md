# Chapter 2: The Obsidian Eye

The vineyard was real. That was the first surprise.

Markos stood in the middle of it with his arms spread like a man presenting the sea itself, beaming, sunburned, entirely pleased with a purchase he could not possibly afford. Rows of young vines ran up the slope behind him in tidy green lines. Somewhere a cicada was making the case for summer at the top of its voice.

"You see?" he said. "You doubted me. Both of you doubted me. And now look. Land. Actual land, with my name on it, or close to my name, close enough."

"Whose money," Kassandra said.

"That is a small detail."

"Whose money, Markos."

He deflated by a careful inch, the way he always did when the truth had cornered him and he was negotiating terms of surrender. "The Cyclops lent it to me. A short arrangement. A friendly one."

Theron already knew this, had known it walking up the hill, but he let his face do the same slow drop that Kassandra's did, because a boy who was never surprised was a boy people started to ask questions about. It was a small discipline, and he practiced it constantly. Look surprised. Look slow. Be the one who happened to guess right, never the one who already knew.

"There is nothing friendly about the Cyclops," Kassandra said.

"Which is why I have a plan." Markos brightened again, indestructible. "But first, a horse. I promised you a horse. Go to Sami, collect what Duris owes me, and the horse is yours. His name is Phobos. He is a magnificent animal and only bites sometimes."

Kassandra looked at Theron. Theron looked at the vines. In his head the shop sat open where he had left it that morning, and against the small dark line of his savings he had already bought two things before breakfast: a coil of good rope and a little clay pot of numbing salve, the kind you pressed to a cut before you stitched it. He had not known exactly why. He rarely let himself think the why all the way through, because the why was usually a name and a date, and some mornings it was easier to just buy the rope and trust that the boy he used to be had read the book carefully.

Today the name was Phoibe. The date was today.

"We should hurry," he said.

Kassandra raised an eyebrow at him. "It is a debt in Sami. It will keep."

"I know." He was already moving. "I've just got a feeling."

She did not argue with his feelings anymore. That was the other discipline, the long one, six years of being right often enough and quietly enough that a Spartan girl who trusted almost nothing had decided, without ever saying so, to trust his feelings. She fell in beside him. Markos shouted something about the horse and the goodness of his own heart, and the vines swallowed the sound behind them.

They did not make it to Sami.

A boy from the harbor caught them on the road, red-faced and gulping, and the words came out in the wrong order but the shape of them was one Theron had been carrying for six years. Thugs. The Cyclops. A girl. They had taken Phoibe down to Ktimene Beach.

Kassandra went very still, and then she went very fast, and Theron had to catch her arm to keep her from simply running the three miles in a straight line over the cliffs.

"Wait," he said.

"Let go."

"Kassandra. If we come over that ridge shouting, they put a knife to her before we're halfway down. You know this. You taught me this." He kept his voice flat and level, the way you talk to a horse that only bites sometimes. "There's a goat path along the east side. Comes out behind them, above the tideline. We go quiet, we come from the dark side of the beach, and they never see us until it's already done."

She stared at him. He watched her want to argue and find nothing to argue with, because he was right, and he was right because he had walked this exact beach in another life with a controller in his hands and had watched a version of this go the slow careful way and the loud stupid way both.

"Since when do you know goat paths on the east side," she said.

"Since I get bored," he said, which was true, and which was also the answer to a great many questions about him, and which she accepted because it was easier than the alternative.

The path was where he remembered it. They went down it low and fast and without a word between them, and the salve and the rope rode quiet against his hip, and below them on the pale evening sand three of the Cyclops's men stood around a small furious shape that would not stop talking even with a blade near her.

"You are all going to die," Phoibe was informing them, with total confidence. "She is going to come, and she is going to be so angry, and you are going to die."

Theron touched Kassandra's wrist once, the old signal, and pointed. She smiled at him then, brief and sharp and not at all kind, and that smile was aimed past him at three men who had made the worst mistake available on the island.

It was over quickly, and Theron did his part of it the way he always did, which was to be exactly where the third man ran and to put a foot in his path and the coil of rope around his ankles so that Kassandra only had to deal with two. He did not need to be the strongest person on any beach. He needed to be the person who had already decided where everyone would be standing.

Phoibe hit him at the waist hard enough to knock the breath out of him and did not let go.

"You took forever," she said into his shirt.

"Sorry." He put a hand on her hair. His chest did the warm and the cold thing, and for once the cold was smaller. Today the date had come and gone and she was here, gripping his ribs and complaining, and the book had said otherwise and the book had been wrong, because he had bought the rope. "We came the quiet way."

"Kassandra came the loud way."

"Kassandra always comes the loud way."

Above them, at the tideline, Kassandra crouched over the man Theron had tripped and asked him three short questions in a voice that got answers. When she came back her jaw was set.

"The Cyclops sent them," she said. "He's hired a mercenary to kill me. Talos, they called him. The Stone Fist." She said it the way she said all bad news, plainly, filing it. "And Markos owes that one-eyed pig his whole vineyard."

"Then we take the thing he loves most," Theron said, "and we make him trade the debt for it."

She looked at him.

"He has an eye," Theron went on. "Not the missing one. A stone one, obsidian, black as anything, worth more than the vineyard and the horse and all of us put together. He keeps it because it makes him feel like a god instead of a fat man who lost a fight. If we take it, we don't have to beat him. He'll come to us."

It was a good plan. It was, in fact, more or less the plan Markos would propose to them tomorrow at the Temple of Zeus, sweating and delighted with his own cleverness. Theron had simply had it first, and had learned the trick of handing his ideas to other people an hour before they thought of them, so that the island stayed a place where a merchant's stray had lucky instincts and nothing more.

Kassandra was quiet a moment. Then she reached out and cuffed him lightly on the back of the head, which from her was the same as a blessing.

"You are a strange one, little brother," she said. "Come on. Phoibe rides. You and I walk. And if that horse bites me, I am selling Markos one grape at a time until he dies of it."

They went up off the darkening beach together, the girl he had saved on his back now and half asleep already, the girl he had followed across two lives walking at his side. Behind them the tide came in and took the marks of what had happened, the way it always did.

Tomorrow there would be a one-eyed man, and a stolen eye, and the first real move on a board only Theron could see whole. Tonight there was only the walk home, and the weight of a sleeping child, and the sea going quietly about the business of forgetting.
