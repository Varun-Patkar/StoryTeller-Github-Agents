# Chapter 1: The House of Markos

Sami was not a town that rewarded standing still. The port sat on the eastern face of Kephallonia, a crescent of harbor with white houses climbing the hill behind it and the Ionian Sea in front, and ships docked three days in four when the weather held. It was busy enough for a merchant to make a living and small enough that everyone kept track of who was making how much and why. Markos made his living selling rope, oil, and cloth to sailors who forgot half of what they needed before they left port. He also made his living through deals that were going to pay off, any day now, once the right person arrived or the right harvest came in or some debtor in the hills finally answered his messages.

Theron had been sleeping in the storeroom for five months. He had figured out by month two that "any day now" was not Markos's strategy. It was Markos.

He sat in the yard with a pile of sea-stiffened rope and worked it loose by hand, the way Markos had shown him the first week. The morning sun came over the wall at a low angle and the harbor smells came with it, brine and old fish and woodsmoke from the cookfires down the hill. He looked at nothing in particular and opened his STATUS.

`[ STATUS — Theron, Age 9 ]`
`STR 6    AGI 7    END 5`
`INT 13   PER 9    CHA 6`
`WIL 7    MEM 16`
`Trait, Enhanced Talent: active.`
`Balance: 3 drachmae, 5 obols.`

The physical stats had moved. Not by much, but Strength and Agility had each picked up a point from five months of carrying, running, and climbing around the hillside town. Perception had gone up from the discipline of paying close attention to a port full of people he needed to understand as fast as possible. MEM 16 meant everything he learned stuck without effort, which was the only reason a nine-year-old who had arrived speaking no Greek could have been functional within a month of landing. He had listened to the harbor, to Markos, to every conversation within earshot, and the memory stat had built the language around him like a scaffold going up overnight.

He closed the screen and went back to the rope.

"You're staring again."

Kassandra dropped off the yard wall and landed without noise. She had been walking its length since before Theron woke up, arms out, the full span of it, back and forth in the early grey. She was twelve and had been on Kephallonia for two years before Theron arrived, and she used the morning hours the way some people use a whetstone: not dramatically, just steadily, working on an edge she did not name out loud.

She picked up the far end of the rope and started working it beside him. She did not look at him. "Markos thinks you're simple. Goes quiet when you do that."

Theron said, "What did you tell him."

"That simple people don't count things." She pulled a knot loose with a sharp tug. "You count the oil jars every morning. And the harbor berths. And how much everyone owes Markos versus what Markos thinks they owe."

He thought about whether to say anything useful. "The Argos account is wrong. He thinks it's forty-two drachmae. It's closer to sixty."

Kassandra paused.

"He miscounted a spring entry," Theron said, "and used the wrong number for two entries after it. Sixty-one if you include the late fee he forgot to add."

She looked at him for a moment, then went back to the rope. "He's going to find out."

"He is. The question is when."

The Argos account was not the most pressing problem in the house. Theron knew that too. He had known the broad shape of the situation before he arrived, in the same way he knew the island's geography and the names of the people in it, from several hundred hours of a game he no longer had a controller for. The debts Markos had accumulated were real and specific and would eventually require someone physically capable and tactically functional to resolve. That was what Kassandra was training for, walking the wall at dawn every morning. She did not say so. She did not need to.

He was five months into building toward the same destination, slower, by a different path.

---

Markos came out of the house with the expression he wore when he had thought of something and needed someone else to do it.

"Kassandra. Theron." He settled himself onto the bench by the wall and folded his hands together the way he did before presenting something as an opportunity. "I have a small matter. Two small matters, actually. The first: Dorokles, a farmer near Kefalos on the south road, owes me twelve drachmae from the spring oil shipment. He has been quiet on the subject for two months. The second: Adrastos, at the harbor near the net-menders' row, owes me twenty drachmae from winter rope and has been quiet for longer." He paused. "I would go myself but I am in the middle of a very delicate negotiation with a Corinthian buyer."

"The Corinthian buyer isn't here yet," Kassandra said.

"He will be. Possibly tomorrow." Markos waved this aside. "If you bring back the money from Dorokles, six obols each. Bring back the Adrastos money as well, two drachmae each on top."

Theron did the sum. Six obols from Dorokles, two drachmae from Adrastos. One drachma and six obols more in his balance if both jobs paid out. He closed his eyes briefly and ran it.

`[ Projected balance: 3 dr 5 ob + 6 ob + 2 dr = 6 dr 5 ob — both jobs close. ]`

Close enough to the iron knife to matter. He had been looking at that knife for six weeks.

"Fine," Kassandra said.

---

The road south out of Sami followed the island's ridge before dropping through olive groves toward the coast. Kefalos was half a day's walk, and Kassandra set the pace like she was trying to end the walk without the running. Theron kept up. His Agility was at seven, which felt like an ordinary fit child's capability, and the hills had been working on him.

She had a staff she had made herself from a pine branch, the bark stripped, the knots smoothed down. She kept it in her right hand and used it for stepping over rocks, nothing theatrical about it. Theron had no staff. He added it to the mental list of things to fix.

"What's the approach," she said, when the town was behind the first ridge.

"Dorokles. He's a farmer, missed harvest, probably couldn't pay rather than wouldn't. Offer him eight now and four at the winter festival. Tell him Markos arranged it this way, prefers the relationship over a lump payment."

Kassandra did not look at him. "Markos didn't arrange anything."

"Markos will take eight drachmae in his hand today over twelve on a ledger he's been staring at for two months. The framing is accurate." He watched the road. "The arrangement gives Dorokles a story he can tell himself about how this ended well for him."

"And if he says no."

"Then we find out what he can actually pay and build around that. But he won't say no."

She moved the staff to her left hand without breaking stride. "You think people need stories."

"Always. Especially when they owe money."

The road curved around the face of a limestone outcrop, and the sea appeared below them, flat and glittering in the late morning sun. Kassandra went up the outcrop to the right, three quick moves, and stood at the top for a moment, scanning the road ahead and the tree line beyond it. She had not said she was going to do it. It was just the obvious thing to do and she had done it.

Theron watched the road while she did, tracking a pair of goat-herders heading north and a cart making slow progress toward Sami with amphorae tied badly to its back. Neither was interesting.

She came back down and they walked.

`[ PER +1 ]`

The ping was quiet, one line, addressed to no one but him. He had been maintaining the habit of actually watching the road instead of only thinking about the day ahead, and something in the accumulated attention had tipped over into a measurable point. He dismissed it and kept walking.

---

Dorokles was a large man with the specific patience of someone who has learned that waiting costs less than explaining. He came out of his shed at the sound of the gate and looked at them with no readable expression.

"Markos's wards," he said. Not a question. He had been expecting this at some point.

"He asked us to carry a message," Kassandra said. Her voice was flat, not aggressive, just the voice of someone who has nowhere particular to be and no reason to be nervous about this conversation. She stood with the staff at her side, not displayed, not hidden. "About the spring balance. Twelve drachmae."

"I told him end of summer."

"End of summer was two months back."

Dorokles looked at his goats. The goats gave him no help.

"Bad harvest," he said. "Everyone on the south side."

"Markos knows," Theron said. "He said he'd prefer eight now and four at the winter festival. Makes his accounts easier to track. His words were that he values the working relationship."

Dorokles turned and looked at Theron properly for the first time. People did this. They looked at Kassandra because she was taller and older and carried the staff, and then they realized Theron had said something specific and they had to recalibrate.

"Eight now," the farmer said.

"And four at the winter festival. That's the arrangement."

The silence ran for several seconds. Theron watched a cloud move behind the hills to the north and waited. Kassandra waited. One of the goats sat down.

"Fine," said Dorokles.

He came back with eight drachmae in a cloth bag tied with a strip of leather. He handed it to Kassandra, which was the sensible read of the situation. She took it and gave him a nod that closed the matter. They were on the road back to Sami before the farm cleared the treeline.

"He'll pay the four," Kassandra said.

"He will."

"You were sure before he agreed."

Theron thought about how to say it without it sounding like a lecture. "He called us Markos's wards. He knew who we were before we arrived. Men who have decided they're not paying don't bother remembering who owes what to whom. He remembered because part of him was always going to pay. He just needed the shape of it to change."

Kassandra turned the cloth bag over in her hand once. "The shape."

"Twelve drachmae owed is a problem he's been carrying. Eight drachmae paid on a structure he agreed to is a negotiation he won." He paused. "He didn't win. But he can feel like he did."

She was quiet for a moment. The road climbed back toward the ridge and the sea dropped out of view.

"That's a useful thing to know," she said.

---

The net-menders' row ran along the south end of Sami harbor, the working end, away from where traders and captains docked. The smell was fish and tar and wet rope left in the afternoon sun, and the men who worked there were the kind who did physical labor for enough years that it left marks.

Adrastos was at the far end. He was older than Dorokles, broader, with the quality of a man who had resolved most arguments in his life by being the largest presence in the room. He was mending a net when they found him and did not look up.

"Not interested," he said.

"We haven't said anything yet," Kassandra said.

He looked up. He looked at her, then at Theron, then back at her. He had expected children and was revising the estimate.

"Twenty drachmae," Theron said. "Rope. December through March."

Adrastos put the net down slowly. "Markos knows where I live."

"He does," Theron said. "He also has the purchase records. If you want, we can go over the figures."

He did not have the records with him. He was making a specific bet: Adrastos did not know whether he had them or not, and men who have been avoiding a debt for months are usually less certain about the exact figures than they project.

"I don't owe twenty," Adrastos said.

The delivery was too quick, which told Theron the man knew roughly what he owed and was hoping the number was flexible.

"Markos is willing to close the account at seventeen," Theron said. "He said that clears it if it settles today."

Markos had said twenty. Seventeen today was worth more than twenty in six more months of silence, and Markos would count it and feel the relief before he felt the gap. It was not a lie that needed to be perfect. It only needed to work once.

Adrastos looked at Kassandra. She looked back at him with the flat patience of someone who has nowhere else to be and no investment in which way this went. The staff rested against her shoulder. She was twelve years old and she looked like someone who had reasons.

Adrastos looked at the harbor for a moment.

"Fifteen," he said.

"Seventeen," Theron said. "That's the number I was given. I can't go below the number I was given."

The pause stretched. A cormorant landed on a mooring post and left again. Someone down the row was hammering.

Adrastos reached into his belt and counted coins out with the slow deliberateness of someone who hates every coin as it leaves. Seventeen drachmae, stacked on the net frame, pushed toward Kassandra. She took them without counting them, which was the right move. Counting them in front of him would have made him feel inspected.

"We're square," Adrastos said.

"Markos will be pleased," Theron said.

---

They made it back to Markos's yard before the sun reached the wall. Markos took the two bags and counted everything twice, which was his standard process, moving his lips slightly. When he finished the second count his expression did the thing it did when the numbers were actually good.

"Seventeen from Adrastos," he said, drawing it out. "I told you twenty."

"He pushed back," Kassandra said. "It was seventeen today or nothing until winter."

Markos weighed this for a count of three. Then he sorted out their shares, coins placed flat on the table with the deliberate care of someone making sure the act of payment was witnessed. Six obols for Dorokles, two drachmae each from Adrastos, put into their hands.

"You two," he said, in the voice he used for things he found difficult to say plainly, "are not the worst investment I have made." A pause. "Not the best. But not the worst."

"High praise," Kassandra said.

Markos laughed, brief and genuine, and went back inside. The yard settled into the early evening: chickens behind the neighboring wall, the harbor at low tide, someone arguing about a cart two streets over. Kassandra sat on the top of the wall and took her sandals off to check for a stone. Theron sat on the ground with his back against the house wall and felt the hills in his legs.

`[ Balance: 3 dr 5 ob + 6 ob + 2 dr = 6 dr 5 ob ]`

Six drachmae and five obols. The iron knife was four drachmae. He had passed the threshold and not noticed, which was a pleasant way to discover it.

"The three obols from the roof-patching job last week," Kassandra said, not particularly to him. "I'm putting them toward leather for the staff grip tomorrow."

"What's wrong with the current one."

"Too smooth. When it's wet it slips." She looked at her hands. "Don't want to lose it in a real situation."

Theron thought about what a real situation meant for a twelve-year-old in this century, in this specific town, with this specific history waiting further down the road. She was already training for something she could feel the shape of without knowing its name. The island was not going to stay small.

"That's sensible," he said.

She looked down at him. "You're not going to ask what I mean by real situation."

"You're a mercenary in training who lives with a merchant who has expensive debts and no muscle," he said. "You don't need to explain it."

Something in her posture shifted, a small degree, the kind of shift that happens when someone realizes they do not have to account for themselves to the person they are talking to.

"Markos doesn't know yet," she said. "About the big debts."

"He knows. He just hasn't looked at all the numbers at once." Theron picked a piece of grit off the ground and rolled it between his fingers. "He'll have to, eventually. The way things are going, probably within the year."

The big debts were not abstract to him. He knew roughly what form they would take when they came into focus, a man in the hills with one eye and a collection of Markos's promissory notes and a tendency to collect interest in ways that became other people's problems. That was not today's concern. It was not even this season's concern. But it was coming, and the answer to it was not to be unprepared when it arrived.

"When he does," Kassandra said.

"When he does," Theron agreed, "we'll deal with it."

She was quiet for a moment, looking at the harbor turning orange in the late light. Then: "You sound sure."

"I'm sure we'll have to deal with it. Reasonably sure we can." He watched the sparrow that had landed on the roof across from them. "Not today. But by then."

She said nothing else. With Kassandra, silence after a statement like that was not dismissal. It was the sound of someone filing something away.

---

The house went quiet around the second hour of night. Theron sat on his grain sack in the storeroom and looked at the ceiling for a while before opening the shop. He did it out of habit, the same way he used to open a news tab at the end of a long day, to look at the things he was working toward before he stopped thinking about them.

`Sandals (good pair) ..... 2 dr 0 ob      [can afford]`
`Rope, 10 cubits ......... 3 ob            [can afford]`

He could buy the knife tomorrow. He made a note to do it.

He scrolled down past the affordable tier, because he always did.

`Permanent +1 STR ............... 250,000 dr`
`Concentrated Isu Blood, 5% ..... 4,320,000 dr`
`Fragment of Eden, minor ......... 71,000,000 dr`
`Isu Blueprint ................... LOCKED`

Four million, three hundred twenty thousand drachmae. He had six drachmae and five obols. The distance between those two figures was not discouraging. It was structural. It told him what the shape of the work had to be: not individual debt-collection jobs but compound growth across years. Trade networks, Isu relics bartered for shop credit, the kind of capital that came from being in the right place at the right time with enough foreknowledge to make luck look like planning.

He closed the shop.

The list in his head was the other thing he looked at before sleeping. MEM 16 kept it current and specific without effort.

Phoibe was not in Sami yet. She would arrive later, young, and Markos would take her in the same way he had taken the two of them. She would become part of the household, part of Kassandra's world. And in approximately fifteen years from now she would be in Athens during the plague, running an errand for someone she trusted, and the Cult of Kosmos would be the last thing she encountered. The exact mechanism was something Theron had played through twice in his old life and still flinched at. He had time to change it. The shape of the change was not clear yet, but it was not going to involve her dying in a plague city doing work she was given by people who used her.

He kept the thread and did not pull on it. Not yet.

Brasidas was on the mainland, fighting in a war that was still in its early years. Amphipolis was two decades away, or close to it. That one he kept in pencil, not ink.

Alexios was the one he set down when the room was quiet, because thinking about him too long before sleeping was not useful. The boy was already taken, already in the Cult's hands, already being built into something that would spend years hurting people he should have been raised beside. The technology to undo what they had done to him did not exist in this century. Theron would have to build it, or buy it at a cost he could not currently imagine, and that was the work of decades. He kept the thread. He did not pull.

The grain sacks smelled dry and faintly sweet. The harbor was quiet below the town. Somewhere outside the cat that lived on the neighboring roof was conducting a slow argument with the cat that wanted to.

He was nine years old. He had six drachmae and five obols. He had a list of people he intended to keep alive and a System no one else could see and a merchant who had just called him one of the least bad investments he had made, which was as good a foundation as any.

Tomorrow he would buy the knife.

He slept.
