# Chapter 1: The Terror of Kephallonia

Six years on the island taught Theron three useful things.

The first was that his body lied to him. His mind still belonged to a man of thirty-some years who had spent a decade learning to be tired precisely on schedule, and that mind kept trying to sit down in the shade and wait for a weekend that was never coming. The body disagreed with all of it. It was small and new and quietly on fire with the System's growing talent, and it wanted to climb cliffs and throw rocks at gulls and swim until the light went. Most mornings the two of them argued about it. The body usually won.

The second was that Markos would put anything on a tab. Bread, wine, a goat, a fishing boat that belonged to another man, the goodwill of people he had already cheated once. Theron had watched him talk his way out of three beatings and into one entire vineyard, and somewhere in those six years he had started loving the man the way you love a dog that keeps eating your sandals and looking proud about it.

The third thing was Kassandra, and the third thing did not fit on a list.

She was older than him by a handful of years and wore them like armor she had forged herself. When the sea spat Theron onto the shingle half-drowned, she was the one who decided, without a word and without asking, that he was hers to keep alive. She did not do it gently. She looked after him the way a young wolf looks after a smaller one, cuffing him when he lagged and putting herself between him and anything with teeth. He grew up knowing the exact weight of her hand steering him out of a fight by the back of the neck, and the exact pitch of her laugh when he had been the one to start it.

He was careful around her, more careful than a boy his age had any business being. He knew things about her that she had no idea he knew. The wound under her ribs that had never left a scar. The mountain. The hand that let go. He knew what the years ahead had lined up for her, every cruelty of it, sitting in his borrowed memory like the plan of a house he had already watched burn to the ground. So he learned to simply be there. At the edge of the trouble, close enough to change one small thing when the moment came, and quiet about the rest.

"You've gone somewhere again," Kassandra said.

They were up on the ridge above Sami, watching the harbor turn the color of poured bronze under a low sun. She sat with Leonidas' broken spear across her knees, the way another girl might hold a cat, working through a fig she had certainly not paid for. Theron lay back on the warm stone and pretended his legs did not ache.

"Just thinking," he said.

"You think too much. It makes your face stupid." She said it the way she reported the weather, without any heat in it. "One day you'll be staring at nothing like that and walk straight off the cliff, and I'll have to climb down and carry you back up, and I promise you I will not be gentle about it."

"You're never gentle about anything."

"No." She sounded pleased with herself. "Eat." She tore the fig and threw him the larger half without looking. He caught it. Six years had taught him to catch whatever she threw.

The System sat where it always sat, a pane of pale light folded into the corner of his vision, patient as a shopkeeper who knew perfectly well you could not afford anything in the window. He thought the word out of habit and the catalogue opened.

**SHOP.**

Six years of Markos's errands had turned into a small and unglamorous pile of drachmae, and he spent his evenings the way other boys spent theirs on prayer: standing in front of prices with no bottom to them. Concentrated Isu blood. A Fragment of Eden. And under those, worse than either, the one thing he genuinely wanted and could not come close to affording, knowledge, the raw stuff, the drawings and workings of the people who had built the world and then lost it. Numbers with too many digits to read at a glance.

What he could afford was bread, rope, a whetstone, and a poultice that would keep a wound from turning. So he bought the poultice. Then he bought a second and tucked it into his belt. He was building a habit one obol at a time, the habit of always having the small cheap thing that kept a person breathing, because he had seen how this story went and he knew where the bodies actually came from. The battles took fewer than people thought. The gaps took the rest. A missing bandage. A guard no one had counted. An errand that looked too dull to be worth worrying about, right until the afternoon it killed someone he loved.

"Theron!"

The voice arrived well ahead of the girl, high and certain and entirely without fear, and then Phoibe herself came around the rocks at a flat sprint and skidded to a stop on the loose stones with the confidence of someone who had never once considered falling.

"Markos wants you. Both of you." She dropped her hands to her knees and caught her breath in big theatrical gulps. She was eight, all elbows and opinions, and she carried the wooden eagle Theron had carved for her last winter and carved badly. "He's bought a vineyard. An actual vineyard. With what, I would love to know, because he still owes you money, and he owes me a honey cake, and Kassandra, out of everyone, he owes you the most."

"He owes me a horse," Kassandra said, and stood, unfolding to a height Theron privately considered unreasonable. "If there is no horse, I am going to sell his vineyard back to him one grape at a time."

"He said you would say that." Phoibe turned her grin up at Theron, gap-toothed and pleased with herself, and his chest went warm and cold in the same breath the way it always did around her. She was bright and brave and, in a city two years and a hundred sea-miles from this ridge, running an errand for a woman she had not yet met, she was going to die on a particular street on a particular afternoon. He had known the date for six years.

He looked at her carving her name into everything she touched, and made the promise again, in plain words this time, because he had learned the plain ones were the only kind that held. He was going to keep her. All of them. He had a lifetime of other people's mistakes memorized, and he meant to spend every last one of them.

"Then we shouldn't keep the horse waiting," he said, and pushed up off the rock, and his legs did not ache at all.

There were things moving that Kassandra could not see yet. Across the water a one-eyed man called the Cyclops was owed coin Markos would never manage to repay. Somewhere a man named Elpenor was already deciding to write a letter that would tip the whole board over. And down underneath all of it, an order that hunted the blood in this laughing girl's veins was beginning to wake up and stretch. The story was going to start whether Theron felt ready or not. It always did.

The one difference this time sat quiet and unremarkable at the table with everyone else, and no one else knew it was there. There were two players now. And he had spent his whole life, both of them, reading ahead.

He fell into step beside Kassandra on the path down, matching her stride the way six years had trained him to, and she gave him a sideways look for the small private smile he had not quite managed to put away.

"What."

"Nothing," he said. "Good day for it, is all."

She snorted. "You say the strangest things, little brother." But she knocked her shoulder into his as they walked, and stayed close, and the harbor burned gold below them the whole way down.
