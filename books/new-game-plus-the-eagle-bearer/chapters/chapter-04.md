# Chapter 4: The Wolf of Sparta

Elpenor of Kirrha smiled the way a knife smiles, which is to say not at all, with a lot of shine.

He found them the day after the Cyclops, in a house on the far side of the island where a handful of bandits had been squatting and making themselves a nuisance. The bandits were dead when Theron and Kassandra arrived, all but the last, and the last one died while they watched, quietly, at the hand of the thin well-dressed man who had hired them and evidently disliked loose ends. Elpenor stepped over the body the way you step over a puddle.

"You must be the misthios everyone speaks of," he said to Kassandra. "The one with the broken spear. Good. I have work that requires someone the gods have not made squeamish."

Theron kept his face arranged in mild, unremarkable interest, and underneath it his whole mind was very still and very cold.

He knew this man. Not from this island and not from this life. He knew the shape of what Elpenor was, what he served, the long patient thing coiled under all of Greece that had been hunting the girl beside him since before she was born. He knew that trusting this man would be a mistake, and he knew that Kassandra was about to do it anyway, and he knew, worst of all, exactly why he was going to let her.

Because the road ran through Elpenor. It always had. You could not get to the people worth saving without walking the path the story had already laid down, and the path started here, with a thin man and a bad smile and a job.

"What kind of work," Kassandra said.

The first job was a test, and Theron let it be one.

Elpenor wanted a shroud, an old thing, Penelope's shroud, from the ruins of Odysseus' palace on Ithaka. It was the sort of errand that meant nothing on its own and everything as a measurement, a man weighing a blade before he decided what to cut with it. So they sailed to Ithaka, and Barnabas told them a story about every rock they passed, and on the island they found the shroud in the keeping of a bandit who did not keep it long.

They also found a woman named Odessa, cornered and about to become someone's convenient death, and Theron steered them a few steps out of their way to make sure she was not. It cost them an afternoon. It was the kind of cost he never argued about. He had a running tally in his head of people the story meant to grind up in passing, the ones nobody remembered, and he spent afternoons on them freely, because afternoons were cheap and people were not.

"You do that a lot," Kassandra said, watching Odessa walk free toward the harbor. "Turn aside for strangers."

"They're only strangers because we haven't been introduced," Theron said.

She shook her head, but she was almost smiling, and she did not tell him it was a waste, because six years had taught her that his turnings-aside had a way of mattering later.

They brought the shroud back. Elpenor was pleased. And then he named the real job.

"There is a general," Elpenor said, "in Megaris. They call him the Wolf of Sparta. A hard man, a dangerous one, in the way of things I am trying to accomplish. I want him dead, and I am told you are the woman who can do it." He set a purse on the table that was too heavy for a stranger's life. "Do this, and we will talk about what comes after. There is always something that comes after."

Kassandra reached for the purse.

Theron did not stop her. That was the hardest thing he had done in either of his lives, and it did not look like anything at all from the outside, a boy leaning against a doorframe with his arms crossed, saying nothing, being in the room. Inside, he was turning it over one more time, the way he had turned it over a hundred nights on the Adrestia's deck while Barnabas snored.

He could warn her. He could say, that man is your father, do not take his coin. And she would ask how he knew, and he would have no answer that did not sound like madness or magic, and the trust he had spent six years building one quiet correct instinct at a time would crack down the middle. She would look at him differently. She would start to wonder what else he knew, and once she started wondering she would not stop, and the whole delicate machine of getting these people to the good ending would seize.

Or he could let her walk into it, and be beside her when it broke, and make sure that when she found the Wolf at the end of the contract she found him with Theron close enough to turn a killing into a reckoning. Nikolaos did not have to die. That was the whole secret he was carrying. The story said the Wolf could be spared, and a family that thought itself shattered could begin, slowly, to be a family again. But only if Kassandra reached that mountain herself, with her own hand on the choice.

So he let her take the coin, and he hated it, and he did it anyway, because loving people in a story you have already read is mostly the discipline of not stealing their moments even when you could.

They sailed at dawn.

Barnabas stood at the tiller with the wind coming fresh off the strait and Megaris a grey smudge ahead, and Kassandra stood at the rail turning Elpenor's purse over in her hand with a frown she could not seem to put down. Theron came and stood beside her, the way he always did, at the edge of it, close.

"You're quiet," she said.

"So are you."

"The Wolf of Sparta." She said the name slowly, tasting it, and something in her face was already reaching toward a truth she did not have words for yet. "Barnabas. This general. You sail these waters. You know the talk. Who is he?"

Barnabas was quiet a moment, which for Barnabas was a kind of alarm. "The Wolf," he said carefully, "is a Spartan general named Nikolaos. A great man, some say. A hard one." He glanced at her, and then away. "Why do you go pale, misthios?"

Kassandra did not answer. Her hand had closed white-knuckled around the purse, and she was looking at the grey line of the coming shore as if it had reached across the water and taken hold of her.

"Kassandra," Theron said quietly.

"That is my father's name," she said. Her voice was very flat and very careful, the voice of someone holding something that might go off. "The man who threw me from the mountain. The general is my father."

The wind pulled at the sail. Megaris came on, closer, grey and waiting. Theron stood beside her and said nothing that was not true, which meant he said almost nothing at all.

"Then we'd better find out what kind of man he is now," he said, "before you decide what to do about it."

She looked at him then, really looked, and whatever she saw she did not question it. She just nodded once, and turned back to the shore, and let him stay beside her the whole way in.
