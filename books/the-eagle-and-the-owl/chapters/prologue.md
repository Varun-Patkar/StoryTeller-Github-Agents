# Prologue: Eighteen

Theron turned twelve on a Tuesday.

His mother made pancakes before school, thin ones with crisp edges because those were the only kind he liked. His father left a wrapped box beside the plate and pretended it had nothing to do with him. Inside was a soldering station Theron had spent three months asking for and two weeks claiming he no longer wanted.

He burned his thumb before breakfast was over.

Until then, illness had been something that happened to other families.

Theron had spent his childhood in a house with too many rooms and a garage his father never managed to use for cars. Half of it belonged to abandoned hobbies. There was a treadmill under a dust cover, a set of golf clubs with clean grips, and three boxes from a home-brewing phase nobody discussed. Theron claimed the other half before he turned eight.

His first workbench was an old dining table. His father shortened the legs, fitted a sheet of plywood across the top, and took Theron to buy plastic drawers for components. Theron labeled every drawer in careful block letters. RESISTORS. MOTORS. THINGS I BROKE BUT MIGHT NEED.

The last drawer filled fastest.

At six, he dismantled the automatic soap dispenser because it made a grinding sound. He put it back together with two pieces left over. It worked better afterward. His father kept the spare pieces in a kitchen drawer in case the dispenser later remembered it needed them.

At seven, Theron built a night-light that turned toward movement. The motor was louder than the family vacuum and woke everybody when someone crossed the hallway. His mother endured it for four nights before asking whether the light could watch a quieter part of the house.

At eight, he programmed a small wheeled robot to carry cold drinks from the kitchen to the sitting room. It struck the same doorframe every trip. Theron adjusted the sensors, rewrote the route, and eventually put a strip of foam around the robot. His father called that practical engineering.

School came easily. Theron finished mathematics exercises while the teacher was still explaining them and then got in trouble for opening programming tutorials on a classroom tablet. He corrected adults too often. He knew this because adults told him so.

His parents found him harder work than his grades suggested.

"Knowing the answer doesn't make you pleasant," his mother said after a parent-teacher meeting.

Theron sat in the back of the car with his arms folded. "She said electricity moves at the speed of light."

"Close enough for ten-year-olds."

"I'm ten. It wasn't close enough for me."

His father looked at him in the mirror. "Your mother is asking you to learn diplomacy."

"Is there a course?"

"Several. You're going to hate all of them."

They gave him room to be difficult. They also made him apologize.

The family traveled during school holidays. Theron saw Singapore from the top of the Flyer and spent most of the ride studying the wheel's support structure. In Rome, he photographed drains, scaffolding, and a broken ticket machine. In Athens, his parents hired a guide who told stories about gods and war while Theron lagged behind to look at tool marks in the stone.

He liked hotels, airports, and railway stations. Every place hid systems inside it. Water appeared on high floors. Luggage crossed whole buildings without getting lost very often. Thousands of strangers moved through the same doors and somehow reached different countries.

At eleven, Theron entered a national student engineering competition with a low-cost sensor network for detecting leaks in apartment buildings. His team consisted of Theron and a classmate named Arjun, whose main contribution was convincing judges that Theron wasn't angry with them.

They won their age group.

Theron came home with a glass trophy and placed it behind a box of servo motors. The prize money went into better tools. A local paper called him a young genius. His classmates used the phrase for two days, mostly when they wanted him to repair something.

He expected life to continue that way. School would lead to university. University would lead to a laboratory or a company with his name buried somewhere in the paperwork. He would build useful things, earn too much money, and complain about meetings.

His parents worried about ordinary problems. Screen time. Bad posture. Whether he had enough friends. Theron worried about battery density and the quality of the school's internet.

He had never once spent a single night in a hospital.

That afternoon he ran the length of the school field, came third, and threw up behind the equipment shed.

Nobody thought much of it. Theron had skipped lunch to help a teacher recover files from a dead laptop. The day was warm. He had run too hard. His mother collected him from the nurse's office, touched the back of her hand to his forehead, and bought him coconut water on the drive home.

He slept for fourteen hours.

On Wednesday, his legs still hurt.

By Friday, the stairs at school had become a problem. Theron stopped halfway up to the physics floor and made a show of checking his bag. Two boys pushed past him. One asked whether he had forgotten how stairs worked.

Theron told him the stairs had a design flaw.

He waited until the landing cleared before hauling himself up by the rail.

The first doctor blamed a virus. The second blamed stress. The third asked his parents whether Theron had always been anxious.

He sat on the examination table, paper crackling under his shoes, while three adults discussed his sleep. Nobody asked why his hands had started shaking.

Tests followed. Blood first. Then scans, electrodes, needles, and rooms kept cold enough to justify the blankets stacked by the door. His family could afford all of it. That helped with appointments and private rooms. Money made waiting more comfortable. It couldn't make the answers arrive.

Theron missed a week of school, then a month.

At first his friends sent him class notes and short videos. They complained about teachers, shared jokes he only half understood, and promised to visit. Some did. Their visits grew awkward as the equipment around his bed multiplied. They had football practice, exams, parties, and new friends who knew what had happened yesterday.

Theron learned to tell them he was tired.

He usually was.

His mother brought his desktop computer from home. His father complained about the number of cables and spent an hour routing every one of them neatly behind the hospital cabinet. The nurses objected to the second monitor. Theron built a folding mount that tucked it against the wall during rounds, and the objections faded.

The computer gave shape to the hours.

Games helped first. In a hospital bed, he could climb mountains, sail between Greek islands, and walk through cities full of people with somewhere to be. He could fail without frightening his parents. He could die and start again five seconds later.

Kassandra became a favorite. She swore at politicians, kicked men from cliffs, and crossed Greece with an eagle circling overhead. Theron liked the bright water around Kephallonia. He spent too long in the starting area, wandering roads he had already cleared because the island felt warm.

He played Red Dead Redemption 2 slowly. Arthur deserved the time. He finished The Last of Us in three nights and then refused to discuss the ending for a week. Metro frightened him enough that he kept one earphone out, listening for nurses in the corridor.

The games ended. The days did not.

So he learned.

He started with programming because he already knew enough to be dangerous. Then electronics, machine learning, biomechanics, genetics, and every piece of medical language that appeared in his records. He read papers with a dictionary open beside them. When the papers referred to other papers, he found those too.

The doctors became less fond of him.

"Your creatine kinase is elevated again," one said during morning rounds.

"By how much?"

"Enough that we need another panel."

"The last three panels showed the same pattern."

The doctor glanced at Theron's parents. "Patterns can be misleading."

"So can averages. Which mitochondrial genes did the lab include?"

His father covered a cough with his fist. His mother stared at the window.

The doctor pulled a stool closer.

That conversation led to another specialist, a revised test, and finally a name.

The name was long. Theron understood each part of it separately. Together, the words described a rare mitochondrial disorder with an ugly appetite. His cells could produce enough energy for ordinary life until they couldn't. Muscles weakened. Nerves degraded. The heart followed. Other organs joined whenever they pleased.

Treatment meant slowing the damage, managing symptoms, and hoping his particular mutation behaved better than the small collection of case studies suggested.

His mother asked for the prognosis.

The specialist took off her glasses.

Theron watched her place them on the desk. He understood before she spoke.

He was thirteen by then.

School became something that happened through a camera. Theron completed assignments in half the allotted time and used the rest to work on his own projects. A robotic arm came first, built from printed parts and cheap motors. It poured water badly, spilled most of it, and knocked a glass onto the floor during its first demonstration.

Theron's father looked at the puddle.

"Promising," he said.

"It missed by three centimeters."

"Very promising."

They improved it together.

His father had no gift for coding, but he was patient with screws and wires. His mother handled the growing stack of research requests, consent forms, and correspondence from laboratories. Neither of them told Theron to stop. They asked him to sleep. They brought food he forgot to eat. They learned when to leave him alone.

At fourteen, he could still walk across his room with braces. At fifteen, he used a powered chair. His hands remained useful if he rested them often. Voice control filled the gaps.

The disease took things in small, insulting pieces.

One month he lost the strength to lift a full kettle. Later he couldn't sit upright through an entire film. A respiratory infection put him in intensive care and left him weaker than before. He recovered enough to return to his computer, which counted as victory.

The diagnostic project began after a message from a girl in Brazil.

She was eleven. Her parents had spent seven years moving between hospitals. The translated records attached to the message filled ninety pages. Theron recognized fragments of his own search in them: symptoms filed under separate specialties, genetic variants dismissed because each appeared harmless alone, test results measured against ranges built from common diseases.

He couldn't diagnose her. He knew that. He also knew the information had been handled badly.

Theron wrote a program to organize the records.

The first version was little more than a parser tied to a medical database. It extracted symptoms, dates, medications, family history, laboratory values, and genetic findings. Then it mapped relationships among them and ranked papers a doctor might want to read.

The program suggested six possibilities for the girl in Brazil. The fifth led her doctors to a test they had never ordered.

Her father sent Theron a photograph after treatment began. She stood in a yellow dress, holding a paper crown over her head.

Theron printed the picture. His mother taped it beside his monitor.

More records arrived.

He called the project Lantern because naming software at three in the morning encouraged bad judgment. A university team offered computing resources. A genetics laboratory gave him access to anonymized data. Doctors who had once answered his questions carefully began asking their own.

Lantern never announced a diagnosis. It found connections and showed its work. Every suggestion linked back to the symptom, variant, scan, or paper that had raised it. Theron refused to build a black box that delivered certainty with a percentage beside it. He had met too many confident people already.

By sixteen, Lantern had helped shorten the diagnostic process for hundreds of patients. Theron released the core system under an open license on a Sunday evening.

On Monday morning, the hospital communications director arrived with two assistants and a smile that made Theron suspicious.

"A reporter would like to speak with you."

"Which reporter?"

She named one from a major international network.

Theron looked at his mother. "Did you do this?"

"I sent an email."

"To international news?"

"I sent several emails."

The first interview led to another. Someone used the phrase tragic genius in a headline, and everyone else copied it. Theron hated the words on sight. They made him sound dead already.

The world learned his face.

For a few weeks, his room became a studio. Cameras framed out the ventilator equipment and the lift near the bed. Interviewers asked where he found his courage. Theron had no useful answer. He worked because he was awake and the computer was there.

They asked what he planned to build next.

That question hurt more.

He smiled anyway. He talked about improving Lantern's support for underrepresented genetic populations. He described privacy tools and hospital integration. He thanked the researchers who had done the real medical work.

After the cameras left, his mother helped him settle back against the pillows.

"You were good," she said.

"I sounded like a brochure."

"A very handsome brochure."

"That's worse."

She laughed, then turned away to wipe her eyes.

Lantern spread. Hospitals adopted pieces of it. Volunteers translated the interface. The system found mistakes, missed connections, and occasionally a path toward treatment. Theron read every story his parents let through.

None of it changed his own prognosis.

At seventeen, his hands stopped cooperating for more than a few minutes at a time. Eye tracking and voice control took over. His lungs needed help at night, then during the day. Pain settled into his joints and stayed there.

Medication softened it. The stronger doses softened everything else too.

Theron returned to games when reading became difficult. He started new saves and rarely finished them. Sometimes he loaded a world and left the character standing beside a road while the music played.

Kassandra waited on a beach in Kephallonia.

Arthur sat near a campfire and worked on his journal.

Joel carried Sarah through a town coming apart.

Artyom listened for a radio signal above Moscow.

Theron knew what waited for all of them. He had once enjoyed that knowledge. Near the end, it bothered him.

People talked about stories as though suffering made them valuable. Perhaps it did. Theron still wanted to reach through the screen and change things.

He wanted Arthur to see John raise Jack.

He wanted Joel to have one honest conversation before lies hollowed out his second chance.

He wanted Kassandra to bring her whole family home.

Mostly, he wanted to go outside.

His eighteenth birthday came on another Tuesday.

There were no pancakes. He could no longer eat them safely. His mother brought a tiny chocolate cake from the bakery he had liked as a child and set it on the table where he could see it. His father placed one candle in the middle.

"Fire code," Theron whispered.

His voice sounded thin even through the microphone beside his bed.

"I know a man," his father said.

The blinds were open. Beyond the glass, rain tapped against the hospital window. Cars moved along the road six floors below, their tires lifting pale fans of water.

His parents had decorated the room with old photographs. Theron at five, missing a front tooth. Theron at nine, holding a homemade drone with one propeller already broken. Theron at twelve, squinting at the soldering station before he burned his thumb.

The photograph of the girl in the yellow dress remained beside his monitor.

Messages had arrived from everywhere. Doctors, patients, strangers, classmates he had not spoken to in years. The hospital offered to play a compilation. Theron asked them not to.

He spent the morning with his parents.

They talked about ordinary things. His father had backed the car into a planter. His mother had started watching a terrible detective series and refused to admit it was terrible. A Lantern update had broken date formatting in three languages.

By afternoon, the pain climbed past what the usual medication could manage.

The nurse explained the next dose. Theron knew what it would do. He had heard the explanation before.

"Will I wake up?" he asked.

The room went still.

The nurse looked at him with the steady kindness of someone who would not lie. "You might."

Theron considered that.

He was scared. The feeling came cleanly, without philosophy attached to it. He wanted another year. He wanted one day with working legs. An hour would have done.

His mother held his left hand. His father took the right, careful of the sensor taped to his finger.

"I didn't get to do anything," Theron said.

His father bent over their joined hands. "You did more than most people manage in a lifetime."

"That's work."

The words cost him a breath.

His mother leaned close enough that he could feel her hair against his cheek.

"What did you want?"

Theron looked through the rain-streaked window.

He pictured blue water. Dusty roads. Snow under pine trees. A horse moving beneath him. A ship deck under bare feet.

"To see it," he said. "For real."

His mother kissed his forehead.

The nurse adjusted the line.

Warmth moved into his arm. The pain loosened slowly. His chest eased. The room remained, but its sharp corners faded.

His father was saying something about the first robot arm and the broken glass. His mother laughed. Theron tried to answer. He wasn't sure whether sound came out.

Rain crossed the window in silver threads.

He thought of the beach on Kephallonia because it had always looked warm.

Then the room slipped away.
