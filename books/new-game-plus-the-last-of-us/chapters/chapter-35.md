# Chapter 35: Worth the Wait

## — Sarah —

August 14, 2016. Crystal Falls, Colorado.

The streets had names now.

Sarah walked down Main Road with her clipboard in one hand and a mug of chicory in the other. Donna had named the street and painted the sign herself, at the intersection of "the path from the gate" and "the other path from the gate." The chicory was Dr. Reyes's recipe. Roasted dandelion root and wild chicory, brewed in the big steel pot Frank had welded from scrap. It tasted like coffee the way a drawing of a sunset looks like a sunset. Close enough to be sad about the difference.

Seven forty-five in the morning and Crystal Falls was already awake.

The compound didn't look like a compound anymore. Somewhere in the last sixteen months it had crossed the line from "a place where people survived" to "a place where people lived." The dining hall had a proper porch now, with a bench Frank had built and a railing Carson had installed because Dr. Reyes tripped on the step in January and Joel declared the step a security liability. The greenhouse complex had doubled. Two main structures, four raised beds, a cold frame, and a root cellar that Carson and David Chen had dug into the hillside. Lena's domain. Lena, who had stopped being "the woman with dirt on her ear" and become something closer to "head of agriculture," a title she refused to use and that Sarah had put on the council agenda three times.

Two hundred and fourteen people. Sarah had counted them last Tuesday, because counting was what she did when she couldn't sleep. Up from a hundred and forty-eight at the harvest festival in April of last year. They'd come in groups and singles. Families from collapsed settlements in Grand Junction and Durango. A pair of doctors, or more accurately one doctor and one veterinarian, but Dr. Reyes said a vet was better than nothing. A retired electrician from Pueblo who'd walked two hundred miles because someone told him about a dam with working power.

They came because the lights were on. In a world that had been dark for three years, Crystal Falls glowed.

Mia Haywood ran past her chasing a boy Sarah didn't recognize. One of the newer kids. Mia was eight now. She'd lost the flower crowns from last year and gained a vocabulary she'd definitely picked up from listening to Joel's militia drills.

"Mia. Don't run near the generator building."

"We're not near it!"

"You will be in about ten seconds at that speed."

Mia adjusted course by three degrees and kept running. Some battles weren't worth the clipboard.

Sarah passed the bulletin boards outside the dining hall. Three boards now. Schedules, announcements, community notices. Gene had posted another complaint about the shower building door being left open and letting moths in. There was always a note from Gene.

Inside, Joel was already at the council table with his own mug of chicory. He drank it every morning, complained about it every morning, and would have been deeply offended if anyone tried to take it away.

"Mornin'," he said.

"Morning. Margaret's on the water trough again."

"The goat?"

"The goat."

"Ain't my goat."

"She's the community's goat, Joel."

"She's on your section of the schedule. Says so right there." He took a sip. The ghost of a smile. Joel Miller had more smiles now than he'd had a year ago. Not many more. But more. He played guitar on Saturday evenings by the fire pit. He'd taught Mia to whittle last month and called it "keeping her hands busy" instead of admitting he enjoyed it. He was softer in places. Not soft. Joel Miller would never be soft. But the sharp edges had rounded, and Sarah was the only person who tracked these things because tracking things was what she did.

The other council members arrived over the next ten minutes. Tommy with a turbine report. Helen with a patrol update. Dr. Reyes with a supply request. Donna with the watch rotation. Marcus last, because Marcus was always last to indoor meetings. He preferred being outside.

He sat down across from Sarah. The acid scars on his forearms from the Shambler fight in Aspen were faded now. Thin pink lines visible only if you knew where to look. Sarah knew where to look. She had eleven pages of Marcus observations in her journal. She hadn't shown them to anyone.

The meeting was routine. Tommy needed two days of reduced power for turbine maintenance. Helen wanted to cut the Redstone patrol from weekly to biweekly, since nothing had come through that corridor in three months except deer. Dr. Reyes needed antibiotics restocked. Marcus approved the purchase. Eighty-seven points.

"Where are we on points?" Sarah asked.

"Twenty-four twelve. We're comfortable."

Comfortable. A word they'd never used in Year 1. A word that felt earned now. The farm produced enough food. The clinic was stocked. The cabins were built. The defenses were solid, including the Shambler protocols Marcus and Joel had developed after the Aspen run. Six-person minimum for any contact south of Carbondale, acid-resistant gear from the Mall, mandatory decontamination afterward. They hadn't encountered another Shambler since, but the protocols stayed active. Because the fungus was evolving, and comfortable didn't mean careless.

"Comfortable is good," Sarah said. "Next item."

## — Marcus —

The meeting ended at ten fifteen. Forty-five minutes for two hundred and fourteen people. The problems had gotten smaller, or the council had gotten faster. Probably both.

Marcus walked out into the August sun and stood on the dining hall porch, watching the town. He still caught himself thinking "compound" and correcting to "town." A compound was temporary. A town was the kind of thing that lasted.

Kids in the school building, which David Chen had reinforced and fitted with real windows. Adults on work details. Keiko running a patrol drill at the south gate, her voice clear across the yard. She'd become Joel's second-in-command in everything but title. Carson and a crew of four framing the new workshop extension that would give Frank a proper metalworking space.

Routine. Normal. The daily life of a small town that happened to exist because a gamer with a heads-up display had decided to build something instead of just survive.

He was halfway to the workshop when he heard Sarah's voice behind the dining hall. She was talking to Raymond about the meal schedule. A logistics question. Nothing important. But her voice caught him the way it did sometimes. Not the words. Just the sound of certainty. The particular way she organized the world into a shape that worked and expected it to stay that way.

He'd been noticing this more lately. The voice. The pen behind her ear when she was thinking. The tilt of her head when she was really listening to someone, with her full attention. The way she looked at him during council meetings with an expression he couldn't quite read.

Marcus walked to the workshop. Picked up a hammer. Drove nails into framing boards for two hours while Carson talked about the foundation depth. Ate lunch with Tommy and Dale at the long table in the dining hall. Walked the north perimeter in the afternoon and checked the tripwire stations that Helen maintained with religious precision.

Normal day.

Except he spent all of it thinking about Sarah Miller. Not as the Community Coordinator. Not as Joel's daughter. Not as the girl he'd pulled off a sidewalk in Austin with a bullet wound in her hip. As the person who ran half of everything he'd built. Who'd told him in a church in Texas that he was weird but she trusted him. Who'd danced with him at a harvest festival and leaned her head against his arm because his shoulder was too high.

The person he was in love with.

"Well," Marcus said to nobody, standing alone on the north ridge with the afternoon sun warm on his face. "That's a problem."

It wasn't new. That was the thing he couldn't get past. There was no single moment. No switch that flipped. It had just built up, one day at a time, one conversation at a time, one council meeting and one clipboard argument and one instance of her being exactly right about something at a time. The way deposits build on the dam's intake grate. Slowly. Invisibly. Until one day Tommy goes to clean it and says, "How did this get so thick?"

He sat on a rock and opened the system, because that was what he did when he didn't know what else to do.

**[Points: 2,412]**
**[Settlement Population: 214]**
**[Morale: High]**

The system didn't have a category for this. It tracked points and population and morale and threat levels and inventory. It did not track the fact that its operator was sitting on a Colorado ridge trying to figure out what to do about being in love with a fifteen-year-old.

He closed the system. The mountains were quiet and didn't judge him.

## — Joel —

Joel noticed because Joel noticed everything.

He was on the platform by the fire pit, tuning the guitar. The strings were proper nylon now, found in a music store during a Glenwood Springs supply run. The tone was cleaner. Still the same cracked body, still the same wood-glue repair, but the sound had improved. His fingers remembered what they were supposed to do.

He was adjusting the E string when Marcus came back from the north perimeter and walked past the dining hall. And Joel saw Marcus look at Sarah through the window.

Not a glance. A look. The kind of look that lasted two seconds longer than it needed to.

Joel set the guitar down.

He'd been watching this for a while. Not spying. Being a father. Marcus and Sarah talked every day. Argued about the clipboard. Finished each other's sentences at meetings. They ran the town together, and half the compound thought they were already together and the other half thought they were both oblivious.

Joel wasn't sure which half was right. But he'd seen the look, and the look was honest. Joel knew what honest looked like because he'd spent twenty years around people who weren't.

He picked up the guitar again. Played a slow G to C transition. Smooth now, after a year of Saturday sessions.

He'd talk to the boy. Sometime. When he had the right words for it. Joel Miller wasn't good with words, especially about his daughter, especially about his daughter and a man whose entire existence Joel still didn't fully understand.

But the boy stayed. That was the thing Joel kept coming back to. Three years in and Marcus hadn't run, hadn't cracked, hadn't turned into the kind of man who used strength as a shortcut. Joel had known men like that. He'd been one, in a different life. Marcus wasn't that.

Joel played "Blackbird." Slowly. He didn't think too hard about who he was playing it for.

## — Marcus —

August 17, 2016.

Three days of not saying anything. Three days of being normal and eating lunch and checking the perimeter and attending a meeting about the goat fence. Three days of being an adult who ran a town and apparently couldn't manage a single conversation about feelings.

He found Sarah on the dam walkway at sunset.

She was leaning on the railing, no clipboard, watching the spillway. The overflow dropped thirty feet into the pool below, white water turning gold in the evening light. Her hair was longer than it used to be. She'd stopped cutting it short six months ago.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey."

He leaned on the railing next to her. A comfortable distance. Close enough to talk, far enough to pretend this was just two colleagues watching the water.

"No clipboard," he said.

"Donna confiscated it. Said I needed ten minutes off."

"She's not wrong."

"She's never wrong. That's the problem with Donna."

The evening was quiet. The turbine hummed below them. Someone in the compound was laughing. A door closed somewhere. The ordinary sounds of a town being a town.

"You've been weird," Sarah said.

"I haven't been weird."

"Three days of weird. You skipped lunch on Thursday. You never skip lunch. You ate an entire jar of pickled beets last week because it was there."

"That's a separate issue."

"Marcus." She turned to face him. Arms crossed. The posture of a person who was going to listen to every word and then say exactly what she thought about it. "What's going on?"

He looked at the water. The concrete railing under his hands, cracked and patched and solid. He'd stood here a hundred times. It was where he came to think, to check the system, to remind himself that all of this was real.

"I need to tell you something," he said.

"Okay."

"And I don't know how to say it. I've been trying to figure out the right way for three days and there isn't one. So I'm going to say it badly."

"I've always preferred badly to not at all."

He looked at her. Fourteen, turning fifteen next month. Five foot four. Clipboard calluses on her right hand. A scar on her left hip from a bullet on a sidewalk in Austin. The person who ran Crystal Falls while he fought infected and stared at a heads-up display. The person who'd told him, three years ago in a church in Texas, that he stayed when other people ran.

"I'm in love with you," he said. "And I know the timing isn't right. I know you're fifteen. I know I'm older and this is complicated and there are two hundred and fourteen people counting on us to be responsible. But I'm bad at pretending, and you deserve to hear the truth."

Four seconds of silence. Marcus counted them.

Sarah looked at him. Closed her eyes. Opened them.

"I've known for a year, idiot," she said. "I was waiting for you to figure it out."

Marcus blinked. "You... what?"

"Since the festival. You danced with me and then stood up here on this dam for forty minutes staring at nothing. You're not subtle."

"I thought I was subtle."

"Donna knows. Tommy knows. I'm pretty sure Mia knows, and she's eight."

Marcus put both hands on the railing. "Does Joel know?"

"Dad knows everything. He hasn't shot you, so take that as a good sign."

The evening settled around them. The turbine hum. Raymond's voice carrying faintly from the dining hall. Stars appearing over the eastern ridge, one at a time, the way they did when the sky was clean and there was no light pollution for eighty miles.

"I'm fifteen," Sarah said. Quietly. Not upset. Just stating it.

"I know."

"I won't be fifteen forever."

"I know that too."

"So we wait." She said it the way she said everything. Like the math was already done and the rest of the world just needed to catch up. "We're not going anywhere. The town isn't going anywhere. I'm going to be sixteen, and then seventeen, and then eighteen, and you're going to be here the whole time because that's what you do. You stay."

He remembered the church in Texas. Her voice in the dark, saying the same thing. You stay.

"I stay," he said.

"So we wait. We don't make it weird. You stop skipping meals because it makes Donna suspicious and I don't need her asking questions." She turned back to the railing and looked at the water. "Also. For the record. Me too."

"You too what?"

She didn't answer. She just looked at him with the expression he'd never been able to read, and he realized he could read it now. He'd always been able to. He'd just been afraid of what it said.

They stood on the dam walkway in the last light. Not touching. Not needing to. Two people who'd built a town together and were honest enough to say what was true and patient enough to wait for the timing to be right.

The lights came on below them. One by one. Cabin by cabin. Two hundred and fourteen people eating corn soup and arguing about goat fences and living the ordinary lives that had been built for them out of points and labor and stubbornness and a decision, three years ago, to run toward something instead of away.

Marcus opened the system one last time.

**[Points: 2,412]**
**[Settlement Population: 214]**
**[Morale: High]**

He closed it. Some things didn't fit in numbers.

"Come on," Sarah said. "Donna's making corn soup, and if we're late she'll give your bowl to Raymond."

They walked down from the dam together. Same distance. Same pace. Everything the same and nothing the same.

Behind them, the water fell and kept falling, patient and steady and in no rush at all.
