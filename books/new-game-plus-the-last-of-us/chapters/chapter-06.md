# Chapter 6: The Economy of Survival

## — Marcus —

The rooster woke him up.

Not a real rooster. A bird on the roof that thought it was a rooster, screeching at the first grey light like it had a personal grudge against sleep. Marcus opened his eyes and stared at the unfamiliar ceiling and took three seconds to remember where he was. Farmhouse. Texas Hill Country. Day three of the apocalypse. Right.

He was on the floor of the second bedroom, the one with the faded quilt and the bookshelf full of Louis L'Amour westerns. He'd slept in his clothes. His shoes were next to his head because Tommy had told him to keep them close, and Tommy struck Marcus as a man whose advice about shoes was worth taking.

His back ached. His neck was stiff. The cut on his calf itched, which probably meant it was healing, or possibly meant it was getting infected. He chose to believe the first one.

"Status," he thought.

**[Name: Marcus Webb]**
**[Age: 24]**
**[Points: 488]**
**[Health: 85%]**
**[Attributes: Strength 6, Agility 5, Constitution 6, Perception 5, Intelligence 7, Willpower 11]**
**[Skills: None]**
**[Traits: Guardian — +15% healing effectiveness when treating others]**
**[Perks: Efficient Provider — 10% discount on consumables]**
**[Evaluation: Day 3. Resting. Don't get comfortable.]**

"Don't get comfortable," he muttered. "Thanks for the pep talk."

He stood up and pulled the blinds. The sky was pale blue, clean for the first time since the outbreak. The smoke had drifted east overnight. The fields outside were gold and brown, dry September grass running out to a line of mesquite trees that looked like they'd been standing there since before Texas was Texas. The windmill turned in a breeze he couldn't feel through the glass. Somewhere in the barn, the calico cat was probably plotting its revenge against Tommy.

Normal. It looked normal. If you didn't know what was happening a hundred miles south, you'd think it was just another morning in the Hill Country.

He found Joel in the kitchen. Already up. Already moving. Joel had a cup of water on the counter and was standing at the window with his arms crossed, watching the gravel drive that led down to the main road. The handgun Marcus had given him was on the counter next to the water. Joel hadn't carried it. He'd set it down the way you'd set down a gift from someone you weren't sure about yet.

"Mornin'," Joel said without turning around.

"Morning."

"Tommy's in the barn. Checking the perimeter."

Marcus nodded and poured himself water from a jug they'd filled at the well pump the night before. It tasted like iron and minerals. Like drinking a penny. He drank the whole glass.

Joel hadn't moved from the window. His eyes tracked something along the fence line. A deer, maybe. Or nothing. Joel was the kind of man who watched even when there was nothing to watch.

"How's Sarah?" Marcus asked.

"Slept through the night. Breathin's good. Fever's down." Joel paused. "She ate crackers around midnight. Got up on her own to use the bathroom. I helped her back."

"That's good. The fact that she's mobile at all is good."

Joel didn't respond. He took a sip of water and kept watching the window. Marcus knew a dismissal when he saw one. He took his glass and went to the back porch.

...

He sat on the porch steps and opened the Mall.

Not for shopping. For studying. He'd been buying things in a panic for two days, grabbing whatever he needed and not thinking about the structure underneath. Now, with a roof and a morning and nobody actively trying to kill him, he had time to actually look.

The Mall had layers he hadn't noticed.

The main categories were the same: Food & Water, Medicine, Tools, Weapons, Clothing, Shelter, Enhancements. But each category had sub-tabs now. Food & Water split into Preserved, Fresh, Bulk, and Specialty. Medicine split into First Aid, Pharmaceuticals, Surgical, and something called Field Remedies that included herbal stuff and natural antiseptics. Tools split into Hand Tools, Power Tools, Agricultural, and Construction.

He tapped Construction. The prices made him sit up straighter.

Lumber, per 100 board feet: 25 points. Concrete mix, 80lb bag: 5 points. Nails, 50lb box: 3 points. Rebar, per bundle: 15 points. A full roofing kit with shingles: 100 points. Razor wire, per 100-foot roll: 20 points.

"Razor wire," he said to nobody. "The Mall sells razor wire."

He scrolled further. Generators: 150 points for a basic gasoline one, 400 for a diesel, 800 for a solar panel array with battery storage. Water filtration, household: 50 points. Water filtration, community scale: 300 points.

The numbers told a story. The system wasn't just a survival tool. It was a civilization-building kit. You could buy building materials, infrastructure, power generation. If you had enough points, you could build a town from scratch.

But the points. That was the problem.

He pulled up his quest log.

**[QUEST LOG]**
**[Completed: Save Sarah Miller — 500 pts + Guardian trait]**
**[Completed: Find Safe Shelter — 200 pts + Efficient Provider perk]**
**[Active: None]**

Two quests completed. Seven hundred points earned from quests total. He'd earned another handful from killing Runners. Call it 730 points earned in three days. He'd spent 242 of that on supplies. Current balance: 488.

The system rewarded actions. Killing infected, completing quests, protecting people. That was the economy. Kill things, do things, earn points, spend points. Simple enough on paper. The problem was the math.

A community-scale water filtration system cost 300 points. A solar array cost 800. A single stat point cost 5,000. Even the cheap stuff added up fast. At his current earning rate, maybe 100 points per day if he actively hunted infected, it would take him three days to afford a water filter. Eight days for a solar array. Fifty days for a single stat point.

And that was assuming he could find infected to kill every day, which meant leaving the farmhouse, which meant leaving Sarah and Joel and Tommy unprotected.

He closed the Mall and leaned back against the porch post.

There had to be a better way. The system was stingy with quest rewards, and infected kills were nickel-and-dime. If the system wanted him to build something, it needed to give him a way to earn faster.

He opened the system again and poked around the edges. Tabs he hadn't explored. Under his profile, past the stats and traits, there was a section called Achievements.

**[ACHIEVEMENTS]**
**[First Blood — Kill your first infected. (Complete) Reward: 5 pts]**
**[Guardian Angel — Save a life using system resources. (Complete) Reward: Trait unlock]**
**[Shelter Seeker — Secure safe shelter for your group. (Complete) Reward: Perk unlock]**
**[Streak: 3 — Kill 3 infected in a single outing. (Incomplete)]**
**[Nest Breaker — Clear an infected nest (5+ infected in one location). (Incomplete)]**
**[First Contact — Establish trade or alliance with another survivor group. (Incomplete)]**
**[Handyman — Complete a significant construction or repair project. (Incomplete)]**

Streak bonuses. Nest clearing bonuses. Rewards for building and for diplomacy. The system wanted him active on multiple fronts, not just farming Runners one at a time. The more diverse his actions, the faster the points would flow.

He stared at the Nest Breaker achievement. Five infected in one location. That was dangerous. But the reward for clearing a nest would probably be worth it, and clearing nests was exactly what you had to do if you wanted to make an area safe.

"So that's the game," he said quietly. "You want me to go out and be a hero."

The system didn't answer. It never did. It just sat there with its blue glow and its neatly organized tabs, patient as a vending machine.

He closed it out when he heard the screen door open behind him.

## — Joel —

The farmhouse was solid.

Joel had built enough houses to know the difference between solid and just-standing-up. This one was solid. Pier and beam foundation, properly set. Two-by-six exterior walls, not the cheap two-by-four junk you got from the big builders. The roof was composition shingle, maybe ten years old, no curling, no missing tabs. Whoever built this place knew what they were doing and didn't cut corners.

The windows were the weak point. Single-pane glass, old-style wood frames. A man could put his fist through any of them. Or a Runner could. Joel made a mental note. Plywood. They needed plywood to board the ground-floor windows. Not all of them. You needed to see out. But the ones on the sides, the ones that faced the barn and the back field where someone or something could approach without being seen from the front.

Tommy came in through the back door, smelling like hay and motor oil. "Barn's got a workbench. Some hand tools. A saw, a hammer, box of nails. There's plywood sheets leaning against the wall behind the tractor."

"How many?"

"Six. Maybe eight."

Joel nodded. "That's enough for the side windows. We'll leave the front and back uncovered so we can watch the road and the field."

They worked through the morning. Joel measured the windows by hand. He didn't need a tape measure. Thirty years of carpentry and he could eyeball a standard window frame to within a quarter inch. Tommy cut the plywood with a handsaw while Joel pried nails from a rotting fence rail and straightened them on the workbench.

Marcus showed up around nine. He stood at the edge of the barn and watched for a minute, then walked over.

"Need help?"

Joel looked at him. The kid was wearing the same grey hoodie from Outbreak Night, still spotted with blood that had dried to a rust color. He had the hunting knife on his belt and the backpack over one shoulder. He looked like a college student who'd wandered into the wrong camping trip.

"You know how to swing a hammer?" Joel asked.

"I've hung a picture frame."

"Close enough. Hold the plywood against the frame while I nail it."

They worked in silence. Marcus held the boards steady while Joel drove nails. The kid had decent hands. Steady. He didn't flinch when the hammer came down an inch from his fingers. That was something. Joel had worked with grown men on construction crews who couldn't hold a board without shaking.

Tommy was on the roof, checking for loose shingles. Joel could hear him walking in slow circles overhead, testing each section with his weight.

"There's a root cellar under the kitchen," Marcus said. "I noticed the door in the pantry floor. If something comes and we can't fight, that's a fallback."

Joel looked at him. "You checkin' escape routes?"

"Always."

Joel didn't respond. He drove another nail. But he filed the information away. The kid thought about exits. That was either paranoia or experience, and either one was useful.

They finished the side windows by noon. Joel stepped back and examined the work. Not pretty. Rough-cut plywood nailed over window frames with recycled nails. But it would hold against a human shoulder and probably against a Runner's fists. A Clicker would tear through it eventually, but Clickers weren't quiet. You'd hear them coming.

"It's good work," Tommy said, climbing down from the roof. He was looking at Marcus when he said it. An offering. A small one.

Marcus nodded. "Thanks."

Joel went inside to check on Sarah.

## — Sarah —

The couch was getting old.

Not the couch itself. The couch was fine. Worn floral print, sagging cushions, the kind of couch that belonged in exactly this kind of farmhouse. It was the lying-on-it that was getting old. Sarah had been horizontal for the better part of two days, and her body was staging a protest. Her back hurt from the springs. Her neck was kinked from the throw pillow. Her right hip, the one with the bullet wound, had settled into a dull ache that spiked whenever she moved wrong, which was every time she moved.

She could sit up now. That was progress. She could swing her legs off the couch and put her feet on the floor and sit there like a normal person, which she was doing when Joel came back inside.

"You should be lyin' down," he said.

"I've been lying down since forever."

"It's been two days."

"Same thing."

Joel's mouth did the thing where it almost smiled but stopped. He sat down next to her and checked her forehead with the back of his hand. She let him. She was twelve, not a baby, but some things weren't worth fighting about, and Joel touching her forehead was one of them.

"Fever's gone," he said.

"I know. I feel fine."

"You got shot two days ago."

"And now I feel fine."

He gave her the look. The dad look. The one that said "I know you're lying and I know you know I know and we're both going to pretend this conversation went differently." She'd been getting that look her whole life. It was almost comforting.

"Can I at least sit at the table?" she asked. "I want to eat lunch like a person, not like a patient."

Joel considered it. She could see him running the math. Risk of her walking eight feet versus the psychological benefit of eating at a table. Joel's brain worked like that. Everything was a cost-benefit analysis with her safety as the only variable that mattered.

"I'll help you," he said.

He put his arm around her waist and she put hers around his shoulders and they shuffled to the kitchen table in a slow, awkward two-person dance that probably looked ridiculous and hurt more than she let on. She sat down in a wooden chair and the simple act of sitting upright at a table made her feel more human than she had since the gas station.

Tommy heated canned chili on the stove. Marcus brought crackers and peanut butter from his backpack. The four of them sat at the kitchen table and ate lunch together, and for about ten minutes it was almost normal. Almost like a family meal, if your family included your dad, your uncle, and a stranger who could make supplies appear from thin air.

Sarah watched Marcus while she ate.

She'd been watching him since the gas station. Not obviously. She was good at watching people without them noticing. Joel had taught her that, or she'd learned it from Joel, which was the same thing. Watch people's hands. Watch their eyes. Watch where they look when they think nobody's looking at them.

Marcus's hands were steady. His eyes moved a lot, scanning, checking corners and windows and exits. Normal behavior for someone in danger. But then there was the other thing. The thing he did when he thought nobody was watching.

He stared at nothing.

Not spaced-out staring. Not daydreaming. Focused staring at a point in the air about two feet in front of his face, his eyes moving like he was reading something, tracking left to right and then down, like a page. Like a screen. He'd done it last night on the porch. He'd done it again this morning. And every time he came back from one of those episodes, he had something new. Water. Crackers. Information he shouldn't have.

Sarah ate a spoonful of chili and thought about that.

"Marcus," she said.

He looked at her. The others looked too. Tommy paused with his spoon halfway to his mouth.

"What did you do? Before all this."

A normal question. A getting-to-know-you question. The kind of thing you'd ask anyone at a table. Marcus's expression didn't change, but she saw his eyes flick sideways, just for a second, to that empty spot in the air.

"Nothing interesting," he said. "Worked in an office."

"What kind of office?"

"The boring kind. Desk, computer, bad coffee."

"Where?"

"Does it matter?"

"I'm making conversation. You're making it weird."

Tommy snorted. Joel didn't react, but Sarah could feel his attention sharpen. He was listening without looking like he was listening.

Marcus took a breath. "Fair enough. I worked in tech support. Answered phones, fixed people's computers when they forgot their passwords. It wasn't exciting."

"And you just happened to be in Austin when the world ended."

"Yeah."

"And you just happened to know first aid."

"I took a CPR class once."

"A CPR class taught you how to pack a gunshot wound with hemostatic gauze?"

Silence. Tommy set his spoon down. Joel was very still.

Marcus looked at her. Really looked, not the careful, friendly look he usually gave her but something sharper. She'd surprised him. Good. She was tired of being the wounded kid on the couch. She had a brain, and her brain had been working overtime while the rest of her healed.

"I've done some research," Marcus said. "Online. Before all this. Survival stuff. Prepper forums."

"You're a prepper."

"No. I just... read a lot."

"About gunshot wounds."

"About a lot of things."

Sarah took another bite of chili. She chewed slowly. Let the silence sit.

"My dad's a contractor," she said. "He can build a house from scratch. Tommy was in the military. He can shoot and he knows tactics. You're a tech support guy who reads prepper forums, and you ran toward gunshots with medical supplies in your pockets."

She paused.

"You don't add up, Marcus."

The kitchen was very quiet. The wind outside pushed against the plywood Joel had nailed over the side window. The propane stove ticked as it cooled.

Marcus put his spoon down. He looked at Sarah, then at Joel, then at the table. She watched him decide. Watched him weigh options in his head, the same way she'd watched Joel do it a thousand times. Except Marcus wasn't weighing wood and nails. He was weighing words.

"You're right," he said. "I don't add up. And I can't explain it in a way that would make sense. Not yet. But everything I've done, everything I've pulled out of that backpack, every decision I've made since I found you guys, has been to keep you alive. That's the truth, even if the rest of it doesn't make sense."

"Why us?" Sarah asked. "There were other people out there. Why did you come looking for us specifically?"

"Because I knew you were in trouble."

"How?"

The question hung in the air like smoke.

Marcus looked at Joel. Joel looked back. Something passed between them, some male communication that Sarah couldn't decode, and Marcus turned back to her.

"I can't answer that," he said. "Not because I don't want to. Because the answer sounds crazy, and you've had enough crazy for one week."

Sarah held his gaze for a long moment. He didn't look away. Didn't fidget. Didn't do any of the things liars did. He looked like a person telling the truth about not being able to tell the truth, which was a strange thing to believe, but she believed it.

"Okay," she said. "But I'm going to keep asking."

"I know you will."

She went back to her chili. Joel exhaled through his nose. Tommy picked up his spoon. The moment passed, but Sarah filed it away in the same place she kept everything else she didn't understand yet. The folder was getting thick.

## — Joel —

Evening light came through the uncovered front window in long gold bars that stretched across the living room floor like the slats of a fence. Joel sat in the armchair by the window with the 9mm in his hands.

He was cleaning it. Not because it was dirty. It had come out of Marcus's backpack brand new, still smelling like factory oil, not a speck of carbon on it. He was cleaning it because cleaning a gun was something his hands knew how to do, and when his hands were busy his mind could think without spinning.

The gun was a SIG Sauer P226. Military issue. Quality piece. He'd fired one at a range once, years ago, when Tommy was home on leave and they'd gone shooting on a Saturday. It fit his hand well. The sights were clean. The trigger pull was smooth, maybe four and a half pounds.

A gun like this cost eight hundred dollars before the world ended. Marcus had pulled it out of a backpack like it was a granola bar.

Joel ran the cleaning rod through the barrel and thought about that.

He didn't trust Marcus. That wasn't new. He didn't trust most people. But distrust had layers, and Joel was experienced enough to know the difference between "this person might hurt me" and "this person is hiding something that I can't figure out." Marcus was the second kind. Everything about him was wrong in a way that didn't feel dangerous but didn't feel right either.

He was too calm. That was the first thing. Joel had been through one day of the outbreak, one day, and he'd already gone to a place in his head that he didn't like thinking about. The man kneeling over his daughter's body on the highway. The man who would have killed Tommy's friend Marcus with his bare hands if Marcus hadn't started helping. The man who watched Austin burn and felt something inside him go cold and flat and functional.

Joel had changed overnight. The apocalypse had reached inside him and rearranged the furniture and he could already feel the new shape of himself, harder and smaller and meaner.

Marcus hadn't changed. Or if he had, he'd changed before they met. He moved through the chaos like someone who'd already processed it, already grieved for the old world, already decided who he was going to be in the new one. That kind of adjustment took weeks. Months. Not two days.

Unless he'd known it was coming.

Joel set the gun down on his knee and watched Marcus through the window. The kid was on the back porch again, sitting on the steps, staring at that spot in the air. Doing the thing. The reading-nothing thing. Joel had seen him do it four times today. Each time, Marcus's eyes moved like he was scrolling through a phone that wasn't there. Each time, he came back with something. Information, a plan, supplies that appeared in his backpack when nobody was looking.

Sarah thought Marcus was a prepper. Tommy thought Marcus was military, maybe government, with access to a supply cache. Joel didn't know what he thought. He just knew that the man on the porch had saved his daughter's life with materials that didn't exist five seconds before he used them, and that no explanation in the world was going to make that make sense.

But Sarah was alive. Sarah was sitting at the kitchen table eating chili and giving Marcus the third degree with questions that would have made a detective proud. Sarah was breathing and talking and being a pain in the ass and alive, alive, alive, because the stranger on the porch had run toward gunshots with bandages that came from nowhere.

Joel picked up the SIG and racked the slide. Smooth. Perfect action.

He put the magazine back in and set the gun on the side table next to the armchair. Close enough to grab in a second. That was what he'd do. Keep the gun close. Keep Marcus close. Keep watching.

And keep his daughter closer than both.

## — Marcus —

Night fell the way it falls in the country, slow and full of sound. The sky went from gold to orange to a deep blue that settled over the fields like a blanket, and the stars came out in swarms that you'd never see from a city. No light pollution. No streetlamps. No headlights on distant highways. Just stars and the thin crescent of a moon and the endless chirp of crickets counting down seconds that nobody was keeping track of.

Marcus sat on the back porch with a bottle of water and an MRE he hadn't opened yet. Turkey and stuffing. He wasn't hungry. His stomach was too busy being a knot.

Sarah's questions had rattled him. Not because they were hard. Because they were exactly the right questions, asked in exactly the right order, by a twelve-year-old who was too smart for any cover story he could come up with. She'd cornered him in five moves. A tech support guy from up north who reads prepper forums and happens to have hemostatic gauze and a SIG Sauer and an infinite backpack. Even a kid could see the gaps.

He needed a better story. Or he needed to tell the truth. And the truth was, "I'm from another world where your life is a video game and I know everything that's going to happen for the next twenty years and I have a magic shopping menu in my head that gives me things for points I earn by killing zombies."

Yeah. The truth wasn't going to work.

He opened the system. Not the Mall. The map function he'd noticed tucked behind the Achievements tab.

**[LOCAL AREA MAP]**
**[Range: 2 miles]**
**[Markers: 0 hostile, 0 infected, 3 friendly (Joel, Tommy, Sarah)]**

Zero infected. Zero hostile. A clean perimeter. For now.

He scrolled out, extending the range. At five miles, the map was still empty. At ten miles, three red dots appeared to the southeast. Infected. Not close. Not moving toward them. Just sitting there in whatever building or culvert they'd wandered into, waiting for something alive to stumble past.

At fifteen miles, more red dots. A cluster of seven, sitting on top of what looked like a small town. Further south, toward Austin, the map turned into a smear of red that he couldn't count. Hundreds. Maybe thousands. The entire metro area, infected and burning and lost.

They were in a pocket of safety. A bubble. The farmhouse sat in a stretch of empty country that the infection hadn't reached yet, insulated by distance and low population density. But the bubble wouldn't last. Infections spread. Runners wandered. People fleeing the cities would stumble through, and some of them would be bitten, and some of them wouldn't know it yet, and one morning the clean perimeter wouldn't be clean anymore.

He needed to start thinking about the long term. Not tomorrow. Not next week. Months. Years.

The plan in his head was rough but taking shape. Stay at the farmhouse until Sarah could walk. Two weeks, maybe three. Use that time to make supply runs, clear nearby infected clusters, earn points. Build up a stockpile. Then move. West, toward Colorado. Find the dam. Not Jackson, that was Tommy and Maria's, and it belonged to them. A different dam. There were dozens of hydroelectric sites in the Rockies. Find one, secure it, start building.

But that plan assumed Joel and Tommy would come with him. And right now, Joel could barely look at him without his hand drifting toward the gun.

A notification blinked in his peripheral vision. He opened it.

**[AREA ALERT]**
**[Infected movement detected: 3 Runners heading NW from cluster at 12 miles SE.]**
**[Estimated arrival at current location: 36-48 hours.]**
**[This is an informational alert. No quest assigned.]**

Three Runners. Heading northwest. Toward them.

Not a crisis. Three Runners were manageable. He'd killed one with a kitchen knife on his first night. With a handgun and the hunting knife and Joel and Tommy backing him up, three would be a problem for about thirty seconds.

But it meant the bubble was shrinking. The safe zone was getting smaller. The farmhouse had a shelf life, and the system was polite enough to tell him about it.

He dismissed the alert and leaned back against the porch railing. Inside, he could hear Tommy washing dishes at the hand pump. The clink of metal in the basin. The creak of the floor as Joel moved from the armchair to the couch where Sarah was already asleep, tucked under the quilt from the spare bedroom, her breathing slow and even.

Three days in. A farmhouse, a group, a system, a girl with too many questions, and a father who didn't trust him but couldn't afford to send him away. Three Runners heading their direction in a day and a half. Four hundred and eighty-eight points and a catalog full of things he couldn't afford yet.

It wasn't much. But it was a start.

He opened the MRE. Turkey and stuffing. It was cold and bland and tasted like cardboard and salt, which was exactly what every MRE in the history of the American military had ever tasted like.

He ate the whole thing anyway. Calories were calories. And tomorrow was going to be a long day.
