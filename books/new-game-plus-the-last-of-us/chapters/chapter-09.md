# Chapter 9: Fifteen Miles a Day

## — Tommy —

FM Road 1431, or what was left of it.

Tommy kept point because nobody asked him to and everybody needed him to. Military habit. The guy at the front of the column sets the pace, picks the route, spots the problems before they become problems. Joel could do it, and did when it mattered, but Joel kept drifting back to walk beside Sarah. Which meant the front was empty. Which meant Tommy filled it.

Day three on the road. Forty-something miles behind them, seven hundred and change ahead. Not bad for a group that included a twelve-year-old with a bullet wound and a fourteen-year-old who stopped every few hours to stare at nothing.

The Hill Country was kind to them. Rolling limestone ridges covered in live oak and cedar, dry creek beds they could follow without being visible from the road, abandoned ranch properties every few miles. They hadn't slept outside yet. Every evening Tommy would find another house with the doors standing open and nobody home, and they'd set up in the kitchen or the living room and eat whatever Marcus pulled from the bottomless backpack and sleep in shifts.

The pace was honest. Twelve to fifteen miles a day, depending on the terrain and Sarah's hip. She didn't complain. She'd limp for the first hour, then it would ease, then it would come back around hour four. Tommy watched the cycle. He didn't say anything about it because Sarah had a look she gave people who commented on her pain that could strip paint off a barn door.

"Hold up," Tommy said.

The road curved ahead around a limestone ridge. A dead truck sat on the shoulder, white Ford dually with the driver's door open. He'd checked it yesterday. Dead battery. The bed was full of feed sacks and a saddle nobody had gone back for.

Joel came up beside him. "What?"

"Want to check around this curve before we walk it blind."

Joel nodded. Looked back at Sarah, who was sitting on a limestone slab fifty yards behind them, drinking from a water bottle. Marcus was beside her, eyes doing the thing again. Tracking left to right, then down, like he was reading a menu printed on the air.

"Take Marcus," Joel said.

"He's doing his thing."

"Then interrupt his thing." Joel's voice was flat. The way Joel talked about Marcus, which was the way Joel talked about everything he didn't understand. Carefully. Without excess words.

Tommy walked back. "Hey. Marcus. Want to scout this curve with me?"

Marcus blinked. The staring stopped, the way it always stopped, with a quick sideways glance like he was closing a browser tab. "Yeah. Sure."

They walked ahead together. The gravel crunched under their boots. A red-tailed hawk circled above the ridge, riding a thermal, not concerned with any of them.

"What are you looking at?" Tommy asked. "When you do the staring thing."

"What staring thing?"

"The thing where you look at the air and your eyes move."

Marcus was quiet for a few steps. "Situational awareness. I'm checking things."

"Checking things how?"

"It's hard to explain."

"Try."

"I can't. Not yet." Marcus looked at him. Honest face, or what Tommy was starting to accept might be an honest face. "I will eventually. When it makes more sense to."

Tommy let it go. He'd learned in the Army that pressing someone who wasn't ready to talk was a waste of everybody's time. The truth came out when it came out. You just had to be paying attention when it did.

The curve was clear. Another stretch of empty road, another ridge, another abandoned ranch with cattle standing in a field like the apocalypse was someone else's problem. Six Herefords, red and white, chewing grass and watching the two men on the road with the blank disinterest of animals who had never needed civilization in the first place.

"Clear," Tommy called back. "Come on up."

They walked. Joel and Marcus in front, Tommy and Sarah behind. The formation had happened on its own by the end of Day One. Joel set the pace, Marcus navigated, Tommy watched the flanks, Sarah kept the middle. It wasn't something anybody discussed. It just formed, the way water finds the lowest ground, and by Day Three it was as natural as breathing.

Tommy watched the road and thought about a phrase his sergeant used to say at Fort Bragg. "You don't pick your team. Your team picks itself. Your job is to not screw it up."

He hadn't screwed it up yet.

## — Marcus —

The map pinged at four in the afternoon.

**[LOCAL AREA MAP]**
**[Infected detected: 3 contacts, bearing 310° (NW), distance 2.1 miles. Stationary.]**
**[Classification: Runners (3)]**

Two miles ahead and slightly off their route. Three Runners, nesting. Sitting in a building somewhere, standing in the dark, waiting for noise. Right in the path they'd planned to walk tomorrow morning.

"There's infected ahead," Marcus said. "Three. Two miles northwest."

Joel looked at him. The look. The one that said "how do you know that" without the actual question, because Joel had decided that not asking was the price of Sarah being alive.

"Three's manageable," Joel said. "I'll come."

Tommy was already scanning for a camp spot. He pointed at a limestone overhang fifty yards off the road, a natural shelter that blocked wind from three sides. "I'll set up here with Sarah."

"I don't need a babysitter," Sarah said. She said this every time. It never changed anything.

"Humor me," Joel said. He said this every time too.

They walked northwest into the late afternoon. The sun was behind them, shadows stretching long through the cedar and prickly pear. The heat had started to break. October was coming, and the Hill Country in early October was almost pleasant if you could forget the parts that were on fire somewhere south.

The house appeared through a gap in the trees. White clapboard, green tin roof, a porch with two rocking chairs and a welcome mat that read BLESS THIS MESS. A tire swing hung from an oak in the front yard, turning slowly in what little wind there was. A bicycle lay on its side near the mailbox. Small. Pink. Handlebar streamers.

Marcus stopped walking.

"What?" Joel said.

"Nothing. Give me a second."

He checked the map. Three contacts, ground floor, two in the back of the house, one in the front room. All classified as Runners. But one of the dots was smaller than the others. The system didn't provide age or height or the color of someone's bicycle. Just dots and classifications and point values.

But the bicycle. And the tire swing.

"Let's go," Marcus said.

They went in through the back door. Joel first, because Joel always went first, because Joel had decided that his life was worth less than a clear hallway and the information you got from being the first person a Runner saw. Marcus had stopped arguing about it on Day Two.

The back hallway was dim. Family photos on the wall. A man and a woman and a girl, maybe ten, standing in a field of bluebonnets. The Texas state flower. Somebody had written "Easter 2013" on the frame in silver marker.

Five months ago. Those photos were five months old.

The two Runners in the back bedroom came at them together. A man and a woman, mid-thirties, still wearing pajamas. Cotton. Faded plaid. The kind of pajamas you buy at Target because they're on sale and comfortable and you don't think about what you'll be wearing when the world ends.

Joel caught the man with a forearm across the throat and drove him into the wall. Marcus put the knife into the woman's temple before she could get her hands up. Quick. Practiced. The word "practiced" bothered him and he filed it away for later.

Joel finished the man. Efficient. A twist and a drop and a body on the carpet.

**[Runner Killed — 11 Points]**
**[Runner Killed — 10 Points]**

The third Runner was in the living room.

Marcus walked through the hallway past the bluebonnet photos and into the front room. She was standing by the window, facing outside, swaying in the slow rhythm Runners fell into when they had nothing to chase. Small. Thin. Pink pajamas with cartoon horses on them. Her hair was blonde and matted with fungal growth on one side, but the other side was still clean, still tied in a ponytail with a purple elastic band.

She turned when his boot creaked on the hardwood.

She was fast. Small and fast and wrong in the specific way that children who aren't children anymore are wrong, limbs moving in patterns no ten-year-old should move in, teeth bared, a sound coming from her throat that was high and thin and nothing like a scream.

He caught her. One hand on her shoulder, holding her at arm's length while she snapped at him. Light. Fragile. Like holding a bundle of sticks wrapped in cotton. The knife went where it needed to go and she stopped moving and he set her down on the floor.

Carefully. Like it mattered.

It didn't matter. She was dead before he got here. She'd been dead since the night the infection came through the door or the window or however it had found this family in their house with the tire swing and the bluebonnet photos and the welcome mat. The fungus did the rest.

He set her down carefully anyway.

**[Runner Killed — 10 Points]**
**[ACHIEVEMENT: Streak 3 — 3 kills in a single outing. Reward: 25 Points]**
**[Total Earned This Outing: 56 Points]**
**[Current Points: 672]**

Joel appeared in the doorway. He looked at the shape on the floor. He looked at Marcus. He didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say. They both knew what she'd been. The bicycle outside said it. The tire swing said it. The purple elastic band said it.

They walked back to camp. Two miles through the cedar and the prickly pear and the long shadows, and neither of them spoke the entire way. The hawk was still circling above the ridge, or a different hawk. Same lazy turns.

Fifty-six points. That's what a family was worth. Two parents and a daughter who rode a pink bicycle and had a tire swing and bluebonnet photos on the wall. Fifty-six points and a streak bonus and the taste of copper in the back of his throat that points couldn't wash away.

"Soup's on," Tommy said when they reached the overhang.

Marcus sat down and ate the soup and didn't talk about what was in the house, and Joel didn't either, and that was its own kind of conversation.

## — Sarah —

Day five, and Sarah had developed a theory about walking.

The theory was this: the first mile hurt. The second mile hurt more. The third mile hurt less, like her hip had decided that complaining wasn't producing results and switched to quiet resentment. By mile four she could almost forget about it. Miles five through ten were fine. Mile eleven was when it came back, a deep pulling ache that started in the scar tissue and worked its way down her thigh. Mile twelve was bad. Mile thirteen was the worst.

And mile fourteen, if they walked that far, was actually okay again, because by then everything below her waist had gone numb and numbness was basically the same thing as comfort if you didn't look too closely.

Today was mile eight. Comfort zone.

Tommy walked beside her. He'd been telling her about winters in Idaho, where he'd done some Army training before they shipped him somewhere else. According to Tommy, Idaho in January was cold enough to freeze the moisture inside your nose.

"Seriously?" Sarah said.

"Dead serious. You breathe in through your nose and the snot just freezes solid. You can feel it crinkle."

"That's disgusting."

"That's winter. Colorado won't be that bad, probably. But it'll be cold. You ever owned a real coat? Not a jacket. A coat."

"I'm from Texas."

"Right. We're gonna need coats." Tommy looked up at the sky, which was blue and wide and about ninety degrees, and the word "coat" felt like a joke. "Somewhere between here and the mountains, we find a department store. A real one. With winter gear."

"If there's anything left."

"People loot food and guns. Nobody thinks about coats in September."

They were walking through what used to be ranch land. Miles of wire fence, cedar posts going grey in the sun, fields of dry grass where cattle had grazed and might still be grazing somewhere. One field had a stock tank with green water in it and a turtle sitting on a log, sunning itself, utterly untroubled by the end of civilization.

Joel and Marcus were a quarter mile ahead, scouting. They'd fallen into the pattern days ago. Joel and Marcus in front, Tommy and Sarah behind. Nobody assigned it. Nobody drew a diagram. It just happened.

"Uncle Tommy?"

"Yeah?"

"You notice how Marcus does that thing with his eyes?"

Tommy walked three steps before answering. "I notice."

"What do you think it is?"

"I think it's something he's not ready to explain."

"That's a non-answer."

"It's the only honest one I've got." Tommy adjusted the rifle strap on his shoulder. The Remington 700 that Marcus had produced from the backpack on Day Two. Tommy hadn't asked where it came from. He'd just checked the action, loaded it, and said thank you. "Whatever it is, it's kept us alive. That counts for something."

"Dad doesn't think that's enough."

"Your dad doesn't trust easy. He never has. But he's here, walking to Colorado with a stranger he met nine days ago, and that's about as much trust as Joel Miller hands out in a calendar year."

Sarah laughed. The small one. The one that cost her a catch in her breath and was worth it anyway.

...

They passed through a crossroads that wasn't really a town. A gas station with the pumps already dry, a barbecue restaurant called Hank's with a faded pig logo on the sign, and a storage facility with rows of orange roll-up doors. The gas station had been picked clean. The barbecue place hadn't.

Tommy checked it while Joel covered the front door. Tommy came out carrying three cans of Dr Pepper from a fridge that hadn't been cold in ten days.

"No way," Sarah said.

The Dr Pepper was warm and flat and it was the best thing she'd tasted since before Outbreak Night. She sat on the curb outside Hank's BBQ between Joel and Tommy, drinking it in small sips, trying to make it last. Marcus was walking the perimeter of the crossroads with his eyes doing the thing, checking whatever he checked.

Joel drank his in three swallows. Tommy savored his, tilting the can back and closing his eyes like it was a cold beer after a long day instead of a warm soda in the middle of the apocalypse.

"This used to be normal," Sarah said. "Sitting somewhere. Drinking something. Not thinking about anything."

Nobody answered. Joel put his hand on the back of her head. Just his hand, warm and heavy, the way he'd done a thousand times before everything happened. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

The Dr Pepper ran out. They kept walking.

## — Marcus —

They saw the smoke first.

A thin grey line, rising from behind a ridge half a mile east of their route. Campfire smoke. During the day. Either someone who didn't know any better or someone who'd stopped caring.

Joel held up a fist. Stop. Everyone stopped.

"Campfire," Joel said. "Half mile east."

"I see it." Marcus checked the map. No infected contacts within five miles. Just the smoke.

"We go around," Joel said.

"Hold on." Tommy came up beside them, keeping his voice low. "Could be people who need help."

"Could be people who take what we got."

"Could be both," Marcus said. "Let me take a look."

He moved east through the brush, staying low, using the ridge for cover. Joel followed without being asked. Tommy stayed with Sarah, which was the arrangement that happened naturally without anyone discussing it.

The camp was in a dry creek bed. A man and a woman, late twenties maybe, and two children. A toddler asleep in a car seat that had been pulled from a vehicle, and a boy around four years old sitting in the dirt, drawing circles with a stick. The man was feeding mesquite branches into a small fire. The woman was hanging wet clothes on a line strung between two mesquite trees.

Laundry. In the middle of the apocalypse, someone was doing laundry. Marcus didn't know why that detail hit him, but it did.

Joel crouched beside him behind the ridge. Watched for thirty seconds. "Family," Joel said, quieter than before. The word changed something in his voice. Joel looked at families with children differently than he looked at anything else in the world.

"I'm going to talk to them," Marcus said.

"Be careful."

Marcus stood and walked into the creek bed with his hands visible. The man saw him immediately. Dropped the branch. Reached behind him for something. A revolver. Small caliber, maybe a .22. The man's hands shook.

"Hey," Marcus said. "Just passing through. Not looking for trouble."

The woman pulled the four-year-old behind her legs. The toddler slept on.

"Where you headed?" the man asked. His voice cracked on the second word.

"Northwest. Colorado. You?"

"Houston. There's a quarantine zone. My wife's cousin texted before the phones died. She said FEDRA's running it, they've got food and walls."

A text from a cousin. The kind of information people staked their lives on when there was nothing else.

Marcus wanted to say don't go. He wanted to say the cities are a trap, that two million people crammed behind razor wire with limited food and armed soldiers and no plan for what comes next was a math problem with one answer. He knew this from a game he'd played in a world that didn't exist anymore. He knew it the way he knew the future of every person he cared about: completely, uselessly, and with no way to explain how.

But this wasn't the game. This was a real family with a real toddler in a real car seat, and the butterfly had already been flapping its wings since Marcus pressed gauze into Sarah Miller's hip on an Austin overpass. Houston might hold. It might fall. He didn't know anymore.

"We heard the cities are rough," Marcus said. Careful. Measured. "Rougher than out here, maybe. Whatever you find, keep a way out. Don't let them lock you in somewhere you can't leave."

The man nodded. The gun was back in his waistband.

Marcus pulled two MREs from the backpack. "Here. For the road."

The woman's eyes went wide. The man took them with both hands. "Thank you. God bless you."

"Good luck."

He walked back to the ridge. Joel was still there, watching.

"You gave 'em food," Joel said.

"Two MREs."

"We don't have food to be givin' away."

"We have enough. They have two kids under five."

Joel looked at the family in the creek bed. The man was showing the woman the MREs. The boy was still drawing circles in the dirt. Patient circles, the kind of thing a four-year-old does when the adults are being strange and the world doesn't make sense yet.

Joel stood up and walked back toward the road without saying anything, which from Joel could mean twelve different things. Marcus chose to believe it was the one that meant "fine."

The family packed up their campfire an hour later. Marcus watched from the road as they headed south, toward Houston, toward whatever was waiting. The toddler was awake now, riding on the man's hip, and the boy held his mother's hand and looked back once at the ridge where Marcus had been.

Marcus waved. The boy didn't wave back. They walked south and got smaller and disappeared behind the ridge, and Marcus turned north and kept going.

## — Joel —

Night watch was the only time Joel was honest with himself.

During the day there was walking and talking and decisions and the constant work of being Joel Miller, the man who handles things, who doesn't flinch, who kept his daughter alive and his brother standing and somehow ended up following a fourteen-year-old stranger across Texas because the stranger happened to be right about everything and that was either the best reason or the worst reason to trust somebody.

At night, with Sarah sleeping and Tommy sleeping and the fire burned down to orange coals, Joel could sit with the rifle across his knees and let his guard down enough to think.

They were camped in a hay barn. Day seven. The barn was three walls and a roof, open on the south side, with stacked hay bales blocking the wind and low enough to shoot over if it came to that. Tommy had picked the spot. Good sightlines, hard to approach without noise, dry floor. Military thinking. Joel appreciated it without saying so, because that's how brothers worked.

Sarah was asleep on a hay bale with a blanket Marcus had produced from the backpack that apparently contained the entire inventory of a sporting goods store. She slept on her right side now. Always the right side. The left hip couldn't take the pressure. Her breathing was even tonight. Steady in, steady out. No hitch. The little catch on the inhale that the damaged muscle had been causing all week, gone.

She was healing. His girl was healing.

Joel watched her breathe and thought about the overpass. The headlights. The blood soaking through her shirt. The weight of her in his arms as he ran, convinced she was dying, convinced he was carrying his daughter's body through the worst night of his life.

And then the stranger. Standing in the road with his hands up. "I can help her."

Joel had nearly shot him. The safety was off and his finger was on the trigger and the only reason Marcus Webb was alive was because Tommy had grabbed Joel's arm and said "let him try, Joel, what do we got to lose?"

Everything. They had everything to lose. Sarah's heartbeat, fading. The last thing in the world that meant anything.

But Tommy was right. And Marcus was right. And Sarah was alive, breathing steady on a hay bale seven days and ninety miles later, because a stranger appeared from nowhere with medical supplies that shouldn't exist and knowledge that didn't make sense.

Joel leaned against the barn post and watched Marcus.

Marcus was on the other side of the dying fire. Sitting against a hay bale, legs crossed, eyes open. Awake, like always. Doing the staring thing. His eyes tracked left and right, reading invisible text, his lips moving in small counts.

Joel had been watching this for a week. He cataloged it the same way Sarah did, because watching people was a survival skill and Joel Miller had always been good at surviving. Marcus stared at invisible things. Marcus knew where infected were before anyone could see or hear them. Marcus pulled supplies from a bag that should have been empty days ago.

And now. Right now.

Marcus looked around the barn. Checked Joel's position. Joel kept his eyes nearly shut, his breathing slow, his body loose against the post. A performance. Joel had been performing "asleep" since Sarah was a toddler who wouldn't nap if she knew Daddy was awake.

Marcus reached to his side. To the empty ground next to the hay bale. He closed his hand around something that wasn't there, and when he opened it, he was holding a water bottle. A sealed plastic Aquafina bottle. Fresh. It hadn't been there ten minutes ago. Joel had looked at that patch of ground. There was hay and dirt and nothing else.

Now there was water.

Marcus twisted the cap, drank, set the bottle down, and went back to staring at nothing.

Joel opened his eyes a fraction more. Just enough to watch through his lashes. A trick from a lifetime of sleeping light, of waking at every creak and footstep, of being the kind of person who never quite turned off even when the lights went out.

Water from empty air. Like a magic trick. Like something from one of Sarah's movies, the ones with wizards and special effects and happy endings. Except this wasn't a movie and the ending was eight hundred miles of open road and whatever was waiting in Colorado.

He should ask. He should grab Marcus by the shirt and put him against a wall and say "what the hell are you?" the way he'd wanted to since Outbreak Night. He should demand an answer because answers meant safety and safety was the only currency that mattered when you had a twelve-year-old daughter with a bullet scar on her hip.

Joel didn't ask.

Because the answer might change things. And right now, things were working. Sarah was healing. They were moving. They had food and water and direction. Marcus was strange. Possibly dangerous. Definitely hiding something enormous. But he was also the reason Joel's daughter was breathing ten feet away instead of buried under the ashes of Austin.

Joel didn't use the word trust. Trust got people killed. What Joel had for Marcus was something smaller and more practical. A decision, renewed every morning, to keep walking and see what happened. A willingness to not ask questions. Not forever. Just not yet.

The fire popped. A coyote called somewhere out in the dark, thin and lonesome, and another answered from further off. The hay smelled sweet and dusty and faintly of rain, even though it hadn't rained in weeks.

Seven days on the road. Ninety-odd miles. Sarah walking on her own. The scar healing. The group falling into a rhythm that wasn't friendship and wasn't family and wasn't anything Joel had a name for. Just four people moving through a broken world toward a place that might be worth reaching.

Joel didn't call it hope. Joel Miller didn't use that word. Hope was a promise, and promises broke, and broken promises left you holding your daughter on a highway overpass at midnight wondering if this was the last time you'd feel her breathing.

But when the eastern sky started to go grey and Sarah turned over in her sleep and mumbled "Dad?" the way she used to at home, half asleep, reaching for the sound of his voice like it was a nightlight she could hold onto.

"I'm here, baby," Joel said. "Go back to sleep."

She did. And Joel watched the dawn come in through the open wall of the barn and sat with the thing he wouldn't name, the warm quiet thing in his chest that had been dead for a week and wasn't dead anymore, and let the morning happen around him.

## — Marcus —

Dawn. Day eight. The barn behind them, the road ahead. Same as every morning, except Sarah wasn't limping.

He noticed it at mile one. Then mile two. Then mile three, and by then he was sure. The hitch was gone. The careful, lopsided gait she'd carried for nine days, the one where she favored the right leg and leaned slightly to compensate, was just... not there. She walked straight. Even. Like someone whose body had finally decided to cooperate.

Joel noticed. Tommy noticed. Nobody said a word, because the group had developed an unspoken rule about Sarah's hip: you watched, you tracked, you never mentioned it out loud.

**[Status]**
**[Points: 647]**
**[Health: 94%]**
**[Map Range: 25 miles]**
**[Local Area: 0 infected within 10 miles. 4 contacts at 18 miles NW. Stationary.]**

He'd spent sixty-nine points during the week. Food, water purification, ammunition, a tarp, two blankets, and a bottle of ibuprofen that he'd slipped into Tommy's pack because Tommy's knees were bothering him and Tommy was the kind of person who'd walk on broken glass before admitting his knees hurt. He'd earned a hundred points from three clearing runs, counting the house with the pink bicycle and two smaller encounters that weren't worth remembering in detail.

Net gain of thirty-one points. Not exactly building an empire.

But four people were alive and fed and walking, and that was a return on investment that didn't fit on a spreadsheet.

Ninety-three miles from the farmhouse. Seven hundred and seven to go.

Ahead: West Texas, then New Mexico, then the long climb into the Rockies. Behind: a farmhouse with Louis L'Amour books on a shelf, a feed store with five bodies on the floor, a white clapboard house with a pink bicycle in the yard, and a family of four heading south toward Houston.

Marcus didn't look back.

"How far today?" Tommy asked from the front of the column.

"Fifteen," Marcus said. "If we're up for it."

"I'm up for it," Sarah said. She was behind Joel, matching his stride, her copy of Last of the Breed tucked under one arm. The morning light caught the side of her face and for half a second she looked like the photo in the game's opening credits. The version of Sarah Miller who went to soccer practice and bought her dad a watch and didn't know what was coming.

Except this Sarah did know. This Sarah had been shot and survived and walked ninety-three miles on a healing hip and was still here, still reading westerns, still keeping a list of every impossible thing Marcus did.

She was going to be something. He didn't know what yet. But something.

"Fifteen miles," Joel said from the front, and started walking.

Marcus followed. Tommy fell in behind Sarah. The formation held. The road stretched west through the Hill Country, limestone and live oak and dry grass and a sky so wide it made everything underneath it feel small and temporary, which it was.

Seven hundred and seven miles. One day at a time. Fifteen miles at a time.

That was the math. Simple and brutal and the only kind that mattered anymore.
