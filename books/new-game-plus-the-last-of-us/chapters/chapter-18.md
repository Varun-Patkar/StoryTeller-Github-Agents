# Chapter 18: Home

## — Marcus —

They found the truck on the third day past the school.

A blue Ford F-250 sitting behind a ranch house twelve miles into New Mexico, keys in the cupholder, half a tank of diesel. The tires were low but intact. The battery was dead.

Marcus bought a jump starter from the Mall. Forty-five points. Expensive for a one-use item, but the math was simple: walking 578 miles at fifteen miles a day meant five more weeks on foot. Driving meant days.

The battery caught on the second try. The engine turned over with a cough and a rattle and then settled into a rough idle that smoothed out as the diesel warmed. Tommy checked under the hood. Oil was low but not empty. Coolant was good. The serpentine belt had some wear but would hold.

"She'll run," Tommy said. "Not pretty, but she'll run."

Joel drove. He'd driven bigger on job sites. Tommy rode shotgun with the atlas and a pencil, marking their route west. Sarah stretched across the back seat with a paperback she'd found at the ranch house, something with a cowboy on the cover and a title she wouldn't tell anyone. Marcus sat beside her, watching the map display and the terrain through the window.

The truck changed everything.

They covered sixty miles the first day. Eighty the second. The county routes were mostly clear if you kept off the interstates. The interstates were graveyards. Cars bumper to bumper, abandoned in the first wave of evacuations, doors left open, luggage scattered on the asphalt like the world's worst yard sale. Some of the cars had bodies in them. They didn't look.

Marcus cleared infected at every stop. Gas stations, rest areas, small towns where they pulled over to stretch and scavenge. The Runners in New Mexico were thinner and slower than the Texas ones, like the dry heat had baked some of the fight out of them. Easy kills. Five points here, nine points there.

**[Points: 412]**

They siphoned diesel from abandoned trucks at a weigh station outside Gallup. Joel found a full can of motor oil in a repair shop and topped off the engine. Tommy rotated the atlas pages and recalculated. Through Farmington, then north into Colorado on 550.

Two weeks on the road, give or take. Marcus lost count of the exact days somewhere in the flat brown nothing between Albuquerque and the state line. Time moved differently in a truck. The landscape scrolled past like a loading screen, hours of sameness interrupted by brief stops where something tried to kill them and Marcus earned points for the trouble.

Then they crossed into Colorado.

...

The landscape changed in ways Marcus wasn't prepared for.

West Texas had been flat and brown and enormous, the kind of empty that made you feel like the planet was mostly dirt. New Mexico was the same but with mesas, red rock shelves rising out of the desert like someone had stacked giant books and walked away. Colorado was different. Colorado went vertical.

The first mountains appeared as a blue smudge on the western horizon. Then the smudge became individual peaks, snow-capped, enormous, filling the windshield. The road climbed. The air got thinner. Cooler. The scrub brush gave way to ponderosa pines, then Douglas firs, then aspens with bare branches scratching at a grey November sky. Most of the leaves were gone. A few gold holdouts clung to branches and trembled in the wind.

Joel drove slower. The roads narrowed and wound through valleys between ridges. Frost on the guardrails in the morning. Ice in the puddles. Their breath fogged in the truck cab until the heater kicked in, which took twenty minutes of rattling and groaning before it produced anything warmer than the outside air.

"Turn here," Tommy said.

They were on Highway 133, south of Carbondale. Tommy had been leaning forward in his seat for the last hour, studying the terrain through the windshield like a man recognizing a face he hadn't seen in years.

"County road half a mile ahead. Gravel. Goes south along the Crystal River."

Joel took the turn. The gravel road was rough but passable, hemmed in by rock walls on one side and the river on the other. The Crystal River was living up to its name. Cold, clear water running fast over grey stones, catching what was left of the November light in places where the canyon walls opened up.

"I worked this road," Tommy said. Quiet. Almost to himself. "Summer of oh-nine. Army Corps sent a team to assess the Marble dam for decommission. I was the junior guy. Drove this road every morning for three months."

"They decommission it?" Joel asked.

"Nah. Owner fought it. Private dam, built in the thirties. Small operation. Sixty feet of head, one Francis turbine, enough juice for maybe fifty houses. Owner was some rancher who'd inherited it from his granddad. Refused to let the government touch it."

"Is it still standing?"

Tommy looked out the window at the river. "It was concrete. Well-built. The kind of thing that outlasts the people who made it. No reason it shouldn't be."

They drove another four miles. The canyon widened into a valley. Meadows on both sides, brown and frost-killed, bordered by dense forest climbing the slopes. A wooden sign on the right shoulder read PRIVATE PROPERTY — NO TRESPASSING in faded red paint.

"That's it," Tommy said. "Turn right past the sign. Service road goes up the ridge."

Joel turned. The service road was two ruts in the dirt, overgrown with dead grass but still navigable. The truck bounced and rattled up the slope for a quarter mile, through a stand of bare aspens, past a rusted gate hanging open on one hinge.

Then the trees opened up.

Tommy said, "Stop."

Joel stopped.

---

They got out of the truck and stood at the edge of the ridge.

Below them, the valley opened wide. The Crystal River ran through the center, narrow and fast, cutting between slopes covered in dark spruce and pale aspen trunks. A quarter mile downstream, a concrete wall crossed the river.

The dam.

It wasn't big. Not Hoover Dam, not Grand Coulee. Not the massive structures Marcus had seen in documentaries where the camera had to pull back to fit the whole thing in the frame. This was a working dam. Practical. A curved wedge of grey concrete maybe forty feet wide and sixty feet tall, fitted into the canyon walls on both sides like it had grown there. Water spilled over the top in a thin, even sheet, catching the low November sun and throwing it back in pieces. Below the dam, a squat concrete building sat against the base of the canyon wall. The powerhouse. A steel penstock, thick as a man's torso, ran from the dam's face down to the building at a steep angle, bolted to the rock with brackets that had gone orange with rust but still held.

On the flat ground east of the powerhouse, three structures. A long maintenance building with a corrugated metal roof. A smaller building that might have been an office or a house. And a barn or garage, its doors closed, its roof sagging but intact.

The river below the dam ran clear and cold into the valley, flanked by rocky banks and meadows. Mountains on three sides. The only approach was the valley floor from the north and the service road they'd just driven.

Tommy walked to the edge of the ridge and looked down at the dam the way a mechanic looks at an engine. Assessing. Cataloging. Running through a checklist that lived in his head from three months of hands-on work and years of training before that.

"Penstock's intact," he said. "No visible cracks in the face. The spillway's flowing even, which means the reservoir behind it is full or close to it. Seasonal snowmelt keeps it topped off." He pointed at the powerhouse. "That building's where the turbine is. Francis-type, single unit. When I was here in oh-nine, it was producing about 200 kilowatts at full capacity. Enough for fifty houses with modern appliances. For a settlement running lights, basic tools, a pump or two? More than enough."

"What's it need?" Marcus asked.

"To get running? Depends on what shape the turbine's in. If the bearings are seized, we need to free them. If the generator windings are corroded, we need to clean or replace them. The intake gate might be stuck." Tommy shook his head. Not frustration. Calculation. "But this is a thirties-era dam. They built these things to last. Cast iron, heavy steel, concrete three feet thick. The electronics are the weak point, and there ain't much electronics in a dam this old. It's all mechanical."

Joel was looking at the buildings, not the dam. His eyes moved from the roofline of the maintenance building to the foundation of the smaller house to the garage doors. The contractor in him had woken up. A different kind of assessment than Tommy's, but just as automatic.

"Maintenance building's roof has some sag on the west end," Joel said. "Water damage, probably. But the walls are solid. Concrete block. That house needs windows. Boards'll do for now." He looked at Marcus. "Glass if you can get it."

"I can get it."

"Garage is the weakest structure. Pole barn construction. Tear it down before you'd fix it." Joel walked a few steps along the ridge, studying the terrain. The slopes. The tree line. The sightlines. "One road in from the north. Ridgeline on the south and east. River on the west. Put a gate at the valley entrance and clear the trees for a hundred yards on both sides, you'd have a defensible position."

"Joel's right," Tommy said. "Natural bottleneck. Couldn't pick a better spot if you drew it on paper."

Marcus checked the map display. A few red dots far downstream. Level 1 Runners, five of them, milling around what looked like an old fishing cabin a mile north. Nothing else. The area around the dam was clean.

The wind came up the valley and hit them on the ridge. Cold. Real cold. Not Texas cold, where you put on a jacket and complained about it. Colorado-in-November cold, where the air bit your ears and your knuckles and the tip of your nose and it didn't apologize. Marcus's jacket wasn't enough. None of their jackets were enough.

Sarah hadn't said anything.

She was standing a few feet to his left, at the edge of the ridge, looking down at the valley. The dam. The river. The mountains on three sides, their peaks white with early snow against a sky that couldn't decide between blue and grey. The wind blew her hair across her face and she didn't push it back.

She looked at the powerhouse and the maintenance building and the little house with its empty window frames. She looked at the meadow by the river where the grass was brown and flat and covered in frost. She looked at the ridge they were standing on and the road curling down into the valley.

"Home," she said.

Not to anyone in particular. Not a question or a hope or a wish. A fact. Stated the way you'd state your own name.

Nobody answered right away. Joel looked at his daughter. Tommy looked at the dam. Marcus looked at the ground between his boots because his eyes were doing something he didn't want anyone to see.

Home. The first time any of them had said that word since Austin. Since the night the world ended and they'd walked out of a burning city and spent six weeks covering eight hundred miles through a country that was eating itself alive.

It wasn't a home yet. It was a concrete dam and three beat-up buildings and a valley that would freeze solid in six weeks. It was a generator cached under tarps in a nameless town five hundred miles south. A pile of lumber and solar panels and seeds that they'd have to drive back for.

But Sarah had called it home. And that was enough to make it real.

## — Sarah —

The walk down to the dam took twenty minutes.

Tommy led them along a switchback trail cut into the ridge, overgrown but clear enough to follow. Whoever had maintained this place had done good work on the paths. Steps carved into the slope at the steep parts, flat stones set into the dirt, drainage channels to keep the rain from washing it out. All of it covered in dead grass and fallen leaves, but the bones were solid.

Sarah's hip ached on the downhill. It always did when the temperature dropped. The cold got into the old wound like it had a key, a deep, dull throb that she'd learned to walk through without showing it. Marcus would notice if she limped. He always noticed. So she didn't limp.

The maintenance building was bigger up close. Sixty feet by thirty, concrete block walls, metal roof rusted orange in patches but mostly intact. The doors were heavy steel, padlocked, but the lock was so corroded that Tommy broke it off with a rock.

Inside: a workshop. A real one. Workbenches along both walls, bolted to the floor, with pegboard backing where tools had once hung. The outlines were still visible, clean shapes in dusty wood where wrenches and hammers and socket sets had blocked the grime. A drill press in the corner. A band saw. Shelving units with parts bins, most of them empty or holding rusted hardware. A calendar on the wall from 2011, still turned to July. A woman in a sundress smiling next to a lake.

"This is a machine shop," Tommy said. He ran his hand along a workbench. His voice had a quality Sarah had never heard from him before. Not quite awe. Closer to the way people talked about places they used to go as kids. Recognition. "The rancher who owned this place maintained the turbine himself. Did his own machining."

Joel checked the roof from inside. A section of the corrugated metal had pulled away from the joists on the west end, letting in water. The floor under it was stained and buckled. The rest of the building was dry.

"This is livable," Joel said. "Tonight, even. Block that west section, get a fire going. It's better than anything we've slept in for a month."

The powerhouse was next. Tommy went in alone while the rest waited outside. He was in there for ten minutes. When he came out, he was smiling.

Sarah couldn't remember the last time she'd seen Tommy smile like that. Not a polite smile or a tired one. A real smile, the kind that started somewhere behind the eyes and just showed up on the face without being asked.

"Turbine's frozen," he said. "Bearings are seized from sitting dry. But the housing is intact. The generator looks clean inside. The control panel is basic. Manual gates, mechanical governors, nothing computerized." He looked at Marcus. "I can get this running. Give me the right tools and a week."

"What tools?" Marcus asked.

Tommy listed them the way Sarah's math teacher used to list homework assignments. Brisk, specific, no room for argument. Penetrating oil. A bearing puller. Pipe wrenches, the big ones. A torque wrench. Marine-grade grease. Wire brushes. A multimeter for the generator windings.

"How much?" Marcus asked. He had that look on his face, the one where his eyes went slightly unfocused and his head tilted two degrees to the left. Reading the invisible menu that nobody else could see.

"All of it? Hundred forty, give or take. Depends on the multimeter."

Marcus was quiet for a few seconds. Then: "Done. I'll have it for you within the hour."

"Within the hour," Tommy repeated.

"He does that," Sarah said. "You get used to it."

She left them talking about intake valves and went to the small building. The rancher's house.

Two rooms and a kitchen, with a stone fireplace and a bathroom that had no running water but still had a bathtub and a toilet and a mirror so dusty she could barely see herself in it. The windows were empty frames, glass broken long ago. But the walls were thick and the fireplace was intact and the chimney drew when Joel came in later and lit a test fire with dry leaves.

Sarah walked through the house. The main room had a wooden floor, warped in places but solid. The kitchen had a cast-iron stove, cold and dusty, with a pipe that went up through the ceiling. The back room was smaller. A bedroom. A closet with a broken door.

She looked at the bedroom window. It faced east.

In the morning, the sun would come through it and hit the opposite wall. She could see it already. The light warming the floor, falling across a space that would have a bed someday and a bookshelf that she'd ask Marcus to buy from his system. Maybe a rug. Maybe curtains, if anyone remembered how to sew.

An east-facing window. She'd asked Marcus for that weeks ago. Under a highway bridge, the night he'd told her she couldn't have children. She'd asked for three things: a room with an east-facing window, a bookshelf, and a dog.

Two out of three. The dog could wait.

"This room," she said. Nobody was in the house to hear her. She said it anyway.

## — Marcus —

They spent the rest of the afternoon on practical things.

Marcus and Joel cleared the five Runners at the fishing cabin downstream. Routine work. Joel didn't even draw the SIG for the last two. Just the knife and the steady patience of a man who'd done this enough times that the fear had been replaced by something quieter. Muscle memory, maybe. Or just habit.

**[Points: 388]**

Post-purchases. He'd bought four insulated jackets, two cold-weather sleeping bags, and Tommy's entire tool list on the walk down from the ridge. Three hundred and sixty points spent in fifteen minutes. The items appeared in the maintenance building in neat piles that Tommy was already sorting through when they got back from the cabin.

The map showed the area around the dam clean for two miles in every direction. Beyond that, a scattering of red dots to the north and east. Rural infected, probably from the small towns that dotted the valley upstream. They'd deal with them in the coming days. Good point farming. Safe enough with two people.

Joel walked the valley entrance and paced out where a gate would go. He found a natural narrowing where the road passed between two rock outcroppings. Twenty feet wide. Loose stone on both sides that could be stacked or reinforced.

"Two days' work with the right materials," Joel said. "Logs, fence wire, some sheet metal for backing."

"I'll add it to the list."

Joel nodded. Then he looked around the valley. The dam. The buildings. The mountains going blue-grey as the afternoon light faded.

"This is it," he said. "Your big plan. All those points. All those miles."

"This is it."

"A busted dam and three sheds in the middle of nowhere."

"In six months, it won't look like this."

Joel pulled his new jacket tighter. Marcus had guessed his size wrong and it was slightly too big. Joel hadn't complained. Joel didn't complain about things like fit.

"No," Joel said. "I reckon it won't."

He turned and walked back toward the maintenance building where Tommy's voice echoed off the walls as he talked to himself about valve seats and runner blade clearances.

...

Night came early in the valley. The sun dropped behind the western ridge at four-thirty and the temperature fell with it. By five, the frost was back. By six, their breath hung in the air inside the maintenance building even with a fire burning in a steel drum that Tommy had rigged near the west end.

They ate MREs that Marcus bought from the Mall. Beef stew for Joel. Chicken and rice for Tommy. Cheese tortellini for Sarah, who ate the whole thing and then the cracker packet and then looked at Marcus until he gave her his pound cake.

"You don't like pound cake," she said.

"I like pound cake."

"You never eat it."

"I'm eating it right now." He was not eating it. She took it.

Tommy spread the atlas on a workbench and started drawing. Not a blueprint. More like a sketch, quick lines showing the layout of the valley, the dam, the buildings, the approaches. He marked water flow, elevation lines, tree cover. A plan taking shape under a pencil and a flashlight.

"Phase one," Tommy said, tapping the powerhouse. "Get the turbine running. That gives us electricity. Lights. Heat if we wire up space heaters. A pump for running water."

"Phase two," Marcus said. "Retrieve the settlement kit from the cache in New Mexico. The generator's a backup power source. The lumber and tools let us start building properly. The solar panels supplement the dam."

"That's a thousand-mile round trip," Joel said.

"In the truck, three days each way. Maybe four if the roads are bad."

"Who goes?"

"You and me. Tommy stays to work on the turbine. Sarah stays with Tommy."

Sarah opened her mouth. Marcus held up a hand.

"You're staying because Tommy needs help with the turbine and because somebody has to be here with a rifle if anything comes up the valley."

Sarah considered this. She closed her mouth. Not agreement, exactly. More like a decision to argue about it later when she had better leverage.

"Phase three," Tommy continued. "Fortify. Gate at the valley entrance. Clear the surrounding area. Establish patrol routes. Set up the solar panels and water filtration." He looked up from the atlas. "After that, we wait."

"Wait for what?" Joel asked.

"People." Tommy capped the pencil. "Once we've got lights and water and walls, people will come. They always do. Somebody builds something worth having, and other people show up wanting in."

The fire popped in the steel drum. Outside, the river ran over the dam and through the valley, steady and constant, a sound that had been there for a thousand years and would be there for a thousand more.

Marcus pulled up the system display.

**[Quest Completed: Survive — Reach a defensible, sustainable location]**
**[Reward: +100 points]**
**[Settlement Management interface unlocked]**

**[Points: 488]**

A new interface opened. Not the Mall. Something different. A management screen with tabs labeled Population, Resources, Infrastructure, Defense, Morale. All blank except Infrastructure, which showed one entry:

**[Marble Dam — Status: Inactive (Turbine Seized)]**
**[Population: 4]**
**[Structures: 3 (Maintenance Shop, Residence, Garage — all damaged)]**

**[New Quest: Foundation]**
**[Restore power to Marble Dam within 14 days]**
**[Reward: +200 points, Tier 3 Infrastructure items unlocked]**

Fourteen days. Tommy said he needed a week. The system was being generous, which made Marcus suspicious, but he'd take it.

He closed the display and looked around the maintenance building.

Tommy was sketching on the atlas, muttering about flow rates. Joel was cleaning the SIG by the fire, the nightly ritual of disassembly and reassembly that he performed whether he'd fired it or not. Sarah was in her sleeping bag, journal open, pen moving.

Four people. A valley. A dam. Three buildings that needed more work than they could do in a month.

And behind them, eight hundred miles of road that ran all the way back to a city that didn't exist anymore.

Marcus leaned against the concrete wall. The cold came through his jacket and settled into his back. The fire was warm on his face. He sat between the two temperatures and thought about what came next.

Not survival. Survival was the old problem, the one they'd been solving since Outbreak Night. Run, fight, eat, sleep, keep moving. The new problem was different.

Building.

A home. Not just a place to sleep, but a place people would find and choose to stay. A place where Sarah could have her east-facing window and her bookshelf. Where Joel could build things with his hands instead of destroying them. Where Tommy could run a dam the way he'd been trained to, not just survive but make something that worked.

Where Marcus could stop running and start spending points on things that lasted longer than a day.

Forty-two days since the world ended. Eight hundred and twelve miles. Over a hundred infected killed. Three humans who'd chosen violence and paid for it. One man named Gerald who they couldn't save. A twelve-year-old girl who'd walked through all of it without breaking. A contractor who fought like a machine and loved like a man afraid of losing everything again. A former soldier who could look at a sixty-year-old dam and see a future in it.

And Marcus. A guy from somewhere else entirely, who'd cried at a death scene on a screen and woken up with the chance to stop it from happening.

The fire burned low. The wind pressed against the metal walls and whistled through the gap on the west end where the roof had pulled away. Somewhere outside, the river kept running, and the dam held it back, and the valley waited.

Tomorrow, they'd start building.
