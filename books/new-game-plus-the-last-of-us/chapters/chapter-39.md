# Chapter 39: Found

## — Marcus —

September 2020. Crystal Falls, Colorado.

Three months married, and Marcus still reached for the wrong side of the bed when he woke up.

He'd slept alone for seven years. The habit didn't care about vows or string lights or the fact that Sarah's pillow smelled like the rosemary soap Dr. Reyes made in batches of twelve. His hand would go left, find empty mattress, and his brain would spend half a second panicking before catching up. She was on his right. She'd always been on his right. He was just slow.

Sarah was already gone. Her side of the bed was cool, which meant she'd been up for at least thirty minutes. Clipboard time. The morning ritual of a woman who managed three hundred and thirty-eight people the way other people managed grocery lists. Methodically, thoroughly, and with the quiet certainty that if she didn't do it, nobody would do it right.

Captain was also gone. The border collie had transferred his loyalty to whoever woke up first, which was always Sarah. Marcus had accepted his place in the household hierarchy. Sarah, Captain, Marcus. In that order.

He got dressed. Boots, cargo pants, a flannel that had been washed so many times the plaid was more of a suggestion than a pattern. Knife on his belt. The hunting knife from Day 1. He'd replaced the handle twice and the sheath three times, but the blade was the same one that had killed his first Runner on a stranger's lawn in Austin seven years ago.

The system map was clean. Green across the valley, nothing within fifteen miles. A calm morning. The kind where you could almost forget that the world had ended.

Almost.

He went downstairs and found Joel at the kitchen table, eating oatmeal and reading a dog-eared paperback that someone had traded from Basalt Station. Joel read the same four books in rotation because that was all they had and Joel didn't complain about things like book selection. He complained about the shower pressure and the goats and Carson's inability to hang a door straight. Books, he accepted as they came.

"Mornin'," Joel said.

"Morning. Sarah?"

"Left twenty minutes ago. Said somethin' about the south greenhouse drainage and a word I won't repeat."

Marcus poured himself chicory from the pot on the stove. Still terrible. Always terrible. He drank it anyway.

"I'm heading north today," Marcus said. "Keiko's patrol flagged movement near the old campground past Crystal River Outfitters. Probably just Runners, but the density looked off on the map."

Joel looked up from his book. "How off?"

"Eight contacts in a cluster. Runners don't cluster like that unless there's a nest forming."

"Want company?"

"I'll be fine. It's a standard clear."

Joel nodded and went back to his oatmeal. He didn't argue about Marcus going solo anymore. Not since Tier 4. The argument had stopped being worth having when the person you were arguing with could hear a pin drop from a quarter mile and heal from a gunshot wound in an afternoon. Joel picked his battles. Always had.

Marcus finished his chicory, filled his canteen, and headed for the north gate.

---

The campground was six miles north, up the Crystal River valley where the road turned to gravel and the pines got thick enough to block the sky. Marcus had cleared this area twice in the last year. Both times it had been small groups. Stragglers moving through, drawn by the river or the remnants of the campground's structures.

This was different.

He heard them before the map updated. Eight heartbeats, erratic, the wet syncopation that meant fungal infection had damaged the cardiac tissue. Runners. All of them, based on the pace. No Stalker rhythm, no Clicker echo-clicking. Just eight bodies shambling through pine needles in the morning cold.

He moved through the trees. Quiet, efficient. Seven years of this. The fear was gone, replaced by something closer to routine. Not comfortable. You never got comfortable. But familiar. The way a plumber was familiar with sewage. You did the job because someone had to.

The first three were in the campground's parking lot. Standing in the open, heads twitching, mouths working at nothing. A man in a ranger uniform so degraded by fungal growth that the badge was barely visible. A woman in hiking boots. A teenager in a college sweatshirt that said "CU Boulder" across the chest in faded gold letters. Young. Maybe eighteen when it happened. Seven years of infection had left the face unrecognizable, but the sweatshirt told a story. A kid heading to college who never got there.

Marcus killed them clean. Knife work, close and fast. Three down in eight seconds.

He moved deeper into the campground. Past the restroom building with its collapsed roof. Past the fire rings choked with weeds. The remaining five contacts were in the main lodge, a log building that had been the campground's check-in office and small store. The door was open. One of them stood in the doorway, swaying. A woman, mid-thirties, with fungal plates across most of her face and jaw.

He took her down from behind. Moved through the door. Dim light from cracked windows. The store's shelves had been picked clean years ago, nothing left but dust and mouse droppings. Four more Runners were in the back room, clustered near the storage area. He killed them methodically. The last one lunged and he sidestepped it, drove the knife through the base of the skull, and pulled it free in one motion.

Eight dead. Clean sweep. No injuries, no problems, no surprises.

Then he heard the ninth heartbeat.

Not infected. The rhythm was wrong. Too fast, too regular, too alive. A human heartbeat, small and rapid, the kind of cadence that meant either a child or someone in severe distress.

Coming from under the storage room floor.

Marcus looked down. Old pine planks, warped and gapped. A crawl space underneath, maybe two feet of clearance. The kind of space someone would hide in if they were small enough to fit and scared enough to try.

He knelt. Put one hand flat on the floor. Listened.

Breathing. Shallow, hitching. The kind of breathing that came from crying so long your lungs forgot what normal felt like.

"Hey," Marcus said. Quiet. Not the voice he used for council meetings or militia drills. The voice he'd used on a highway in Austin seven years ago, kneeling over a twelve-year-old girl with a bullet wound, telling her it was going to be okay. "Hey. I'm not going to hurt you. The monsters are gone. All of them."

The breathing changed. Faster. Not calmer. The opposite. Someone who'd been hiding for God knows how long and had just heard a voice that wasn't growling or clicking and didn't know what to do with that information.

Marcus pulled back one of the loose floorboards. Then another. The gap was wide enough to see into the crawl space.

A kid. A boy, maybe five or six. Brown hair matted with dirt and pine needles. Wearing a T-shirt two sizes too big, stained and torn. Shorts. No shoes. His knees were pulled to his chest and his arms were wrapped around them and his eyes were open and looking at Marcus with the kind of fear that went past crying into something still and hollow.

There was a dead woman in the crawl space with him. An adult, mid-thirties, lying on her side with her arm extended toward the boy. She'd been dead for a while. Days, maybe a week. Not infected. No fungal growth. No bite marks. Just dead. Dehydration, maybe. Starvation. Exposure. The causes that killed quietly, without the drama of teeth and spores.

She'd put the boy under the floor. Hidden him from the Runners above. And then she'd crawled in after him, and she'd died there, with her hand reaching for him.

Marcus closed his eyes. Opened them.

"My name is Marcus," he said. "I live in a town about six miles south of here. There are a lot of people there. We have food and water and beds. I'm going to get you out of here and take you somewhere safe. Okay?"

The boy didn't move. Didn't blink. The heartbeat stayed at 140 beats per minute. A hummingbird pace.

Marcus reached down slowly. Not toward the boy. Toward the woman. He checked for a pulse he already knew wasn't there. Confirmed. Dead. He didn't move the body.

"Was this your mom?" he asked.

Nothing. The hollow stare.

Marcus sat back on his heels. He looked around the storage room. Eight dead Runners on the floor. Blood on the walls and the pine planks. The smell of fungal decay and old violence. Not a place for a child. Not a place for anyone.

He'd killed infected in rooms like this a hundred times. He'd cleared nests and earned points and bought supplies and built a town. The system made it clean. Numbers going up, notifications popping, a gamified layer over the ugliness of what the world had become.

But there was no notification for a dead mother and a child who'd stopped crying because crying hadn't helped.

He pulled off his flannel. Laid it on the floor next to the crawl space opening. Then he sat cross-legged on the pine planks, three feet from the gap, and waited.

Five minutes. Ten. The boy's heartbeat dropped from 140 to 120. Still scared. Still frozen. But the man sitting on the floor wasn't moving, wasn't reaching, wasn't doing anything threatening. Just sitting.

At fourteen minutes, the boy moved. One hand uncurled from his knees and touched the edge of the crawl space opening. Feeling the air. Testing.

"Take your time," Marcus said.

The boy crawled out. Slowly, on hands and knees, through the gap. He stopped at Marcus's flannel, looked at it, looked at Marcus. Then he sat on it. Not because it was comfortable. Because it was something that wasn't dirt or blood or the crawl space.

He was thin. Too thin. His arms were sticks. His cheeks were sunken in a way that meant days without food. A scrape on his left knee had scabbed over and reopened and scabbed again. He smelled like earth and sweat and the sour tang of a body running on nothing.

Marcus reached into his pocket. A granola bar he'd grabbed from the kitchen before leaving. Mall-purchased, wrapped in foil. He unwrapped it slowly, broke it in half, and set one half on the flannel next to the boy.

The boy looked at the food. Looked at Marcus. Picked it up with both hands and ate it so fast he choked, coughing, eyes watering. Marcus handed him the canteen. The boy drank until Marcus gently pulled it back.

"Slow down," Marcus said. "There's more. There's always more."

The boy finished the other half of the granola bar. Then he sat on the flannel, looking at his own hands, and said the first word Marcus had heard from him.

"Mama."

Marcus looked at the crawl space. The body lying in the dark, arm still reaching.

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry."

He bought a blanket from the Mall. The boy didn't flinch when it appeared. He was past flinching. Marcus wrapped it around the thin shoulders and picked him up. The boy weighed nothing. Less than the supplies Marcus routinely carried on clearing runs. A child-shaped absence where weight should have been.

He carried the boy out of the lodge, past the dead Runners in the parking lot, and into the morning sun. The boy squinted and buried his face against Marcus's shoulder. Too much light after too long in the dark.

Marcus walked south. Six miles. The boy fell asleep after the first two.

## — Sarah —

Sarah was in the south greenhouse arguing with a drainage pipe when she heard the gate call. Not the alarm. The "someone's coming in" call, two short whistles from Donna's watch. Friendly.

She wiped her hands on her jeans and walked out. The drainage pipe could wait. The drainage pipe had been waiting for a week. It would survive another hour.

Marcus was coming through the north gate. He was carrying something. Not supplies, not scavenged material. Something wrapped in a blanket, held against his chest, small and still.

Sarah stopped walking.

She'd seen Marcus come back from clearing runs hundreds of times. Dirty, tired, sometimes bleeding. He always walked the same way. Steady stride, shoulders level, knife wiped clean and re-sheathed. The walk of a man coming home from work.

He wasn't walking like that. He was walking slowly. Carefully. The way you walk when you're carrying something that might break.

She met him at the courtyard. Helen was there too, clipboard at her side, watching with the evaluating look she used for anything that deviated from schedule.

"What happened?" Sarah asked.

Marcus stopped. The blanket moved. A small head, brown hair, dirt-streaked, lifted from his shoulder and looked at Sarah. A child. A boy, five or six, with enormous dark eyes and the gaunt, bruised face of someone who'd gone too long without enough of anything.

"Found him at the campground," Marcus said. His voice was steady. His hands were not. Sarah could see the fine tremor in his arms. Not from the weight. From something else. "He was under the floorboards. His mother was with him. She didn't make it."

Sarah looked at the boy. The boy looked at her. The hollow stare of a kid who'd run out of reactions days ago and was just absorbing whatever happened next without processing any of it.

Sarah reached out and touched the boy's hand. Small, cold, dirty. His fingers curled around hers. Not a grip. A reflex. The automatic response of a child who'd lost the only hand he'd been holding and was reaching for the next one.

"Okay," Sarah said. "Let's get him inside."

She didn't ask questions. Not yet. The questions could wait. What couldn't wait was food, water, warmth, and the particular kind of quiet that a child needed when the world had taken everything and the only thing left was strangers being kind.

They brought him to the residence kitchen. Sarah heated soup from the morning batch. Vegetable, nothing heavy. Dr. Reyes had taught her that starving people couldn't handle rich food. You started with broth and worked your way up.

The boy sat at the table. Joel's chair, specifically, because it was the closest. He ate the soup with both hands on the bowl, drinking from the edge because he either didn't know how to use a spoon or had forgotten. Sarah sat across from him and watched and said nothing, because saying nothing was sometimes the most useful thing you could do.

Marcus stood in the doorway. He hadn't sat down. He hadn't taken off his boots. He was watching the boy the way he watched the system map when it showed something he didn't understand.

"He said one word," Marcus told her. "Mama. That's it."

Sarah nodded. "He'll talk when he's ready."

"There were eight Runners in the lodge. Right above where he was hiding."

"How long?"

"The mother's been dead for days. He was under the floor the whole time."

Sarah closed her eyes for a second. She opened them and looked at the boy. He'd stopped drinking the soup and was staring at the kitchen wall. At a specific spot on the wall. Sarah followed his gaze. Mia's crayon drawing of the dam, the one she'd pinned there five years ago. Faded now, the colors washed out, but still there. A child's drawing in a world that had almost run out of children.

The boy looked at the drawing. Then he looked at Sarah. Then back at the drawing.

Sarah got up, walked to the counter, and came back with a pencil and the blank side of a supply requisition form.

She put them in front of the boy.

He picked up the pencil. Held it in a fist grip, the way kids did before anyone taught them different. And he drew a line. Then another. Then a circle. Not a picture of anything recognizable. Just marks on paper. The first voluntary action he'd taken since Marcus found him.

Sarah pulled her chair around the table and sat beside him. Not touching. Just close. Present.

"You're safe here," she said.

---

Dr. Reyes came within the hour. Examined the boy gently, talking to him in the soft, continuous narration she used for patients who were scared. Describing everything she was doing before she did it. "I'm going to listen to your chest now. This is a stethoscope. It's cold, sorry."

Malnourished but not critically. Dehydrated. The scrape on his knee was infected, but minor. No bites. No fungal exposure. Physically, he'd survive.

The rest would take longer.

"He's around five," Dr. Reyes told Marcus and Sarah in the hallway. "Hard to tell without dental records. He's been surviving on very little for at least a week, probably longer. The mother kept him alive as long as she could."

"Any idea where they came from?" Marcus asked.

"His shoes are gone, but the clothes aren't local. That T-shirt has a Kansas City zoo logo. They traveled a long way."

Marcus and Sarah stood in the hallway after Dr. Reyes left. The boy was asleep in the room behind them, wrapped in the blanket Marcus had bought from the Mall, with Captain curled at the foot of the cot. The dog had appeared from somewhere, sniffed the boy once, and settled in like he'd been assigned a post.

"We can find a family for him," Marcus said. "The Haywoods would take him. Raymond's offered before to take in any kids that needed a home."

Sarah leaned against the wall. She looked at the closed door. Looked at Marcus.

"Or we keep him," she said.

It wasn't a question. Not exactly. More like a sentence she'd been building since the moment she'd seen the boy's fingers curl around hers in the courtyard.

Marcus looked at her. The woman he'd carried out of Austin. The girl who'd asked for an east-facing window and a bookshelf and a dog. Who'd been told at thirteen that she couldn't have children and had handled it with a composure that shouldn't have been possible. Who'd spent seven years building a town and managing three hundred people and being the heart of something bigger than either of them, and who was now standing in a hallway looking at a closed door with an expression Marcus had never seen on her face before.

Not sadness. Not pity. Something fiercer than both. The look of someone who'd found the answer to a question she'd stopped asking.

"Yeah," Marcus said. "Or we keep him."

Sarah nodded. Once. The nod she used for things that were decided.

"We should ask Joel," she said.

"We should."

"He'll say yes."

"I know."

"Then why ask?"

"Because it's the right thing to do. He's family. Family gets a vote."

Sarah pushed off the wall. Reached for Marcus's hand. Squeezed it once.

"Go find my dad," she said. "I'll stay with the boy."

## — Joel —

Joel was in the workshop, sharpening an axe that Carson had dulled trying to split knotted pine, when Marcus walked in.

Joel could read Marcus by now. Seven years. You learned a man's tells in seven years whether you wanted to or not. Marcus's tells were subtle because the system had given him control over his body that most people didn't have. But the eyes were still honest. The eyes were always honest.

Marcus had been crying. Not recently, and not where anyone could see. But the redness was there if you knew where to look, and Joel knew where to look because Joel had spent twenty years learning what grief looked like on faces, including his own.

"What happened?" Joel said. He set the axe down.

Marcus told him. The campground. The Runners. The boy under the floor. The dead mother with her arm still reaching.

Joel listened. He didn't interrupt. He looked at the workbench while Marcus talked, his hands resting on the wood, and he thought about a night in Austin when he'd carried his own daughter and screamed at God and the soldiers and anyone who could hear him. A different night. A different child. The same desperate math of someone trying to keep a kid alive in a world that didn't care about kids.

"How old?" Joel asked.

"Dr. Reyes says about five."

"He talk?"

"One word. His mother."

Joel picked up the axe again. Turned it over. Not sharpening. Just holding it. Something in his hands while his mind worked.

"Sarah wants to keep him," Joel said. Not a guess.

"Yeah."

"And you?"

Marcus looked at him. "I'm asking you."

Joel almost laughed. Not quite. The corner of his mouth moved, and his eyes went somewhere else for a second, somewhere twenty years and eight hundred miles away, and then he was back.

"You know what Sarah's first word was?" Joel said. "Her actual first word. Not 'dada' or 'mama.' She pointed at the TV and said 'dog.' I don't even know what was on. Could've been anything. She just decided the first thing worth naming in the entire world was a dog."

He set the axe down again.

"She's been ready for this since the day you told her she couldn't have kids," Joel said. "She never said it. But I know my daughter. She's been waitin' for a kid who needed her as much as she needed one."

"Joel."

"Bring him home, son. You don't need my vote for that."

Marcus stood in the workshop doorway. Joel had called him "son" exactly twice before. Once on a back porch in Texas, and once at the wedding. Three times now.

"Thank you," Marcus said.

Joel picked up the axe and the whetstone. "Tell Sarah I'll build a bed frame by tomorrow. Small one. Kid's gonna need a room."

"He can have the room next to ours."

"The one with the east window?"

"That's ours. The one beside it. North-facing."

"North-facing gets cold in winter. I'll insulate the wall."

Marcus nodded. Turned to leave.

"Marcus."

He stopped.

"The mother," Joel said. "Under the floor. Someone should go back and bury her proper."

"I'll go tomorrow. After he's settled."

"I'll come with you."

Joel picked up the whetstone and started sharpening. The sound of stone on steel filled the workshop, steady and rhythmic, the sound of a man preparing for ordinary work in a world where ordinary work was the closest thing to prayer anyone had left.

## — Sarah —

The boy woke up at six in the evening. Sarah was sitting in the chair beside the cot, reading the supply ledger by the last of the daylight through the window. Captain lifted his head from the foot of the bed, tail thumping once against the blanket.

The boy looked at Sarah. At the room. At the dog. At Sarah again.

"Hi," Sarah said.

He didn't answer. But he didn't look away, either. Progress.

"Are you hungry?"

A small nod. The first response to a direct question.

Sarah went to the kitchen and came back with bread and butter and a cup of water. She put them on the side table and sat back down. The boy ate slowly this time. Chewing. Looking at the bread like he was trying to figure out what it was made of.

Captain inched up the bed until his chin rested on the boy's knee. The boy looked at the dog. Looked at Sarah.

"That's Captain," Sarah said. "He likes everybody, but he'll pretend he only likes you. Don't let it go to his head."

The boy's hand moved. Slowly, carefully, like he was reaching for something that might disappear. His fingers touched Captain's ear. The dog leaned into it. Tail going.

The boy's face changed. Not a smile. Not yet. But something loosened behind the eyes. A crack in the wall he'd built around himself in a crawl space under a lodge in the mountains, lying next to his mother's body, listening to monsters walk on the floor above his head.

A crack was enough. You didn't need the whole wall to come down at once. You just needed the crack. The rest came with time, and food, and a dog who didn't care about walls.

Marcus came in. He stood in the doorway the same way he had that morning, watching. Sarah looked at him. He looked at her. The conversation happened without words, the way it did when two people had spent seven years learning each other's silences.

"Joel's building a bed frame," Marcus said. "Tomorrow."

"I know," Sarah said. "I heard the saw start ten minutes ago. He's not waiting until tomorrow."

Marcus smiled. Sarah smiled.

The boy petted the dog. Captain's tail thumped against the blanket.

Outside, the last light was going from the valley. The turbine hummed. The creek ran. Somewhere across the compound, Joel's saw cut through pine, the sound carrying in the evening air, steady and sure. Building something small for someone who needed it. The way he'd always built things. Not because anyone asked. Because he saw what was needed and his hands knew what to do.
