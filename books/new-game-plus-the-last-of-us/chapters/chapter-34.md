# Chapter 34: Harvest

## — Sarah —

April 26, 2015. Crystal Falls, Colorado.

The chickens were not cooperating.

Sarah stood in the yard behind the dining hall with a clipboard in one hand and a bag of feed in the other, watching three Rhode Island Reds completely ignore the temporary fence she'd spent twenty minutes building out of scrap wire and garden stakes. One was already on the other side, pecking at something in the gravel. The other two were considering their options.

"Donna, the chickens are out again."

Donna Pruitt looked up from the folding tables she was hauling out of the maintenance building. "They're your chickens."

"They're the community's chickens."

"They're in your section of the schedule. Says so right there on the board." Donna pointed with her chin toward the bulletin board on the dining hall wall. The board that Sarah had reorganized twice this week. The board that was currently her third-favorite possession after her journal and the ballpoint pen that still worked.

Sarah dropped the feed bag, caught the nearest hen with both hands, and deposited it back inside the fence. It looked at her with the flat, judgmental stare that chickens had perfected over millions of years of evolution and immediately walked back through the gap in the wire.

"Fine," Sarah said. "You win."

She left the chickens to their rebellion and went back to the clipboard. The clipboard was the real problem. Not because the list was long. It was long. But because the list kept getting longer every time someone walked past her with a new idea.

Lena wanted a second serving table for the pies. Raymond wanted a spot for the Denver group to set up something he was calling "the grill station," which apparently involved a fifty-five-gallon drum, some rebar, and the kind of confidence that came from a man who'd been a high school principal and was used to making things happen by deadline. Dr. Reyes wanted a medical tent in case anyone ate something that disagreed with them, which Sarah thought was reasonable until she realized it meant moving the music area twenty feet west, which put it directly under the power line that Tommy had strung between the dining hall and the greenhouse.

A hundred and forty-eight people. One festival. Eighteen items on the clipboard. Sarah was starting to understand why event planners in the old world drank.

She crossed the yard toward the greenhouse. The morning sun was warm on her face. Late April in the Rockies. The snow was gone below nine thousand feet and the valley was green in a way that still surprised her, even after a full year of watching seasons happen without anyone mowing lawns or trimming hedges. Wildflowers in the meadow south of the dam. Yellow and purple. Mia Haywood had picked a fistful of them yesterday and presented them to Sarah like a tiny ambassador offering tribute.

The greenhouse door was propped open with a brick. Inside, Lena was surrounded by produce.

Not a lot of produce, by old-world standards. But by apocalypse standards, it was absurd. Three bushels of potatoes. Two crates of green beans. A pile of carrots that looked like they'd been pulled from the ground that morning because they had. Tomatoes, small and misshapen but red and real. Squash. Garlic. Onions. A modest pyramid of corn ears stacked on a table near the door.

Lena had dirt on her hands, her jeans, her forehead, and somehow her left ear. She was sorting potatoes by size with the focus of a woman performing surgery.

"These are the small ones for roasting," Lena said without looking up. "Those are the big ones for mashing. Don't mix them."

"I won't mix them."

"Barbara mixed them last week. Took me an hour to re-sort."

"I won't mix them, Lena."

Lena looked up. Smiled. The smile of a woman who'd spent fourteen months turning rocky Colorado soil into something that grew food, and was about to see a hundred and forty-eight people eat the results.

"We did it," Lena said.

"We did it," Sarah agreed. She wrote on the clipboard: potatoes sorted, beans ready, corn needs husking — assign to Mateo and Ava.

The harvest festival had been Sarah's idea. She'd pitched it at the council meeting three days ago, the morning after Marcus, Keiko, and Dale had come back from the Aspen run with bandaged arms and a story about acid-spitting infected that nobody wanted to hear twice.

Morale was bad. Not crisis-bad. Not we're-going-to-fall-apart bad. But the kind of bad where people ate dinner without talking much and went to bed early and didn't volunteer for extra shifts. The Denver refugees were still sleeping on dining hall floors and borrowed mattresses. The original residents were sharing supplies they'd earned with strangers they hadn't invited. Everyone was tired and nobody was saying it out loud.

Sarah knew what that looked like. She'd watched it before, twice, in the first year. The solution was the same both times. You couldn't fix exhaustion with a speech. You couldn't fix resentment with a schedule. You had to give people a reason to be in the same room together that wasn't about work or survival or watching each other's backs.

You had to throw a party.

Joel had voted against it. "We don't have the calories for a party."

Marcus had voted for it. "We don't have the morale for not having one."

Tommy had voted for it. "I'll tap the honey mead."

The vote had been five to two, with Joel and Helen dissenting on security grounds. Joel had then spent ten minutes explaining why gathering the entire community in one location was a tactical liability, and Helen had backed him up with a perimeter coverage analysis that used the word "vulnerability" seven times.

Sarah had listened. Nodded. Written it all down. Then said: "Joel, when was the last time you heard someone laugh?"

He hadn't answered. Which was the answer.

## — Marcus —

The bandage on his jaw itched.

Marcus stood in front of the small mirror in his quarters and peeled back the edge. The skin underneath was pink and shiny. New skin. The system's passive healing had been working on the acid burns for four days now, and what had started as raw, weeping chemical damage was closing up into something that looked like a bad sunburn.

His forearms were worse. The burns there had been deeper, the acid exposure longer. The new skin was tight when he flexed his hands. It would scar. Not badly, but enough that people would notice.

He put the bandage back. Left the forearms wrapped. Not because they needed covering. Because a hundred and forty-eight people were about to gather for a party, and showing up with visible acid burns invited questions he didn't feel like answering today.

Keiko's hand was healing too. Dr. Reyes had called it a second-degree chemical burn. Blisters that had drained, raw skin underneath, a compression wrap that Keiko wore like a badge. She'd been back on patrol two days after they'd returned. Joel hadn't argued. That told Marcus everything about how Joel felt about Keiko Tanaka.

Marcus checked the Mall out of habit. 1,859 points. He'd spent nothing since the burn kits. Sarah's festival request list had included items that could have come from the system. Decorations. Tablecloths. Music speakers. He'd said no to all of them. The point of the festival was the community doing something together with what they had. If he pulled a sound system out of thin air, it defeated the purpose.

Sarah hadn't argued. She'd just looked at him with the look that said she'd already figured that out and was waiting for him to catch up.

He pulled on a clean shirt. Rolled the sleeves down over the bandages. Walked outside.

The compound was louder than he'd ever heard it.

## — Sarah —

By noon, Crystal Falls looked different.

Not structurally. The dam was the same concrete and steel. The cabins were the same rough pine. The dining hall was the same cinder block building with the same bulletin board and the same crack in the west wall that Carson kept promising to patch.

But someone had strung a line of small flags between the dining hall and the maintenance building. Old fabric, cut into triangles. Reds and yellows and blues. Donna's work. Frank Garvey had built a raised platform out of scrap lumber near the fire pit. Three feet high, eight feet square. A stage. It wasn't level. Marcus would have noticed that. Sarah noticed it too but decided not to care.

Raymond Osei's grill station was operational. The fifty-five-gallon drum had been cut in half lengthwise and propped on a frame of welded rebar. Charcoal. Actual charcoal, made from hardwood by Gene Pruitt in a pit behind the greenhouse over the last week. The smoke smelled like summer. Like the Fourth of July. Like a world where people cooked outside because they wanted to, not because the kitchen was rationed.

Raymond stood over the grill with a pair of tongs and the expression of a man who'd found his purpose. Two of the Denver men Sarah didn't know well yet were next to him, turning ears of corn and arguing about timing.

"Three minutes per side," Raymond said.

"Four," said the shorter one.

"Three. You turn it at three or you get char."

"Char is flavor."

"Char is carbon."

Sarah walked past them and checked the tables. Six folding tables in a row, covered with... not tablecloths. Sheets. Bedsheets that Helen had pulled from the supply closet and washed twice. White, mostly. One had flowers. Nobody had commented on the flowers.

The food was coming out. Lena's potatoes, roasted in the big cast-iron pans that Frank had found in a restaurant in Marble six months ago. Green beans with garlic. Cornbread. Tommy's honey from the four hives he'd set up behind the generator building in January. The mead, in mason jars, sealed and waiting. And three pies. Apple, from dried apples that Lena had been hoarding since October. The crusts were uneven and the filling was more brown than golden, but they were pies. Real pies. Lena had cried when they came out of the oven and then denied it.

Sarah counted heads as people arrived. She couldn't help it. Counting was how she managed things. Fourteen. Thirty. Fifty. The original residents came first, gravitating to their usual spots near the dining hall entrance. The Denver group came slower, bunched together, uncertain about where to stand.

She watched Raymond notice this. He left the grill, walked to the Denver cluster, and pointed them toward the tables. "Sit anywhere. If someone tells you that's their seat, tell them Raymond said to move."

Nobody told anyone to move. People sat down. The tables filled. Kids ran between the legs of adults. Mia Haywood was wearing a crown made of wildflowers that was falling apart. Owen, Jessica Okafor's baby, was asleep in a carrier on Patricia Delgado's chest. Patricia was eight months pregnant now and she had the particular walk of a woman carrying a lot of extra weight uphill.

The noise built. Not the noise of the dining hall at mealtime, which was functional. Chewing and passing and brief conversation. This was different. This was people talking to people they didn't usually talk to. Laughter that came from somewhere genuine.

Sarah stood by the greenhouse and watched and didn't write anything on her clipboard for the first time all day.

## — Joel —

Joel ate three pieces of cornbread and didn't complain about any of them.

This was significant. Joel Miller's relationship with food had been adversarial for twenty months. He ate what was available, commented on what was wrong with it, and moved on. He'd told Marcus once that the best meal he'd had since Austin was a can of peaches he'd found in a gas station bathroom in Paonia, and he'd said it like a man confessing something private.

The cornbread was good. Lena's cornmeal, Helen's recipe, Frank's cast-iron pan. It wasn't restaurant quality. It wasn't even pre-outbreak home quality. But it was warm and it had butter. Actual butter, from the milk that Carson's three dairy goats produced in quantities that were, according to Lena, "insulting."

"This ain't bad," Joel said.

Tommy, sitting across from him with a mason jar of mead, raised his eyebrows. "High praise."

"Don't push it."

Tommy grinned. He looked different today. Everyone did. It was the clothes, partly. People had put on their better shirts. The ones without patches. A few of the women had done something with their hair. One of the Denver women was wearing earrings. Earrings. In the apocalypse.

Joel noticed these things because noticing things was what he did. He noticed that Helen had positioned herself at the east end of the tables with a sightline to the valley road. He noticed that Dale Briggs was on the observation platform with binoculars, covering the south approach. He noticed that Keiko was eating left-handed, her wrapped right hand resting on the table, and that she'd positioned herself next to the path between the dining hall and the armory.

Security coverage at a community barbecue. Joel hadn't asked for it. He hadn't needed to. His people had just done it. That was the part he'd never say out loud. The part that mattered.

Frank Garvey climbed onto the platform with a guitar.

Joel looked at the guitar. Then at Frank. Then at the guitar again.

It was a beat-up acoustic. Nylon strings, two of them replaced with what looked like fishing line. The body had a crack running from the sound hole to the bridge that someone had filled with wood glue. Frank sat on a stool that was slightly too short for the stage and adjusted the tuning pegs.

"Found it in a house in Redstone last month," Tommy said, following Joel's gaze. "Frank's been fixing it up."

"Does he play?"

"We're about to find out."

Frank played.

He was not good. He was adequate. He knew three chords and he used them in the right order and his rhythm was close enough that you could follow along if you were generous. He played something that might have been "Take Me Home, Country Roads" if you squinted at it musically. The melody wandered. The lyrics came in fragments. He forgot the bridge and hummed through it.

Nobody cared.

People clapped along. Someone whistled. Mia danced in the grass in front of the stage, the flower crown sliding over one eye. Two of the Denver kids joined her. They didn't know each other's names yet. They didn't need to.

Joel watched Frank's hands on the fretboard. Wrong finger placement on the G chord. Too much pressure on the strings. The transition between C and G was choppy.

He could fix that. He could fix all of it. He hadn't played guitar in almost two years. Hadn't wanted to. The guitar had been a Sarah thing. Playing in the living room while she did homework. Learning new songs to surprise her. The muscle memory was still in his hands. He could feel it when he watched Frank struggle through the chord changes. His fingers twitched on the table.

Tommy noticed. Of course Tommy noticed.

"You should get up there," Tommy said.

"No."

"Come on, Joel. You're better than him."

"Everyone's better than him. That ain't the point."

"What is the point?"

Joel looked at the festival. At the tables. At the food. At the flags strung between the buildings. At Mia dancing. At Patricia with the baby on her chest, swaying to music that barely qualified as music. At Raymond and Gene arguing about charcoal. At Dr. Reyes sitting in a lawn chair with a plate on her lap and her eyes closed, face turned toward the sun.

At Sarah.

Sarah was standing by the greenhouse door with her clipboard tucked under her arm, watching everything she'd built happen in front of her. Fourteen years old. Running the civilian half of a community of a hundred and forty-eight people. Managing food and labor and housing and governance and now, apparently, event planning.

She looked happy. Not the Sarah version of happy, which was usually just a brief absence of worry. Actually happy. Smiling at something Ava said to her. The kind of smile that used the whole face.

Joel hadn't seen that smile since Austin.

"Ain't the point," he said again. Quieter.

Tommy left it alone.

Frank finished the song. Or stopped playing, which was functionally the same thing. The applause was enthusiastic and undeserved and exactly right.

Joel got up.

He didn't walk to the stage. He walked to Frank. Put a hand on his shoulder.

"Mind if I borrow that?"

Frank looked up. Looked at Joel. Looked at the guitar. Then handed it over with the relief of a man who knew his limitations.

Joel sat on the stool. The guitar was lighter than his old one. The fishing-line strings felt wrong under his fingers. The crack in the body made the resonance tinny, hollow.

He played the first chord and something in his chest that had been closed for a very long time opened about an inch.

He played "Wish You Were Here." Not the full thing. His voice was rough and he forgot a line in the second verse and substituted a hum. But the guitar work was clean. Years of muscle memory in his hands, laying down chords that rang out across the compound and made people stop talking.

The compound went quiet. Not silence. The quiet of people listening.

Joel didn't look at the crowd. He looked at the fretboard. At his own hands. At the crack in the guitar's body, filled with glue, still holding together.

## — Marcus —

Marcus found Sarah by the fire pit after Joel's second song.

The sun was getting low. The afternoon had burned through in the way good afternoons do. Without you noticing. People were still eating. The grill was down to embers. Someone had found a jar of pickled beets that Donna had been saving, and a small crowd had formed around the jar with opinions about beets.

Sarah was sitting on the stone wall around the fire pit, legs dangling, clipboard on the ground beside her. She had a plate with a piece of apple pie on it. The pie was untouched.

"You're not eating your pie," Marcus said.

"I'm looking at it."

"That's not how pie works."

"I'm savoring the anticipation. It's Lena's first pie. First real pie since Outbreak Day. This is a historic pie."

"It's pie."

"You have no soul." She picked up the fork. Took a bite. Closed her eyes.

"Good?" he asked.

"The crust is terrible. The filling is perfect. I don't know how that's possible."

Marcus sat on the wall next to her. His forearms ached under the bandages. The late afternoon sun was warm on the back of his neck. Joel was playing something slow and quiet on the stage, barely audible over the conversation. A few people were still dancing in the grass near the platform. Not real dancing. The shuffling, swaying kind of dancing that happened when people were full and tired and the light was gold.

"Morale check," Sarah said.

"You're always working."

"Morale check," she repeated.

Marcus looked around the compound. At the tables, where original residents and Denver refugees were mixed now. Not perfectly. There were still clusters. But Margaret Chen was sitting with Lena, and they were comparing something on a piece of paper. David Chen was talking to Carson, and both of them were pointing at the maintenance building roof, which probably meant they were planning something structural that Marcus would hear about at Monday's council meeting.

Raymond was sitting with Gene and Donna, all three of them laughing about something. Raymond had barbecue sauce on his collar. Gene's blood pressure medication was working. Donna's watch rotation board was on the wall behind her, ignored for the first time in months.

Mateo and Ava were throwing a ball with two Denver kids near the creek. Not a real ball. Something Mateo had made from tape and rags. It didn't bounce right. They didn't care.

"Better," Marcus said.

"Better," Sarah agreed. She ate another bite of pie. "That's all I needed. Just better."

The music shifted. Joel had passed the guitar back to Frank, who'd been joined by Tommy with a pair of wooden spoons and a overturned bucket. The rhythm was terrible. It was the best thing Marcus had heard in months.

"Dance with me," Sarah said.

Marcus looked at her. "What?"

"Dance. You know what dancing is."

"I know what dancing is. I'm questioning the invitation."

"It's not an invitation. It's a directive from the Community Coordinator." She hopped off the wall and held out her hand. The one without pie residue.

Marcus took it.

They danced. Badly. In the grass near the fire pit, with the sun going down behind the mountains and Frank murdering a song that might have been "Lean on Me" if you used your imagination. Sarah's head came up to Marcus's shoulder. She stepped on his feet twice. He stepped on hers once.

"You're terrible at this," she said.

"You invited me."

"Directed you."

"Same outcome."

They shuffled in a circle. Not a waltz. Not a two-step. The formless, comfortable movement of two people who knew each other well enough that the dancing was beside the point.

Marcus looked over Sarah's head at the compound. At the lights coming on in the cabins as the sun dropped behind the western ridge. At the flags, limp now without the afternoon breeze. At Joel, who was watching them from the stage with an expression that was either protective or approving and was probably both.

For a moment. Just a moment. He forgot the points. Forgot the system. Forgot the Shamblers in a flooded basement and the timeline that was wrong and the twenty years of disasters he was supposed to prevent. Forgot all of it.

The music was bad and the food was running out and the crust on the pie was terrible and a fourteen-year-old girl was stepping on his feet and the apocalypse was eighteen months old and none of it mattered right now.

Sarah looked up at him. "What are you thinking about?"

"Nothing."

"Liar."

"I'm thinking this is the first time I've thought about nothing since September 2013."

Sarah considered this. Then she put her head against his arm, because his shoulder was too high, and they kept dancing until Frank ran out of chords.

## — Marcus —

Later.

The festival wound down the way good things wind down. Slowly, reluctantly, with people lingering at tables and finishing conversations and finding reasons not to go back to their cabins yet. The kids went first. Mia fell asleep in Ray's arms, the flower crown lost somewhere in the grass. The Denver families drifted toward the dining hall, where their bedrolls were laid out. The original residents stayed longer, cleaning up, stacking plates, putting leftover food into containers that Helen would inventory in the morning.

Joel brought the guitar back to Frank and said something quiet. Frank nodded. Joel walked to the cabin he shared with Tommy and didn't look back.

Sarah supervised cleanup until Marcus told her to stop supervising and go to bed. She argued. He pointed out that she'd been awake since five in the morning and had organized a festival for a hundred and forty-eight people and eaten one piece of pie.

"One and a half," she said.

"Go to bed, Sarah."

She went. Clipboard under her arm. The pen behind her ear. Fourteen years old and the most capable person in Crystal Falls, and Marcus included himself in that assessment.

He walked to the dam.

The walkway across the top was concrete, cracked in places, patched with material Tommy had mixed from the system-purchased supplies. The water was high. Spring runoff from the snowpack. The turbine hummed below his feet. A hundred and sixty-eight kilowatts of power flowing into a settlement that existed because a gamer with a knife and a heads-up display had decided to build something instead of just surviving.

Marcus leaned on the railing and looked at the valley.

Lights. Not many. Not like a city. But enough. The cabins. The greenhouse. The dining hall, where someone had left the porch light on. The observation post where Dale was on night watch, a flashlight beam sweeping the south approach in slow arcs.

A hundred and forty-eight people, asleep or getting there. Full stomachs. One good day behind them.

He opened the system out of habit.

**[Points: 1,859]**
**[Settlement Population: 148]**
**[Morale: Stable]**

Stable. Not High. Not Uncertain. Stable. One festival, one good meal, one evening of music and dancing and arguments about beets, and the morale indicator had climbed back from the edge.

Marcus closed the system. He stood on the dam in the dark and listened to the water and the generator and the wind in the pines and didn't open the map. Didn't check for contacts. Didn't scan the perimeter.

Just stood there.

The mountains were dark shapes against a sky full of stars. No light pollution for eighty miles in any direction. The Milky Way was visible. Actual stars, the kind people used to drive hours into the desert to see. Now they were the default.

Somewhere north, in a flooded basement in Aspen, the acid residue from three dead Shamblers was eating through the concrete floor. Somewhere east, the Denver QZ was a graveyard. Somewhere in the world, the fungus was evolving into things he couldn't predict from a video game he'd played in another life.

But here. Right now. A community of a hundred and forty-eight people had eaten together and danced to bad music and argued about cornbread and nobody had been afraid.

That was worth 1,859 points. It was worth more than that.

Marcus adjusted the bandage on his jaw. The new skin itched. Tomorrow he'd talk to Joel about Shambler protocols. Tomorrow he'd run the numbers on cabin kits. Tomorrow the clipboard would be full again and the council would meet and the chickens would escape their fence and the world would keep being the world.

Tomorrow.

Tonight, the lights were on and the music was over and that was enough.
