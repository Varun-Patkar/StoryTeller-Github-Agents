# Chapter 19: First Light

## — Marcus —

Day forty-three started with Tommy banging on something metal inside the powerhouse at six in the morning.

Marcus rolled out of his sleeping bag on the maintenance building floor, neck stiff, back cold where the concrete had leached the warmth out of him through the night. The fire in the steel drum had died down to embers. Joel was already up, standing by the open door with a cup of instant coffee that Marcus had bought from the Mall at some point yesterday. Steam rose from it in a thin line.

"He's been in there since five," Joel said, not looking away from the powerhouse door across the yard. "Started with the intake gate. Then something with a wrench. Then whatever that is."

A clang. A pause. A string of words that Marcus couldn't make out but assumed weren't complimentary toward the turbine.

"Should we help?"

"You ever rebuilt a Francis turbine?"

"No."

"Me neither." Joel sipped his coffee. "Let him work."

Marcus bought two more cups of instant coffee from the Mall. Eight points. Expensive for coffee, but the alternative was no coffee, and no coffee on a Colorado morning in late November was not a viable option. He made one for himself and left the other by Sarah's sleeping bag. She was still out, buried under the cold-weather bag with only the top of her head visible, hair fanned across the rolled-up jacket she used as a pillow.

He sat next to Joel in the doorway and drank his coffee.

The valley looked different in the morning. Colder. Sharper. The frost on the meadow caught the first light coming over the eastern ridge and turned the dead grass silver-white. The river ran dark below the dam, throwing up mist where it hit the rocks downstream. The mountains on three sides were grey and white and enormous in a way that still hadn't gotten normal.

"Roof first," Joel said.

Marcus looked at him.

"The maintenance building. That west section where the metal pulled away. Water gets in there, it rots the floor, weakens the walls, and by spring you've got structural damage." Joel finished his coffee. "I need roofing screws, a sheet of corrugated steel, and a caulk gun. And a ladder."

"How much does a ladder cost?" Marcus said it to himself, pulling up the Mall. The answer was fourteen points. Corrugated steel sheet, twenty-two. Roofing screws, six. Caulk gun and tube, nine. Fifty-one points total.

**[Points: 437]**

The items appeared in a neat stack against the interior wall. Joel walked over, picked up the ladder, tested its weight, extended it, locked the joints. Didn't comment on where it had come from. Just carried it outside and leaned it against the maintenance building's west wall.

Marcus followed with the steel sheet. It was awkward to carry, flexing and catching the wind. Joel took one end without being asked and they walked it to the base of the wall together.

"Hold the bottom. I'll go up."

Joel climbed the ladder with the caulk gun in his back pocket and the drill hanging from a belt loop. He moved the way he always moved on job sites, Marcus imagined. Steady. No wasted motion. The kind of guy who'd done this a thousand times and didn't need to think about hand placement or foot position.

From above, the sound of old screws being pulled, then new ones going in. The drill whined and bit. Joel cursed once, softly, when a screw stripped. Marcus handed up the steel sheet and Joel positioned it over the gap, overlapping the existing panels by six inches on each side.

Twenty minutes. The gap was sealed. Joel came down the ladder, looked up at his work, and nodded once.

"That'll hold through winter. Do it properly in spring when we can get up on the whole roof."

Two sentences. More than Joel usually gave for a finished job. Marcus took it as approval.

## — Sarah —

The residence smelled like dust and cold stone and something faintly animal, like mice or squirrels had been nesting in the walls. Sarah didn't mind. She'd smelled worse in the last six weeks. A lot worse.

She started with the main room. Swept the floor with a broom Marcus bought her, which felt absurd and normal at the same time. Sweeping a floor in the apocalypse. Like the world hadn't ended. Like she was doing Saturday chores back on Winslow Drive.

The broom kicked up so much dust she had to open the front door and stand outside coughing for two minutes.

She went back in.

The cast-iron stove worked. She tested it with some dry leaves and twigs, and the chimney drew clean. No blockage. The kitchen had a stone counter and open shelving, everything covered in a grey film that came away when she wiped it with a rag. Underneath, the stone was smooth and pale. Someone had taken care of this place once.

The bathroom was a lost cause for now. No running water meant the toilet was decorative and the bathtub was a large, cold, cast-iron suggestion. But the mirror cleaned up okay. She rubbed it with the damp rag until she could see herself clearly for the first time in weeks.

She looked older. Not in a dramatic way. Not hollowed out or hardened like some movie version of a post-apocalypse survivor. Just older. Her face was thinner. Her hair was longer and tangled in places she'd given up reaching. There was a scar on her left temple from the tree branch that had caught her during the Stalker fight in the school, a thin white line she hadn't noticed until now.

Thirteen in three months. She looked thirteen already.

She left the mirror and went to the back bedroom. Her room.

The east-facing window let in a bar of morning light that crossed the floor at an angle and climbed the far wall. Just like she'd imagined. The light was pale and cold this time of year, not the warm gold it would be in summer, but it was there. Real light through a real window in a real room that was hers.

The floor was warped near the exterior wall where water had gotten in through the broken window. She'd need glass for that. Or boards, at least.

She could ask Marcus. Marcus could get anything.

Instead, she found a piece of cardboard in the garage and wedged it into the window frame. It blocked the light but kept the wind out. A trade-off. She'd deal with it until they had glass.

She dragged her sleeping bag into the room and unrolled it on the floor. Placed her journal on the windowsill. The pen beside it.

Good enough.

## — Marcus —

Joel stayed at the maintenance building, working through a list he'd made in his head. Tightening door hinges. Checking the concrete block walls for cracks. Testing the steel drum's chimney draw and adjusting the height of the flue pipe with a hacksaw Marcus bought him. Joel didn't ask for things. He stated what he needed in flat, specific terms and waited for them to appear. Like ordering from a restaurant where the waiter was invisible.

Marcus left him to it and headed downstream.

The map showed seventeen red dots within a three-mile radius of the dam. Mostly Level 1 Runners. Two Level 2s. Scattered through the valley and the surrounding forest, concentrated around a small cluster of structures a mile and a half north that the map labeled as "Marble, CO — Pop. 131 (pre-outbreak)."

A town. A tiny one, barely a town at all. But a town meant buildings. Buildings meant Runners. Runners meant points.

He found Joel at the maintenance building door.

"I'm going to clear the town north of here. Seventeen infected on the map."

Joel set down the hacksaw. "I'll come."

"You don't have to. I can handle seventeen Runners."

"You can handle seventeen Runners," Joel agreed. "But you shouldn't. Not from a fixed position. You clear the area around your base with two people, minimum. One to fight, one to watch. That's not paranoia, that's doctrine."

Marcus didn't argue. Joel was right, and Joel being right about tactical things was a pattern he'd stopped resisting somewhere around the third week on the road.

They took the handguns and the hunting knife. Joel carried the SIG. Marcus had the Glock 19 he'd bought from the Mall back in Texas. They walked down the valley road, past the rusted gate, through the stand of aspens where the truck was parked.

The morning was bright and cold. Frost on the road. Their boots left prints in it. The river ran beside them, louder here where it dropped over a series of small cascades, the water white and fast.

Marble, Colorado was exactly the kind of town that stopped existing when the world ended and nobody noticed.

A main street. Two blocks long. A post office built from the white marble the town was named for. A general store with a wooden porch and a sign that read MARBLE MERCANTILE in faded green letters. A gas station. A bar called the Slow Grind. A church. A handful of houses set back from the road, small and neat, with yards gone wild.

The Runners were spread through the buildings. Four in the general store. Three in the gas station. Two in the church. The rest scattered in houses and yards, standing in rooms or wandering hallways, doing whatever infected did when nobody was around to kill. Existing. Waiting. Rotting slowly into something worse.

Joel took the left side of the street. Marcus took the right. They'd developed a system over four hundred miles of road: clear one building, signal, move to the next. No talking. Hand signals that Joel had taught him, borrowed from wherever Joel had learned them. Tommy, maybe. Or twenty years of doing this kind of thing with people who were all dead now.

The general store first.

Marcus went in through the back door. The store was dark and cluttered, shelves still stocked with canned goods and dry goods and cleaning supplies covered in a layer of dust. A Runner stood in the center aisle, facing away, swaying slightly. A woman in a flannel shirt. Her hands were bloody and her neck was thick with fungal growth.

He put her down with the knife. Quick and quiet. The blade went in at the base of the skull and she dropped without a sound.

**[Runner killed. +7 points]**

The second Runner came from the storeroom, drawn by the noise of the body hitting the floor. Faster than the first. Marcus sidestepped and caught it in the temple. Messy. The knife glanced off bone and he had to wrench it free and go again. The second strike was clean.

**[Runner killed. +6 points]**

The third and fourth were together in the back office, slumped against a desk like they'd been sitting there since the outbreak. They came up fast when Marcus kicked the door. He got one. Joel appeared in the doorway behind the second and put a bullet through its head.

"Heard you fumble the second one," Joel said.

"I didn't fumble."

"You hit bone. That's a fumble."

"I corrected."

"You corrected slow." Joel ejected the spent casing and stepped over the body. "Angle your wrist ten degrees. The temple's thin bone, but only if you hit the flat. You're hitting the ridge."

Marcus filed that away. Joel didn't give advice often, and when he did, it was worth remembering.

They worked through the town in forty minutes. Seventeen Runners. Joel took six. Marcus took eleven. The gas station was the messiest, three infected in a tight space between pumps and shelving. Marcus used the Glock for those. Louder, but in a town this empty, noise discipline mattered less than not getting bit in a space where you couldn't swing a knife.

**[Runners killed (x11, Joel Miller assist on 6). +93 points]**
**[Area Clear: Marble, CO — Bonus: +25 points]**
**[Streak 17: +15 points]**
**[Points: 570]**

A hundred and thirty-three points. Not bad for a morning's work.

The general store was worth searching. Marcus and Joel spent twenty minutes going through the shelves. Most of the food was expired or spoiled, but canned goods lasted longer than their labels promised. They found sixteen cans of beans, eight cans of soup, four cans of peaches, and a sealed bag of rice that had somehow avoided moisture and mice. A case of bottled water. Three boxes of matches. A first aid kit behind the counter with bandages and antiseptic that were still sealed.

Real scavenging. Not Mall purchases. There was something satisfying about finding things the old-fashioned way. Like it proved the world still had something to offer if you went looking.

Joel found a pair of work gloves in his size and put them on. He flexed his fingers, testing the fit. Looked at Marcus.

"Good store."

High praise from Joel Miller.

They carried the supplies back to the dam in two trips. Sarah met them at the gate and helped unload without asking what they'd been doing. She counted the cans and sorted them by type on the kitchen counter in the residence. Beans on the left. Soup in the middle. Peaches on the right. The rice went on the top shelf.

Organizational habits from a twelve-year-old who was learning to run a household in the apocalypse.

## — Tommy —

"Marcus."

Tommy's voice came from the powerhouse door, flat and focused. The voice of a man who'd been arguing with a machine for nine hours and had just won a round.

Marcus crossed the yard. Inside the powerhouse, Tommy stood next to the turbine housing with grease up to his elbows and penetrating oil soaked into his shirt. The bearing puller was on the floor beside a set of bearings the size of dinner plates, grey and corroded but intact.

"Bearings are free," Tommy said. "Seized from sitting dry, like I thought. Soaked them in penetrating oil since five this morning. They broke loose about an hour ago." He wiped his forehead with the back of his wrist, leaving a dark streak. "The runner blades are in good shape. Minimal corrosion. The shaft is straight. I greased everything and hand-rotated the assembly. It turns."

"It turns," Marcus repeated.

"Smooth, too. The rancher who maintained this thing knew what he was doing. These bearings have decades of life left if we keep them greased." Tommy pointed at the generator. "That's tomorrow. I need to check the windings, clean the commutator, test the insulation. If the generator's good, we can open the intake gate and spin up."

"How long?"

"Two days. Maybe three."

The quest said fourteen. Tommy was going to do it in four or five. Marcus felt something loosen in his chest that he hadn't realized was tight.

"There's something else I need," Tommy said. "Wire. Electrical wire. Twelve-gauge, copper, at least five hundred feet. And a breaker panel. If we're running power to the buildings, we need a distribution system. Otherwise we've got a spinning turbine and nowhere for the electricity to go."

Marcus checked the Mall.

**[12-gauge copper wire (500 ft spool): 45 points]**
**[60-amp breaker panel w/ breakers: 38 points]**

Eighty-three points. He had 570.

"Done."

The wire and panel appeared against the powerhouse wall. Tommy looked at them, then at Marcus.

"I know you do that," Tommy said. "And I know I'm not supposed to ask how."

"You can ask."

"Would you answer?"

"Not today."

Tommy nodded. "Fair enough." He picked up the wire spool and hefted it onto the workbench. "Help me run a line from the generator to the maintenance building. We'll do the residence tomorrow."

They worked until the light faded. Stringing wire through conduit along the outside wall of the powerhouse, across the yard on posts that Joel cut from saplings, and into the maintenance building through a hole Tommy drilled in the concrete block. Tommy wired a breaker panel to the interior wall and connected three circuits: overhead lights, wall outlets, and a dedicated line for the space heater that Marcus bought for forty points.

Not the turbine. Not yet. But they had the infrastructure ready. When the turbine spun up, they'd have power in the building at the flip of a breaker.

For tonight, though, Marcus had something else.

## — Sarah —

She heard the generator before she saw it.

A low, steady hum coming from behind the maintenance building. Not loud. Not the aggressive rattle of a construction site generator. More like a purr. Constant and mechanical and alive.

She came around the corner and found Marcus standing next to a compact generator, the kind you'd see on a camping trip or a job site. It was connected to the breaker panel through an orange extension cable that snaked through the hole in the wall.

"Backup generator," Marcus said. "The one from the settlement kit was cached in New Mexico, but I had enough points for a smaller unit. Runs on diesel. We've got about forty hours of fuel."

"Forty hours of what?"

Marcus reached through the door and flipped a switch on the breaker panel.

The overhead lights in the maintenance building came on.

Not all of them. Two of the four fluorescent fixtures were dead, bulbs burned out or ballasts fried. But two worked. They flickered once, buzzed, and then held steady. A flat, institutional white light that filled the building and pushed the shadows into corners.

Sarah stood in the doorway and stared.

Light. Electric light. Not firelight. Not a flashlight. Not the pale beam of a headlamp bouncing off walls. Actual overhead light, the kind she'd lived under her whole life without thinking about it. The kind that came from a switch on a wall and stayed on.

She'd been in the dark for forty-three days.

Campfires. Flashlights. The dashboard lights of the truck. Once, the glow of a burning town on the horizon. But never this. Never a room with lights that someone turned on because they could.

Tommy came out of the powerhouse, still wiping his hands on a rag. He looked at the maintenance building. At the light spilling out of the open door and the two windows, falling across the yard in long yellow rectangles on the frost-killed grass.

"Well," Tommy said. He put his hands on his hips. "Look at that."

Joel appeared from the side of the building where he'd been stacking firewood. He stopped. Looked at the lit windows. Looked at Marcus.

"Generator?"

"Generator."

"How long?"

"Forty hours on this one. When the turbine's running, as long as the river flows."

Joel looked at the lit windows again. Something passed across his face. Not a smile. Joel didn't do smiles the way other people did. But the corners of his mouth moved, and the lines around his eyes shifted, and for a second he looked like a man who remembered what houses were supposed to look like at night.

"Sarah," he said. "Come inside."

She went inside. The light was warm even though it was fluorescent. The workbenches looked different lit from above. The drill press had a shadow. The tool outlines on the pegboard were crisp and clear. The calendar from 2011 was brighter than it had any right to be, the woman in the sundress smiling at a lake that probably didn't exist anymore.

Sarah sat on a workbench and looked up at the lights.

Forty-three days of dark. Of huddling around fires and squinting at pages by flashlight and sleeping in rooms where you couldn't see the ceiling. Of knowing that the dark was where the infected lived and the dark was where the bad people did bad things and the dark was what the world had become.

And now. Lights.

She didn't cry. She wasn't a crier, not anymore. But she sat on the workbench and looked up at the fluorescent tubes and didn't say anything for a while, and nobody asked her to.

...

## — Marcus —

They ate dinner in the lit maintenance building. Canned soup heated on the steel drum fire, because they hadn't wired the residence kitchen yet and the camp stove was still in the truck. Bean with bacon for Joel and Tommy. Chicken noodle for Sarah and Marcus.

The light made everything feel different. Not better, exactly. Different. Like they were people again instead of shapes in the dark. Marcus could see Joel's face clearly for the first time in days, the stubble and the lines and the way his eyes tracked from the door to the windows on a cycle, checking entry points even here. Even now.

Tommy had the atlas out again, sketching the wiring plan for the residence and the powerhouse. He'd drawn a site map of the whole compound. Buildings, distances, wire runs, the path of the penstock down the canyon wall. Professional. Clean. The kind of drawing that could go on a wall in an engineering office.

"When we get the turbine running," Tommy said, tracing a line with his pencil, "we'll have consistent power. Two hundred kilowatts at peak capacity. This generator puts out five. The difference is the difference between a flashlight and a floodlight."

"We need to talk about the retrieval trip," Marcus said. "The settlement kit is cached in a feed store in Sweetwater, New Mexico. Five hundred miles south."

"When?" Joel asked.

"Soon. Within the week, if we can. The cache is hidden, but it's not locked down. Anyone could find it."

"I'll go," Joel said. Flat. Not volunteering. Stating.

"Both of us. You and me. Tommy stays on the turbine. Sarah stays with Tommy."

"I can go," Sarah said.

"Somebody needs to be here with a rifle."

"Tommy has a rifle."

"Tommy'll be inside the powerhouse with his head in a turbine housing fourteen hours a day. He needs someone watching the valley entrance. Someone who can shoot."

Sarah looked at him. The fluorescent light made her eyes very clear.

"You taught me to shoot three weeks ago."

"And you're good at it. That's why you're staying."

She held his gaze for another two seconds. Then she reached across the workbench and took his crackers.

"Fine. But you bring me back something."

"Like what?"

"Surprise me."

Joel's mouth twitched. The Joel Miller version of a laugh.

Marcus pulled up the Settlement Management interface while the others finished eating. The display had updated since the last time he'd checked.

**[Marble Dam Settlement — Day 1]**
**[Population: 4]**
**[Structures: Maintenance Shop (Repaired — Roof Patched), Residence (Habitable — No Running Water), Garage (Damaged — Not In Use)]**
**[Infrastructure: Backup Generator (Active — 38 hrs fuel remaining)]**
**[Defense: Perimeter Unfortified. Valley Entrance Unsecured.]**
**[Morale: Stable]**

**[Quest: Foundation — Restore power to Marble Dam]**
**[Time Remaining: 13 days]**
**[Status: In Progress (Turbine bearings freed, generator windings pending)]**

**[New Quest Available: Supply Run]**
**[Retrieve cached Settlement Starter Kit from Sweetwater, NM]**
**[Distance: 487 miles one way]**
**[Reward: +150 points, Settlement Starter Kit activated]**

Two active quests. One for the turbine, one for the supply run. The system was keeping pace with their plans, which was either helpful or controlling. Marcus chose to see it as helpful. The alternative was thinking too hard about who or what was running the system, and that was a rabbit hole he'd been avoiding since Day 1.

He closed the display.

The generator hummed outside. The lights buzzed overhead. Tommy sketched. Joel cleaned the SIG. Sarah wrote in her journal, pen moving across the page in the steady, deliberate strokes of someone recording things that mattered.

Four people. A valley. A dam that would spin in two days. A supply run south and back. A town a mile and a half away, freshly cleared, with a general store full of canned goods. A perimeter that needed securing. A gate that needed building. A residence that needed glass in the windows and water in the pipes and heat in the walls.

All of it ahead of them. All of it possible.

Marcus leaned back against the workbench and looked at the lights on the ceiling. Two fluorescent tubes, buzzing and steady, filling the room with the kind of flat, ordinary light that nobody in the old world had ever thought twice about.

In the old world, light was nothing. You flipped a switch and there it was. Background noise for a life full of brighter things.

In this world, light was everything. It meant power and safety and warmth and the difference between a camp and a home. It meant someone had built something that worked, that the dark wasn't permanent, that the world could still be pushed back.

Tomorrow, Joel and Marcus would plan the supply run. Tommy would crack open the generator housing and start on the windings. Sarah would patrol the valley entrance with the rifle and her journal and the quiet competence that made her the most dangerous twelve-year-old in Colorado.

Tonight, though. Tonight they had soup and light and four walls and a roof that didn't leak anymore.

It was enough.
