# Chapter 31: Year Two

## — Marcus —

April 14, 2015. Crystal Falls, Colorado.

The chickens were louder than the infected.

Marcus stood on the dam walkway at six in the morning, coffee in hand, real coffee from a 25-point bag of Colombian medium roast he'd bought himself as a reward for clearing the nest in Eagle. Below him, thirty-seven chickens argued about breakfast in the coop on the south slope. Lena's chickens. Lena's pride and joy. The flock Tommy had driven twelve miles to collect from that abandoned ranch seventeen months ago had multiplied with the enthusiasm of creatures that had no concept of the apocalypse.

The compound below him wasn't a compound anymore.

Crystal Falls. The name the community had voted on back in January 2014, three months after Sarah put it to the council. Fourteen options on the ballot. Carson wanted "New Hope." Helen wanted "Bastion." Gene, the retired teacher, suggested "Marble Heights." Mia, who was seven and shouldn't have been allowed a vote but Sarah couldn't say no to, wrote "Rainbow Town" in crayon on her ballot.

Crystal Falls won with thirty-one votes out of forty-eight. Named for the Crystal River and the small waterfall two hundred yards north of the dam that froze solid every winter and came back louder every spring.

Marcus sipped his coffee and looked at the town.

A hundred and six people lived here now. He knew because Helen updated the census board outside the maintenance building every Sunday, and because Sarah knew every single one of them by name, occupation, and sleeping arrangement.

The original maintenance building and residence were still standing, but they were buried in newer construction. Twelve cabins along the east slope, built by Carson and Frank's crew over the winter of 2014. A dining hall with seating for sixty, because feeding a hundred people from a kitchen table had stopped being funny around person number forty. Three bunkhouses north of the dam, including one that had been designated the school and nothing else after Barbara and Elliot threatened rebellion if their classroom got repurposed one more time.

The clinic was a real building now. Not a corner of the maintenance building with a curtain. Dr. Reyes had four rooms: exam, recovery, supply closet, and a waiting area that doubled as a break room. She had two trained assistants. Victor Huang, a former paramedic from Denver. Rosa Espinoza, a veterinary technician who turned out to be better at stitches than anyone expected.

The farming operation covered the entire south slope. Lena had gotten her eight garden beds. Then twelve more. Then she'd stopped counting beds and started counting acres. Two acres under cultivation now, with a greenhouse Marcus had bought from Tier 3 for 600 points in November, when Lena explained with terrifying precision exactly how many calories they'd lose without year-round growing capacity.

Tommy's dam ran at full capacity. Ray had rewired the distribution grid twice as the town expanded, and the power situation was the one thing nobody complained about. Lights, heat, water filtration, the clinic's equipment, the welding station, the greenhouse heaters. A hundred people lived with electricity in a world without it, and they took it for granted the way people always take miracles for granted once they become routine.

Marcus drained the coffee. He'd started allowing himself small luxuries from the Mall. Coffee, decent soap, a book every few weeks. The point economy had shifted over the past year. Clearing runs in the expanding perimeter brought in steady income. Enhanced Runners had become common enough that his weekly haul averaged three to four hundred points, and a good week with a Clicker nest could push seven hundred.

He'd spent most of it. Thousands of points over eighteen months, converted into lumber, nails, glass, tools, food, medicine, seeds, generators, clothing, textbooks, a piano that Barbara requested and Marcus couldn't justify but bought anyway because the sound of someone practicing scales in the dining hall at sunset made the town feel like a place where people lived instead of a place where people survived.

But the balance was building again. And the Tier 3 catalog had items he'd been watching for months.

He opened the Mall interface.

**[Tier 3 — Industrial]**
**[Portable CNC Machine: 1,200 points]**
**[Hydraulic Press: 800 points]**
**[Industrial Welding Suite (upgrade): 400 points]**
**[Vehicle Armor Kit (Humvee-class): 1,500 points]**
**[Diesel Engine (rebuilt, 6.5L): 900 points]**
**[Machine Lathe: 650 points]**

The settlement was past the point where hand tools and Frank's forge could keep up. They needed manufacturing capability. The kind that let you make parts instead of buying them, repair vehicles instead of abandoning them.

And the armored vehicle. That was the big one.

Tommy had found a Humvee chassis on a forest service road east of Carbondale back in February. Engine seized, tires gone, but the frame was solid. It had been sitting in the back lot for two months while Marcus accumulated the points.

Today, he had enough.

He bought the diesel engine and the vehicle armor kit.

**[Purchase: Diesel Engine (rebuilt, 6.5L) — 900 points]**
**[Purchase: Vehicle Armor Kit (Humvee-class) — 1,500 points]**
**[Remaining Points: 447]**

The engine and armor plates appeared behind the maintenance building in the designated materialization zone, the gravel area Marcus had fenced off with a "SUPPLY CACHE — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY" sign. Helen's inventory system logged everything that appeared there. The fiction was thin. It held.

Marcus looked at the engine sitting on the gravel. Nine hundred points. Eight months ago, that would have been everything he had. Now it was a Thursday morning purchase.

"That wasn't there yesterday," Tommy said.

He was walking up from the powerhouse with a wrench in one hand and a grease rag in the other. His morning routine. Check the turbine, check the distribution panel, check the backup generator, walk the grid. Tommy had become the infrastructure backbone of Crystal Falls the way Joel had become its shield.

"Engine for the Humvee," Marcus said.

Tommy crouched beside it, pulled the dipstick. Clean oil. Factory spec. A rebuilt engine appearing fully serviced behind a maintenance building in the Colorado Rockies, eighteen months into the apocalypse. He didn't comment on it.

"I can have it running in a week," Tommy said. "Carson can fabricate the engine mounts. The armor's Frank's department. Two weeks for the full build?"

"Sounds right."

"We're going to need a garage. A real one. Something with a lift and a pit."

"Put it on the list."

"The list is four pages long, Marcus."

"Then it's five."

Tommy shook his head and went to find Carson.

## — Sarah —

The school had outgrown Bunkhouse B twice.

First when the student population hit fifteen and Barbara started holding classes in shifts. Then again in February, when Marcus bought a prefabricated building frame from the Mall and Carson's crew turned it into a proper school building in eight days.

Sarah stood in the doorway at nine in the morning, watching twenty-three children between the ages of four and thirteen learn things.

Barbara had the younger kids in the left room. Alphabet, basic math, reading. Mia, now eight, was helping the four-year-olds with their letters, which mostly meant correcting them with an authority that was deeply impressive for someone who couldn't spell "necessary."

Elliot had the older kids in the right room. He'd expanded beyond English into history, basic science, and geography. Not because anyone told him to, but because the kids asked questions and Elliot couldn't not answer them. His classroom had a world map on the wall. One that showed the old world, borders and cities and highways that most of these kids had never seen and the youngest ones never would.

Diego Vasquez, eleven now, was reading from a battered copy of Hatchet. Sophie Marsh was writing in a notebook. Still silent. Pages and pages of tiny, precise handwriting that Elliot called the most advanced creative writing he'd seen from any student, pre-or post-apocalypse.

Owen Okafor, sixteen months old, was not enrolled. He was in the corner of Barbara's room in a playpen that Frank had built, chewing on a wooden block and making sounds that Jessica claimed were words.

"How's attendance?" Sarah asked Barbara.

"Perfect. Third week running." Barbara adjusted her taped glasses. "Turns out, when you give children structure and snacks, they show up."

The snacks were Sarah's addition. Oatmeal cookies, baked by Donna's crew, distributed at the midmorning break. A bribe. Sarah didn't pretend otherwise. Morale was built on small things. Coffee for the adults. Cookies for the kids. Lena's garlic in the evening soup.

She left the school and walked to the farming plots.

The south slope was green. Not the hypothetical green of Lena's planning notebooks but the real, photosynthesis-happening green of plants in dirt. Lettuce in the cold frames. Potato sprouts in the raised beds. Onion sets in rows that Lena had marked with string so precise you could measure them with a ruler.

The greenhouse was Lena's cathedral. Twenty by forty feet of polycarbonate panels and dam-powered heating, where tomatoes and peppers and herbs grew year-round regardless of what the Colorado weather thought about it.

"Beans are up," Lena said from inside. She was on her knees in the dirt, which was her natural habitat. "Two weeks ahead of schedule. I want to plant the south field in corn this year."

"How much labor?"

"Four people, full time, for the planting. Then maintenance."

Sarah wrote it down. Labor allocation was her permanent puzzle. A hundred and six people, minus the children, minus Dr. Reyes and her staff, minus the militia, minus Tommy's infrastructure crew, minus the construction team. That left roughly forty able-bodied adults for farming, cooking, cleaning, laundry, and everything else that kept a community of a hundred alive.

It wasn't enough. It was never enough. She made it work anyway.

"I'll put it on the rotation," Sarah said. "Can you draft a planting schedule? I need dates and headcount."

"Already done." Lena handed her a folded piece of paper. Detailed. Color-coded. Soil temperature projections based on her own daily readings.

"This is beautiful," Sarah said.

"It's a planting schedule."

"It's beautiful."

Lena went back to her beans.

Sarah flipped through her notebook, the third one now. The first two were full. She was fourteen years old and she ran the civilian half of a hundred-person settlement and she'd stopped thinking of that as unusual around month eight. Titles still didn't interest her. People called her "the coordinator" or just "Sarah," and both meant the same thing: the person you found when something needed doing.

She had a meeting with Dr. Reyes at ten about maternity supplies. Three pregnancies in the community. Three families choosing to bring children into a world full of fungal monsters and armed strangers, because Crystal Falls had lights and walls and a school and a doctor and chickens. Because it felt like enough.

Some days Sarah thought about that. About what "enough" meant when the world outside was what it was.

Most days she just looked at the list and picked the next item.

## — Joel —

The militia was thirty-two people now.

Joel ran them through close-quarters drills in the meadow every Tuesday and Thursday. Not Marcus's formation exercises from that first December. Real scenarios. He'd put them in one of the empty cabins on the north side and send a threat team of four experienced fighters through the doors while the defenders tried to hold, communicate, and not shoot each other.

Keiko Tanaka ran the threat team. She was merciless.

"Dead," she said, pointing at Garrett, who'd stepped into a doorway without checking the corner. "Dead." She pointed at Priya, who'd moved without signaling her partner. "Also dead." She pointed at Dale Briggs, who'd done everything right but froze when Keiko came around the corner at full speed.

"Dale held position," Joel said from the doorway. He'd been watching.

"He froze," Keiko said.

"He froze and held position. That's different from freezin' and runnin'."

Keiko considered this. She was the best fighter in Crystal Falls after Marcus and Joel, and she'd earned it through eighteen months of relentless training. She thought like a predator. Fast, aggressive, always pressing forward. Joel had spent the past year teaching her that defense was as important as attack, and she was starting to believe it.

"Dale," Joel said. "Next time, call it out. You see her comin', you say 'Contact left.' Don't wait for your brain to catch up. Let your mouth do the work."

Dale nodded. He was breathing hard. The cold air clouded around his face.

Joel dismissed the group. They dispersed toward the dining hall, talking in low voices, replaying the drill. The conversations afterward were where the real learning happened. Joel knew that from experience. The drill showed you what you did wrong. The conversation with your partner was where you figured out what to do differently.

Helen fell into step beside him.

"We need another patrol team," she said. "The south route is too long for one group. Three hours minimum. By the time they're back, the north route is overdue."

"How many?"

"Eight for two full teams on rotation. I have the personnel. I need you to sign off on the weapons draw."

Joel had sole authority over the armory. Every weapon in Crystal Falls was locked in a reinforced storage room in the maintenance building. Every bullet was counted. Every draw required his signature.

"Which eight?"

Helen listed them. Joel knew all of them. He'd trained most of them.

"Approved. Rifles only for the new team. No sidearms until they qualify."

Helen made a note.

"One more thing," she said. "Marcus is putting an armored Humvee together."

"I know."

"What's it for?"

"Extended range. Clearing runs past twenty miles are on foot right now. That's a full day there and back, if nothin' goes wrong. Vehicle changes that."

"And if something does go wrong?"

"That's what the armor's for."

Helen nodded. She didn't look entirely satisfied, but she didn't push. She'd learned that about Joel. Short answers weren't dismissals. They were the whole answer.

Joel watched her walk toward the maintenance building. Efficient stride. Clipboard in hand.

He stood in the meadow for another minute. The April sun was warm on his face. From here he could see the cabins on the east slope, smoke coming from three chimneys. The school, where he could hear a kid laughing at something. The gate at the valley entrance, where Donna was starting her afternoon shift.

Eighteen months ago he'd built houses in Austin for people who wanted granite countertops and walk-in closets. Now he built walls and gates and trained volunteers to defend a town that ran on dam power and one man's impossible supply chain.

It was different.

He walked toward the dining hall. It was meatloaf night. Frank had slaughtered a chicken that stopped laying, and Donna had combined it with canned meat and some of Lena's onions into something that was almost good if you didn't think about it too hard.

Joel was hungry.

## — Marcus —

The council meeting ran long.

Seven seats now. Marcus, Joel, Sarah, Tommy, Helen, Dr. Reyes, and Lena. The original five had grown because the community had grown, and the domains were too big for five people to cover.

Tommy reported on the dam. Full capacity, no issues. He wanted to run a new power line to the south field for an irrigation pump.

Lena reported on farming. Corn planting in two weeks. Chicken flock stable. She wanted a goat before winter.

Dr. Reyes reported on medical. Three pregnancies. One broken arm, Garrett's, from Tuesday's drill. Supply request for antibiotics and surgical gloves.

Helen reported on security and population. Hundred and six residents. Two families expected from a contact on the western highway. Patrol routes reorganized, Joel's approval secured.

Sarah reported on everything else. School enrollment, meal rotation, work schedules, the community's request for a laundry building, and a suggestion from Barbara for a community library.

Joel reported on defense. Militia at thirty-two. Qualification drills ongoing. Armored Humvee in progress, estimated two weeks to operational.

Marcus reported on threats.

"The ten-mile perimeter is clean," he said. "I cleared three nests this week. Two Runner groups, one Clicker pair in the old campground east of Redstone. The twenty-mile perimeter has activity. Enhanced Runners moving south from the Aspen area. I'm seeing more Stalkers in buildings than last spring."

"More infected?" Tommy asked.

"Same number, maybe. But the composition is shifting. Fewer basic Runners. More evolved types. The fungus is maturing. Runners that survived the first year are becoming Stalkers and Clickers."

Quiet around the table.

"What does that mean for us?" Lena asked.

"It means clearing runs get harder. I can handle it. But the militia patrols need to be aware. Stalkers don't behave like Runners. They hide. They wait."

Joel nodded. "I'll adjust the trainin'."

The meeting moved on. Water. Sanitation. A dispute about wood-cutting rights on the north slope that Sarah resolved with two sentences and a scheduling change. The kind of governance that happened in details, not declarations.

When it was over, the council members filed out into the April evening. The sky was clear over the Rockies.

Marcus stayed. Sarah stayed.

She was flipping through her notebook.

"A hundred and six people," she said.

"Yeah."

"It was four. On that first day at the dam."

"Four people and a seized turbine."

She looked out the window. The lights of Crystal Falls spread down the valley. Warm amber from cabin windows. The blue-white of the floodlight Joel had mounted on the gate. The greenhouse glowing faintly on the south slope.

"Lena wants a goat," Sarah said.

"I heard."

"She's already named it."

"She doesn't have a goat yet."

"She's named it Patricia. She showed me a drawing."

Marcus leaned back in his chair. Frank's pine table, somehow still standing after a hundred meetings and ten thousand elbows. The surface was scratched and stained and had a ring from Tommy's coffee mug that would never come out.

"I'll add a goat to the list," he said.

"The list is five pages long."

"Then it's six."

Sarah closed the notebook. Outside, someone was playing piano in the dining hall. Scales, slow and careful, the sound carrying through the open window. One of the Denver refugees, a woman named Audrey who'd taught music before the outbreak. She practiced every evening after dinner, and nobody told her to stop because the sound of piano scales at dusk was the sound of a world that still had room for things that weren't necessary.

"The soup was better tonight," Marcus said.

"Lena put rosemary in it."

"It's almost edible."

"It's good soup, Marcus."

"It's adequate soup. The rosemary is doing heavy lifting."

Sarah stood up. She tucked the notebook into her jacket pocket.

"Two families coming in this week," she said. "Helen's contact on Highway 82. I need to figure out housing."

"We have the new cabin frame."

"We have the frame. We don't have glass for the windows."

"I'll get glass."

She looked at him. The same look from a year and a half ago on the dam walkway, when she'd proposed naming the town. Not impressed, not unimpressed. Just aware, in the way Sarah was always aware, that the glass would appear from the cache and the fiction would hold and the community would keep growing and the list would keep getting longer.

"Goodnight, Marcus."

"Night."

She left.

Marcus sat at the council table and looked at his status screen.

**[Points: 447]**

Not enough for the CNC machine. Not enough for the hydraulic press. He needed a big clearing run. Something north, past the clean perimeter, into the areas where the infected had been concentrating. Aspen. Glenwood Springs. Maybe the edge of the Denver ruins, if the Humvee was ready in time.

The infected were evolving. The community was growing. The space between those two facts was where Marcus lived, and he intended to keep it wide.

He closed the interface and went to bed.

Tomorrow, he'd start planning the run.
