# Chapter 52: Who Goes, Who Stays

## — Marcus —

**June 2033. Crystal Falls, Colorado. Day 1 of 2.**

The meeting was in the lodge kitchen. Not the council room. Not the powerhouse office. The kitchen, because that's where the four of them had always done the real talking, going back to a ranch table in 2013 that Tommy had wiped down with a rag while Joel stood against the wall with his arms folded and Sarah slept in the next room with a bandaged hip.

Same four people. Different table. Frank Garvey's pine council table from Day 52, scratched and stained with twenty years of spilled coffee and pencil marks and at least one set of gouges from Noah learning to whittle when he was seven.

Sarah had cleared the schedule. Tommy had come straight from the powerhouse. Joel had finished the morning militia drill and handed the clipboard to Keiko. Nobody else knew what this meeting was about.

Marcus laid out the transcription from Vasquez. Then the route map. Then the RETRIEVAL PLANNING file, sixty-three pages, opened to the operational summary Sarah had rewritten last night.

"Here's where we are," Marcus said. "Ellie Williams was bitten three weeks ago in the Boston QZ. She didn't turn. Marlene knows. Firefly personnel are moving west along I-90, which means Marlene is organizing transport to Salt Lake City. That's where Dr. Jerry Anderson and the surgical team are. The procedure to extract the Cordyceps growth from Ellie's brain will kill her."

He let that sit. They all knew the broad strokes. Joel and Sarah had been briefed years ago. Tommy had been brought in on the operational side in 2031. But hearing it with a timeline attached was different from knowing it in the abstract.

"Our window is narrow," Marcus continued. "If Marlene takes two weeks to organize departure from Boston, and we leave tomorrow morning, we can reach Boston before she moves Ellie. If she's faster than that, we're chasing a convoy across the country instead of extracting from a fixed location."

"Fixed location's better," Joel said. Not a question.

"Much better. The Boston QZ is fracturing. FEDRA and the Fireflies are in open conflict. That chaos gives us cover. Once Ellie's on the road with a Firefly escort, the variables multiply."

Tommy leaned forward. "Route."

Sarah pulled the map across the table. The western half was clean. Thick pencil lines along confirmed roads through federation territory, marked with resupply points at Basalt, the Kansas corridor, and Topeka.

"Crystal Falls to the Mississippi. Nine hundred miles through mostly cleared territory. We take the armored truck. Tommy's diesel engine, Marcus's armor kit, four-wheel drive. She'll do sixty on open road and handle rough terrain. Four days, maybe five."

"After the river?" Tommy asked.

"On foot. The eastern corridor is reconstructed from radio intercepts and trader contacts. Less certain. More populated. More dangerous. Joel and I can manage twenty to twenty-five miles a day on foot through hostile territory."

"That's three weeks from the Mississippi to Boston," Tommy said.

"Approximately. Total travel time: twenty-five to twenty-eight days one way."

Joel was doing the math behind his eyes. "Round trip of two months. Maybe more, dependin' on what we find."

"Yes."

The kitchen was quiet. Morning light through the windows. The mess hall crew was starting breakfast next door. Bread smell. The clatter of trays.

Sarah spoke.

"Two people go. Two people stay. That's not a debate. That's arithmetic."

She looked at Joel. At Tommy. At Marcus. Her thumb tapped against her index finger. One beat per second.

"Marcus goes because he's the only person who can get in and out of a hostile QZ and survive what's waiting there. Joel goes because this is a fourteen-year-old girl who needs to trust the people taking her away from the only life she knows, and Joel is the best person alive at earning that trust when it matters."

"That's generous," Joel said.

"It's accurate. You raised me. You trained a militia. You've been the person people turn to when they're scared for twenty years. A teenage girl in a war zone isn't going to trust a stranger who runs faster than a truck. She might trust an angry grandpa with a guitar."

Joel's mouth twitched. Almost a smile.

"Which means I stay," Sarah said. "And Tommy stays."

Tommy nodded. He'd already worked this out. Marcus could see it in the way he was sitting. Leaned back, arms loose. A man who'd made his decision before the meeting started and was waiting for everyone else to arrive at it.

"The federation has eighteen settlements and 4,100 people," Sarah continued. "The quarterly council meets in three weeks. Glenwood Springs is renegotiating water rights with Basalt. Willow Creek needs a new radio transmitter before the August storms. We have two pregnancies in the clinic, a harvest to manage, and a militia rotation that Joel hasn't handed off to anyone because he doesn't trust anyone else to run it the way he runs it."

"I trust Keiko," Joel said.

"You trust Keiko to do what you tell her. That's not the same as trusting her to make the calls when you're not here to tell her anything."

Joel looked at the table. He didn't argue. Sarah was right, and they both knew it.

"Helen can manage security logistics," Sarah said. "Keiko runs the militia drills. I handle the council and the federation. Tommy keeps the lights on, the water running, and the radio network functioning. That's the division."

"What about the Beacon sweeps?" Tommy asked. "Marcus has been running those personally."

"The last confirmed Beacon was in April. The federation's early warning network covers the corridor. If a new one surfaces while Marcus is gone, we contain and wait. Nobody tries to clear a Beacon without Marcus. That's a standing order."

Marcus watched Sarah work. She'd been preparing for this longer than anyone at the table realized. Not seven years, not since the RETRIEVAL PLANNING file was opened. Longer. Since the first council meeting on Day 52, when she'd created the weekly work schedule and split labor assignments and made herself the person who held the threads. She'd been building a settlement that could function without its strongest member since before she knew that was the test.

"Two months is a long time," Tommy said quietly. He wasn't talking about logistics.

"I know."

"If somethin' goes wrong out there, we can't reach you. The relay network dies at the Mississippi. After that, you're dark."

"I know."

Tommy looked at Joel. Brothers. Fifty-three and fifty. They'd built this place together. Tommy had wired every light, maintained every turbine, installed every relay station from Crystal Falls to Willow Creek. He'd walked into the first council meeting twenty years ago with dam grease on his hands and walked out with a domain that covered the entire power grid of a growing civilization. He'd never left.

"You bring him back," Tommy said to Marcus.

"I will."

"Both of you. You bring both of you back. I'm not explainin' to Sarah why her husband's dead in Massachusetts."

"Tommy."

"I'm serious." His voice was even. No dramatics. The same tone he used when reporting that a generator needed a part replaced. Factual. "I've watched you two walk into things that should've killed you since Day 7. The farmstead with the Runners. The school with the Stalkers. The horde at Snowmass. Every time, you come back because Joel's smart and you're strong and together you cover each other's gaps. Don't stop doin' that because the stakes are higher."

Marcus met his eyes. "We won't."

...

The rest of the meeting was logistics. An hour of route details, supply lists, and contingency plans. Marcus ran through the resupply points. Sarah assigned handoff responsibilities for every open project. Tommy listed the truck's maintenance needs. Joel dictated the militia transition plan, including three scenarios for Keiko to handle without contacting him.

By the time Sarah closed the RETRIEVAL PLANNING file, the bread smell had given way to eggs and the mess hall was filling up.

"One more thing," Sarah said. "What you tell people."

Marcus had been thinking about this. Six hundred and thirty-seven people in Crystal Falls. Most of them knew Marcus and Joel as fixtures. The two men who'd built the south gate, trained the militia, cleared every Beacon in the valley. Their absence for two months would be noticed on the first day.

"Long-range clearing run," Joel said. "Eastern corridor, past the relay network. Scouting for new federation contacts. We've done runs like that before."

"Not for two months."

"Then make it a special assignment from the council. Classification: operational security. Sarah announces it at Monday's meeting and nobody asks follow-up questions because Sarah's the one announcing it."

Sarah considered. "That works for the general population. Helen and Keiko will need more."

"Tell Keiko I'm scouting a threat in the east and I'll be back in eight weeks," Joel said. "She won't ask for details. Helen gets the logistics version. Supply run with a security component."

"And Noah?" Sarah asked, looking at Marcus.

"Noah knows. He figured it out last night."

Sarah nodded. Not surprised.

## — Joel —

He spent the afternoon doing something he hadn't done in fifteen years. Handing things off.

The militia schedule. A binder, handwritten, with three months of patrol rotations, training cycles, and equipment maintenance logs. Joel had kept it in the same format since 2018. Keiko had been copying his system for a decade. She didn't need the binder. She needed permission.

He found her at the range, cleaning rifles with Ana Gutierrez and Paul Crane. Twenty years since those two had been rookies on a clearing sweep. Now they were squad leaders with their own teams.

"Walk with me," Joel said.

They walked the north perimeter. Late afternoon. The sun was behind the ridge and the shadows were long and cool.

"I'm goin' east," Joel said. "Me and Marcus. We'll be gone about eight weeks."

Keiko waited. She was forty-seven now. Former gym teacher from Telluride who'd shown up at Crystal Falls in Year 2 with nothing but a camp stove and a talent for reading terrain that Marcus had called the best natural instinct he'd ever seen. She'd been running advanced militia training for twelve years.

"While I'm gone, you run it. All of it. Patrols, training, screening, threat assessment. You report to Sarah and the council. If a Beacon surfaces, you contain and hold. You do not attempt a clearance operation. Clear?"

"Clear."

"I'm leavin' the binder in the armory office. You don't need it, but it's there."

"Okay."

Joel stopped walking. Looked at the ridge, the treeline, the watchtowers. Twenty years of sightlines he'd memorized. Every approach angle, every dead zone, every improvement he'd made since that first tripwire system with Helen on Day 53.

"You're the best I've trained," he said. He didn't look at her when he said it. Joel Miller did not distribute compliments while making eye contact.

"I know," Keiko said.

He almost smiled.

## — Marcus —

**June 2033. Crystal Falls, Colorado. Day 2 of 2.**

Morning. The armored truck sat in the maintenance bay. Tommy had spent the previous evening checking every system. Diesel engine rebuilt in 2015, armor kit installed in the same year, reinforced axles from a military surplus find in 2022. The truck had made supply runs to Basalt and Willow Creek dozens of times. It had never gone east of the Kansas corridor.

Marcus loaded supplies into the truck bed while the compound woke up around him. Canned food for two weeks. Water purification tablets. Ammunition. Medical kit. Two spare tires. A radio with enough range to reach the Mississippi relay. Maps. The sixty-three-page retrieval file, waterproofed in a plastic sleeve.

Noah appeared at the maintenance bay door with a thermos of coffee and a paper bag.

"Lena made sandwiches," he said. "She doesn't know why. She just saw me in the kitchen and started making them."

"Lena makes food when she's worried. It's her thing."

"I didn't tell her anything."

"I know."

Noah set the thermos and bag on the tailgate. He stood there for a moment. Eighteen years old, lean and tall, with Frank's steady hands and Sarah's habit of building what he wanted to say before he said it.

"The route goes through Kansas," Noah said.

"Yeah."

"That's where I'm from. Kansas City."

Marcus stopped loading. Looked at his son. Noah met his eyes.

"I don't remember it," Noah said. "I was five. I remember the zoo shirt and the crawl space and you. That's it. Everything before that is gone."

"That's okay."

"I know it's okay. I'm not asking you to stop. I'm telling you that I know where you're driving through, and I'm fine." He paused. "Bring her home. That's the only thing that matters."

Marcus pulled him into a hug. Noah tolerated it for exactly three seconds before stepping back.

"The sandwiches have mustard. Lena puts mustard on everything now."

"Noted."

Noah walked back toward the carpentry shop. He didn't look back. Marcus watched him go and then finished loading the truck.

...

Sarah found him in the powerhouse office at noon. She had a single sheet of paper.

"Radio schedule," she said. "You check in every evening at 2100 through the Mississippi relay until you're out of range. After that, I'll be listening on the emergency frequency every morning at 0600 and every evening at 2100 in case you find a way to bounce a signal. If I don't hear from you in sixty days, I send Tommy to Topeka with a relay team to extend the network east."

"Sixty days is generous."

"Sixty days is realistic for a round trip with complications." She set the paper on the desk. "I built in extra."

Marcus looked at the schedule. Clean block letters. Time zones accounted for. Frequency rotation to avoid interception. Seven years of planning condensed into a single page.

"The federation meeting," he said.

"I'll handle it."

"The Basalt water rights."

"I'll handle it."

"If FEDRA sends another—"

"Marcus." She put both hands on the desk and leaned forward. "I have been running this settlement since I was fourteen years old. I managed fifty people before I could drive. I held the coalition together after the horde, after the FEDRA battle, after Dale and Hector died. I negotiated Glenwood Springs into the federation when Diane Pryor wouldn't return anyone else's radio calls. I built the governance structure that eighteen settlements use to coordinate defense, food production, and infrastructure across two hundred miles of mountain corridor."

She straightened up.

"I'll handle it."

Marcus picked up the radio schedule. Folded it. Put it in his jacket pocket.

"I know you will."

"Then stop listing things."

"Old habit."

"Break it." She came around the desk. Stood in front of him. Twenty years since a highway in Austin. A twelve-year-old girl bleeding on the ground. A stranger with bandages and no explanation. Everything since then, built from nothing, standing on a foundation of the single decision to stop and help.

"Come back," she said.

"I will."

"Come back with her. Come back with Joel. Come back and let me meet this girl you've been talking about for seven years."

"You'll like her. She's got a mouth on her."

"I already like her."

She kissed him. Then she straightened his jacket collar, the way she always did, a gesture so old and automatic that neither of them noticed it anymore.

"Go."

## — Joel —

**Dawn. June 2033.**

The truck idled at the south gate. Marcus behind the wheel. The engine rumbled low and steady, Tommy's work, a machine built to run.

Joel stood on the packed dirt with his pack at his feet. His rifle slung across his chest. The SIG in a hip holster. The same model Marcus had bought from the Mall on Day 3 and handed to him in an abandoned house while his daughter slept with a bandaged hip in the next room.

Different gun. Same weight.

Tommy was there. Sarah. Noah. Keiko had come down from the range without being asked. Helen stood by the gate controls.

Nobody made a speech. That wasn't how this family worked.

Tommy gripped Joel's forearm. Held it. His brother's hand, rough from twenty years of turbine work and wire splicing and pipe fitting. Calluses on calluses.

"Eight weeks," Tommy said.

"Eight weeks."

"Don't do anything I wouldn't do."

"That leaves a lot of room."

Tommy let go. He stepped back. Hands in his jacket pockets, the same way he'd stood twenty years ago when he'd backed Marcus's plan to head west instead of to a quarantine zone. The same instinct. Trust the play. Hold the home.

Sarah hugged Joel. She was thirty-two. Taller than she'd been at twelve, but she still fit under his chin the same way.

"Be careful, Dad."

"Always am."

"That's a lie and we both know it."

He kissed the top of her head. "I'll bring him back."

"You bring both of you back. Tommy already gave Marcus that speech. I'm giving it to you."

Joel picked up his pack. Threw it in the truck bed next to Marcus's. Walked to the passenger door.

He looked back once. The dam behind the compound, the greenhouse panels catching the early light, the school roof, the watchtowers. Twenty years of work from four people on a ridge with nothing but a seized turbine and a stranger's impossible supplies.

Six hundred and thirty-seven people in there. Waking up. Making coffee. Sending their kids to school. Arguing about tool storage and water rights and whose turn it was to clean the mess hall. Living.

Joel got in the truck.

Marcus put it in gear. The south gate opened. Helen raised a hand as they passed. The gravel road curved through the bottleneck and onto the access road that wound down the Crystal River valley.

The rearview mirror held Crystal Falls for about a mile. Then the road bent, the trees closed in, and it was gone.

"How far to the Kansas border?" Joel asked.

"Six hundred miles. Two days if the roads are clear."

Joel settled into the seat. Adjusted the rifle across his lap. Rolled down the window an inch, letting the morning air in. Pine and river water and distance.

"You got a plan for when we find her?" Joel asked. "Beyond 'explain everything and hope she doesn't run'?"

"Working on it."

"She's fourteen. Fourteen-year-olds don't listen to plans."

"You'd know."

"I raised Sarah. Sarah was reasonable. I get the feelin' this girl's a different kind of problem."

Marcus kept his eyes on the road. "She grew up in a quarantine zone. Military school. No parents. Marlene's been her guardian, more or less, but from a distance. The Fireflies are the closest thing she has to family, and they're the ones planning to kill her."

Joel was quiet for a while. The truck ate miles. The Crystal River valley opened up and the road straightened through the first stretch of federation territory. A patrol marker on a fence post. A cleared farmhouse with a federation flag.

"I know what that's like," Joel said. "Not the specifics. But the shape of it. Growin' up with people who are supposed to take care of you and realizin' they won't. That they need somethin' from you more than they need you."

"That's exactly what it's like for her."

"Then we don't lie to her. We don't promise her anything we can't deliver. We tell her the truth and we give her a choice. That's probably the first time anyone's done that."

The road crossed the river on Tommy's bridge. Steel and concrete, built in 2019, reinforced twice. The water was high from spring melt. Sunlight on the rapids.

"You thought about this," Marcus said.

"I've been thinkin' about it since you told me. Three years of thinkin'." Joel looked out the window. The mountains moving past. "I keep seein' Sarah at fourteen. What she was like. How she talked, how she carried herself. And I think about this girl who didn't have any of that. No dad. No home. No one who stayed."

He was quiet.

"I stayed," Joel said. "That's what I know how to do. I'm not good at speeches. I'm not good at explainin' things. But I know how to stay in a room with a scared kid and not leave. That's the whole thing, isn't it? Just bein' there."

"Yeah," Marcus said. "That's the whole thing."

The truck drove east. The mountains shrank behind them. The road opened up into the high plains, brown and wide and empty.

Two thousand miles ahead, a girl they'd never met was waiting for something she didn't know was coming.
