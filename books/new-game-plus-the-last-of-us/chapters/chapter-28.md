# Chapter 28: Below

## — Marcus —

Day fifty-seven. Morning.

Marcus sat on the dam walkway with a cup of coffee that Sarah had made, which meant it was drinkable, and studied the map overlay in the corner of his vision.

The five-mile radius around the compound was getting clean. Two months of clearing runs had thinned the infected population in the Crystal River valley to almost nothing. The town of Marble was empty. The trailheads north and south were clear. The ranch properties along the river had been swept twice. The orange dots that used to cluster on his map like a rash were down to scattered singles, lone Runners wandering through forest too far from anything to be worth the walk.

Except for one spot.

Seven miles northwest, past the end of the maintained road, up a drainage that climbed toward the ridgeline, there was a cluster of contacts. Five orange dots, grouped tight, not moving. They'd been there for three days. Marcus had noticed them on Day 55 and filed them as a future target. Now, with the closer zones cleared and points sitting at 587, it was time.

He pulled up the area map and zoomed. The cluster sat at an elevation marker near something labeled YM on the topographic overlay. An old mine. The Crystal River valley was full of them. Marble had been a quarrying town, and the surrounding hills were riddled with prospect holes and small mining operations that had been abandoned since the 1940s. Tommy had mentioned a few during infrastructure discussions, marking them as potential sources of scrap metal.

Five contacts, stationary, in a mine shaft. Probably Runners that had wandered in and gotten stuck. An easy hundred points.

Marcus finished his coffee and went to get his gear.

Joel was at the access road, fitting a crossbar to the gate with Carson. The gate was taking shape. Two posts sunk three feet into gravel, a swinging frame of scrap lumber reinforced with steel strapping that Frank had bent in his workshop. It wouldn't stop a vehicle. It would stop people from walking in without being seen.

"Clearing run," Marcus said. "Northwest, past the road end. Back by afternoon."

Joel looked up from the crossbar. "How far?"

"Seven miles. There's a cluster on the map near an old mine."

"Take somebody."

"Five contacts. Standard Runners. I'll be fine."

Joel's jaw worked. It was the face he made when he disagreed but didn't have a tactical reason to argue. Marcus had done a dozen solo runs. The math worked. Five Runners was a light morning.

"Back by three," Joel said. It wasn't a request.

"Back by three."

Marcus headed northwest.

---

The road ended at a washout two miles past the compound, where spring runoff had taken a twenty-foot bite out of the gravel surface and dropped it into the creek below. Beyond the washout, the drainage narrowed into a V-shaped canyon choked with spruce and dead aspen. The trail, if you could call it that, was a game path along the creek bank. Elk tracks in the mud. A few deer prints. No human footprints.

The morning was cold and overcast. Clouds sat on the ridgeline like a gray lid. The air smelled like wet pine and something else, something faint and organic that Marcus couldn't place. He'd learned to pay attention to smells. In the early days, the infected had been identifiable by sound. Clicking, groaning, the wet shuffle of feet on pavement. But the Enhanced Runner he'd fought at the fishing lodge had been quiet, and the Stalkers at the school had been worse than quiet. Smell was the backup system. Fungal growth had a specific odor, musty and sweet, like bread left in a bag too long.

He was smelling it now. Faint, but there.

The drainage climbed for another mile before opening into a small hanging valley. A flat bench of meadow grass, brown with autumn, bordered by rock outcrops on three sides. At the far end, cut into the base of a limestone cliff, was the mine entrance.

Marcus stopped at the tree line.

The mine opening was about eight feet wide and six feet tall, framed with old timber that had gone gray and soft with rot. A rusted ore cart sat on its side next to the entrance, half buried in dirt. A collapsed wooden structure, probably an old equipment shed, lay in pieces to the left. The remains of a road, two ruts in the meadow grass, led from the mine entrance down the drainage toward the washed-out road.

Three Runners were in the meadow.

They stood in the grass between the mine entrance and the tree line, spaced apart, not moving much. One was near the ore cart. One was at the edge of the collapsed shed. The third was in the middle of the meadow, facing uphill, swaying slightly in the breeze.

Marcus studied them through the trees. Standard Runners, all three. Orange on the map. The fungal growth on their faces was moderate, weeks old, the uneven patches of Cordyceps that covered the eyes and cheeks and turned the skin gray-white. Their clothes were outdoor gear. Hiking boots, fleece jackets, cargo pants. Weekend hikers or miners, maybe. People who'd been up here when the world ended and never came back down.

The other two contacts were inside the mine. The map showed them deeper, maybe fifty yards in, stationary.

Marcus drew the hunting knife and moved out of the tree line.

The first Runner was the one near the ore cart. It heard him at twenty yards and turned, the jerky head-snap that all Runners did when they locked on to sound. It charged. Marcus sidestepped, caught its shoulder, and drove the knife into the base of its skull. It dropped.

The second Runner came from the shed. Faster than the first. Marcus met it with the hatchet, a short swing that caught it in the temple. It went down sideways and didn't get up.

The third was the meadow Runner, and it was already running. Marcus let it close the distance, then pivoted and put the knife through its jaw from below. Ugly, but effective. The blade scraped bone. The Runner folded.

Three kills. Maybe forty seconds.

**[Runners Cleared: 3]**
**[Points Earned: 38]**
**[Total Points: 625]**

Marcus cleaned the knife on the meadow grass and looked at the mine entrance.

The smell was stronger here. Much stronger. Not the faint bread-mold scent from the drainage. This was thick and warm, a living smell, like sticking your head into a greenhouse where everything had gone wrong. Sweet rot and damp earth and something underneath that was almost animal.

The timber framing around the entrance was covered in fungal growth. Not the thin patches you saw on infected faces. Thick mats of it, pale yellow and orange, spreading across the wood in branching patterns that looked almost deliberate. Some of it had crept onto the rock walls of the cliff face. A tendril of growth ran along the ground from the entrance into the meadow, eight or nine feet long, like a root reaching for sunlight.

Marcus had seen fungal growth before. Every infested building, every Runner nest, every Stalker den had some version of it on the walls and floors. But this was different in scale. This was the kind of growth that happened when an infection had been in one place for a long time. Weeks. Months, maybe.

He checked the map. Two contacts inside. Orange dots, stationary, deep in the shaft.

"Just Runners," he told himself. "Stuck in a hole."

He didn't believe it.

---

The mine shaft went straight into the cliff for about thirty feet, then turned left and dropped. The floor was packed earth and loose rock, worn smooth by decades of foot traffic and ore carts. Old rail tracks, rusted to the color of dried blood, ran along the center of the floor and disappeared into the darkness.

Marcus had a flashlight. Mall-purchased, LED, good for four hours. He held it in his left hand and the hatchet in his right. The knife was on his belt. The Glock was in his waistband. He didn't usually bring the gun on clearing runs, but something about the mine had changed his mind at the last second, and he'd pulled it from the shelf before leaving.

The fungal growth was everywhere.

It covered the walls in sheets. It hung from the ceiling in curtains. It grew across the floor in ridged mats that crunched under his boots like walking on stale cereal. The color shifted as he went deeper, from the pale yellow of the entrance to darker orange, then to a brownish-red that looked wet even where it was dry. The air was warm. Not Colorado-in-October warm. Body warm. The kind of warm that came from biological processes happening in an enclosed space.

The smell was bad enough that he breathed through his mouth.

Thirty feet in, the shaft turned left and sloped downward at about fifteen degrees. The ceiling dropped to five feet. Marcus ducked and kept going. The rail tracks continued, running along the right wall now, and the floor was wet, a thin film of moisture that seeped from the fungal mats and pooled in the low spots.

The map showed the two contacts forty yards ahead. Still stationary. Still orange.

At fifty feet, he found the first one.

A Runner, wedged into an alcove cut into the left wall. It was standing upright, arms at its sides, head tilted forward, not moving. The fungal growth on its body was so thick that it had fused the Runner to the wall. Cordyceps covered its torso and legs like a cocoon, and tendrils of growth connected its shoulders and head to the rock behind it. The face was barely visible under the fungal mass. One eye socket was completely covered. The other held something that might have been an eye, or might have been a fruiting body.

It was alive. Marcus could see the chest moving, slow and shallow, one breath every ten or fifteen seconds. But it wasn't reacting to his flashlight or his footsteps. It was dormant. Rooted.

He killed it with the knife. The blade went in through the gap between the fungal mass and the skull, and the body shuddered once and went still. The Cordyceps didn't release. The Runner stayed fused to the wall, dead now, a permanent fixture of the mine shaft.

**[Runner Cleared: 1]**
**[Points Earned: 11]**
**[Total Points: 636]**

One contact left on the map. Deeper. Thirty yards ahead.

Marcus kept going.

The shaft opened up. The ceiling rose to eight feet, then ten, and the walls pulled back into a wider chamber. An intersection. Three tunnels branched off from the main shaft, two heading left and one heading right. Support timbers crossed the ceiling in an X pattern, old but still holding. A pile of rubble from a partial collapse blocked the left-center tunnel. The right tunnel was clear.

The last contact was in the right tunnel. Twenty yards in. Stationary.

And the dot on the map wasn't orange.

It was red.

Marcus stopped. He stared at the map overlay, at the single red dot pulsing in the right tunnel. He'd never seen a red contact before. Orange for Runners and Stalkers. The Enhanced Runner at the fishing lodge had been a brighter orange, almost amber. But this was red. Deep, saturated red.

The system pushed a notification into his vision.

**[Warning: Stage 4 Infected Detected]**
**[Classification: Bloater]**
**[Threat Level: EXTREME]**
**[Recommendation: Avoid engagement. Retreat to safe distance.]**

Marcus read the notification twice. His hands were sweating. The hatchet handle was slick against his palm.

A Bloater.

He'd fought them a hundred times. In the game. On a screen. With a controller in his hands, a regenerating health bar, and the knowledge that dying meant reloading from the last checkpoint. He'd learned their patterns. The slow, lumbering charge. The mycotoxin bombs they tore from their own bodies and threw. The armored fungal plates that covered their torso and limbs like natural body armor, thick enough to stop bullets, thick enough to break blades.

In the game, you fought them with molotovs, shotguns, and distance. You kept moving. You stayed out of grab range, because if a Bloater got its hands on you, it ripped your jaw apart. One grab, one death, every time.

"This isn't the game," Marcus said out loud.

His voice sounded wrong in the mine. Too loud. Too human.

He should leave. The system was telling him to leave. Walk back up the shaft, out into the meadow, back to the compound. Report the Bloater to Joel. Come back with backup, with a plan, with enough firepower to deal with a Stage 4 infected.

Except Joel didn't have the firepower to deal with a Bloater. Nobody at the compound did. They had hunting rifles, a few handguns, the shotgun Carson had brought. None of it would penetrate the fungal armor. You needed fire. Explosives. Heavy weapons. Things Marcus could buy from the Mall, but only if he had the points.

And the only way to get enough points for those things was to kill this thing.

Marcus checked his inventory. Hatchet. Hunting knife. Glock with twelve rounds. LED flashlight. No molotovs. No explosives. No fire.

"You're an idiot," he thought. "You walked into a mine with a hatchet to fight a Bloater."

He looked at the mine shaft around him. The intersection. The support timbers overhead. The partial collapse blocking the left-center tunnel. The rubble on the floor, chunks of limestone and old timber, some of them the size of his head.

The support timbers.

Marcus looked up. The X-pattern beams were old-growth pine, thick and solid, darkened with age but not rotten. They held the ceiling of the intersection, and the ceiling was fractured limestone, the kind of rock that was stable when supported and catastrophically unstable when it wasn't. The right tunnel, where the Bloater sat, had its own set of support beams at the entrance. Two verticals and a horizontal. A doorway, essentially. The oldest trick in mining disaster history.

If the Bloater was in the tunnel, and the tunnel entrance collapsed, and the Bloater was trapped under a few tons of limestone...

Marcus set the flashlight on a rock, angled toward the right tunnel. He holstered the hatchet and pulled the Glock.

He was going to need noise.

---

The first shot was the loudest thing Marcus had ever heard.

The Glock barked in the confined space and the sound slammed off the walls and came back doubled, tripled, a physical force that made his ears ring and his teeth ache. The bullet hit the rock wall inside the right tunnel and ricocheted with a high whine.

For two seconds, nothing happened.

Then the Bloater moved.

The sound came first. A deep, wet grinding, like boulders shifting in mud. Then footsteps. Not the quick slap of Runner feet. Heavy, deliberate impacts that shook loose dust from the ceiling. Each step sent a tremor through the floor that Marcus felt in his knees.

The flashlight beam caught movement in the right tunnel. A shape. Massive. Filling the tunnel from wall to wall, ducking under the ceiling, shoulders brushing rock on both sides. The fungal armor caught the light and threw it back in mottled patches of brown and gray-white, thick ridged plates that covered the torso and arms and face like the bark of a diseased tree.

The Bloater stepped into the intersection.

It was eight feet tall. Maybe eight and a half. The fungal growth had distorted the original human proportions into something that barely registered as a person anymore. The arms were too thick, the shoulders too wide, the head a lump of Cordyceps plates with a mouth-hole that opened and closed in a slow, rhythmic motion. No eyes. No nose. Just armor and a mouth and the slow, grinding movement of something that had been growing in the dark for months.

The smell hit Marcus like a wall. Sweet, rotten, thick enough to taste. His stomach lurched and he swallowed hard.

"Okay," he whispered. "Okay."

The Bloater turned toward him. Not fast. Bloaters weren't fast. But it was already between him and the exit shaft, which meant his plan of luring it into the right tunnel and collapsing the entrance behind it was backwards. The thing was out. In the open chamber. Moving toward him.

Marcus fired. Three rounds, center mass, as fast as he could pull the trigger.

The bullets hit the fungal plates and did nothing. Literally nothing. No flinch, no stagger, no indication that the Bloater had registered being shot. The rounds buried themselves in the armor and disappeared like pebbles thrown into mud.

The Bloater charged.

Slow, by Runner standards. But a Bloater's charge wasn't about speed. It was about mass. Eight hundred pounds of fungal armor and infected muscle, moving with the inevitability of a truck rolling downhill. The floor shook. Loose rocks jumped. A support beam groaned as the Bloater's shoulder clipped it.

Marcus ran.

He went left, toward the partially collapsed tunnel, ducking under the rubble pile and squeezing through a gap between fallen rock and the tunnel wall. The Bloater followed. It hit the rubble pile and didn't stop. Rocks shifted. Timbers cracked. The gap Marcus had squeezed through disappeared under a cascade of limestone chunks that the Bloater simply walked through.

The left tunnel was a dead end. Twenty feet of shaft ending in a solid rock face. A pickaxe lay rusted on the floor. A wooden crate, collapsed and empty.

Marcus turned. The Bloater filled the tunnel entrance behind him. It had to crouch to fit, and the crouching slowed it, and that bought Marcus exactly the amount of time it took to think one clear thought.

"Not this tunnel. The other one."

He needed it back in the right tunnel. Back where the support beams were intact and the ceiling was fractured and a collapse would actually work.

The Bloater reached for him. The arms were long, too long, and the hands were masses of fungal growth that ended in something like fingers, thick and blunt and strong enough to crush a skull. Marcus ducked, felt the hand brush the top of his head, and threw himself flat. He rolled between the Bloater's legs, a move that worked only because the thing was crouching and the gap between its knees and the floor was just wide enough for a human body.

The fungal mats on the floor were slick and warm against his back. Something crunched under his shoulder. He came up running, back into the intersection, flashlight still burning on its rock.

The Bloater turned. Slow. The crouch cost it time, and time was what Marcus needed.

He grabbed the flashlight and ran into the right tunnel. Ten feet. Fifteen. Twenty. Past the spot where the dormant Runner had been fused to the wall. Past a shallow alcove full of rusted tools. He stopped and turned the flashlight back toward the entrance.

The Bloater was coming.

It moved through the intersection with the grinding inevitability that made Bloaters the worst thing in the game. Not because they were fast. Because they didn't stop. They didn't get tired. They didn't lose interest. They came and they came and they came until you were dead or they were, and killing one with a hatchet and a pistol was not something the game had ever intended you to do.

The Bloater reached the right tunnel entrance. The two vertical support beams and the horizontal crossbeam framed it like a doorway, and the Bloater had to duck again to fit through. Its shoulder hit the left vertical. The beam shifted. Dust fell from the ceiling.

Marcus raised the Glock and fired.

Not at the Bloater. At the horizontal crossbeam.

The first round hit wood and blew a chunk out of the beam's center. The second hit two inches to the left. The third cracked the beam along its grain, a split that ran from the bullet holes to the bottom edge.

The Bloater was through the entrance now. Inside the tunnel. Moving toward Marcus. Twenty feet away. Fifteen.

Marcus fired twice more. Both rounds into the left vertical beam, at the joint where it met the ceiling.

The vertical cracked. The horizontal shifted. A slab of limestone the size of a dining table separated from the ceiling and dropped.

It hit the Bloater across the shoulders.

The thing staggered. For the first time since Marcus had seen it, the Bloater reacted to damage. It stumbled forward, driven to its knees by the weight of the stone, and a sound came out of its mouth that wasn't a roar or a scream. It was a deep, wet bellow, like air forced through a pipe full of mud.

More rock came down. The ceiling above the tunnel entrance was failing now, the fractured limestone breaking apart in sheets and slabs, crashing down in a cascade that filled the entrance with rubble and dust. The support beams buckled. The horizontal snapped in half and dropped into the growing pile of debris. The left vertical toppled sideways and disappeared under falling rock.

The Bloater tried to stand. A slab hit it across the back and drove it down again. Another hit its head, snapping the thick neck forward. Dust filled the tunnel and Marcus couldn't see, could only hear the grinding crash of rock on rock on fungal armor, the wet bellowing that was getting quieter, and the slow, terrible creak of a mine shaft rearranging itself.

Marcus backed up. Ten feet. Twenty. The dust was thick enough to choke on, and he pulled his shirt over his nose and breathed through the fabric.

The collapse lasted maybe fifteen seconds. It felt longer.

When the dust began to settle, the tunnel entrance was gone. A wall of broken limestone and shattered timber filled the space from floor to ceiling, eight feet thick at least. Somewhere behind it, the Bloater was buried.

But not dead.

Marcus could hear it. Movement, deep and grinding, under the rubble. Rock shifting. Something pushing. The red dot on his map was still there, still pulsing, dimmer maybe but not gone. Bloaters were tough. In the game, a building could fall on one and it would crawl out of the wreckage and keep coming.

He had to finish it.

Marcus looked at the rubble wall. Near the top, where the collapse hadn't fully sealed, there was a gap. Two feet wide, maybe eighteen inches tall. Not big enough to climb through. But big enough to see through, and big enough to reach through.

He climbed the rubble pile. Loose rocks shifted under his boots and he grabbed a protruding timber for balance. At the top, he shone the flashlight through the gap.

The Bloater was three feet away.

It was pinned under a slab of limestone that covered most of its lower body. One arm was free, clawing at the rock, the thick fungal fingers scraping grooves in the stone. The other arm was buried. Its head was turned toward the gap, the mouth-hole opening and closing, and Marcus could see the interior of the mouth, the pinkish-gray tissue that was the only part of a Bloater not covered in armor.

The mouth.

Marcus holstered the Glock. He had four rounds left and they wouldn't penetrate the skull plates. He drew the hunting knife.

He reached through the gap. The Bloater's free hand swiped at him and missed, the fingers closing on air six inches from his wrist. Marcus pulled back, waited for the arm to swing past, and reached again.

The knife went into the mouth.

He drove it deep, angling upward, feeling the blade scrape past something hard, fungal plate or bone or both, and then sink into something soft. The Bloater convulsed. The free arm thrashed, hitting the rubble, sending rocks tumbling. Marcus held the knife handle with both hands and twisted.

The Bloater's mouth closed on the blade. The pressure was enormous. Marcus felt the handle flex in his grip and for a terrible second he thought the thing was going to bite through the steel. Then the convulsing stopped. The arm dropped. The mouth relaxed.

The red dot on his map flickered and went out.

Marcus pulled the knife free. The blade was covered in something that wasn't blood, a thick grayish fluid that smelled like the inside of the mine concentrated into a single terrible moment. He sat on the rubble pile and breathed.

His hands were shaking. His shirt was soaked with sweat under the jacket. His ears were ringing from the gunshots and the collapse and the Bloater's bellowing. The flashlight beam trembled against the wall because his arms wouldn't hold still.

This was the crash. The real one. Not the controlled calm of the gravel bar fight, where adrenaline and system-enhanced reflexes had kept him steady through fifteen seconds of violence. This was the aftermath of being trapped in a mine with something that could have torn him in half, something he'd killed by the barest margin with a collapsing ceiling and a knife through the mouth.

He sat there for a while. He wasn't sure how long. Long enough for his hands to stop shaking and his breathing to even out and the ringing in his ears to fade to a low hum.

The system notification was waiting.

**[Bloater Eliminated]**
**[Base Points: 400]**
**[First Kill Bonus (Stage 4 — Bloater): +75]**
**[Tactical Kill Bonus (Environmental Collapse): +50]**
**[Total Points Earned: 525]**
**[Current Points: 1,161]**

**[Achievement Unlocked: Giant Killer — Defeat a Stage 4 Infected]**
**[Reward: Tier 3 Mall Access unlocked for all categories]**

Marcus stared at the numbers.

One thousand, one hundred and sixty-one points. More than he'd ever had. More than double the highest balance he'd carried since Outbreak Day. One Bloater was worth more than fifty Runners. More than a week of dedicated clearing runs.

And Tier 3 was unlocked.

He opened the Mall.

## — Marcus —

The Tier 3 catalog was a different world.

Tier 1 was survival. Food, bandages, basic tools. The things that kept you alive from one day to the next. Tier 2 was infrastructure. Generators, solar panels, building materials, weapons. The things that turned survival into settlement.

Tier 3 was civilization.

Marcus scrolled through the categories, sitting on the rubble pile in a collapsed mine shaft with a dead Bloater three feet behind him, and felt the same feeling he'd had when he first opened the Mall back at the ranch in Texas. The vertigo of possibility. The weight of choices that mattered.

**[Tier 3 — Medical Equipment]**

- Portable surgical kit (full): 300 points
- Portable X-ray unit: 250 points
- Defibrillator (AED, rechargeable): 150 points
- Autoclave (sterilization unit): 100 points

**[Tier 3 — Industrial Tools]**

- Arc welding station: 200 points
- Hydraulic press (bench-top): 350 points

**[Tier 3 — Advanced Infrastructure]**

- Water treatment system (modular): 400 points
- Electrical transformer (step-down, 240V): 200 points

**[Tier 3 — Defense]**

- Night vision goggles (Gen 2): 300 points
- Body armor (Level IIIA): 250 points

Marcus stopped scrolling at the medical category. He thought about Gene's blood pressure, managed with pills Marcus bought ten at a time from Tier 1. He thought about Helen's field dressing on his shoulder, competent but limited. He thought about the raider Joel had shot on the gravel bar, the one Helen had patched with gauze and hemostatic powder and then watched walk away with a wound that would probably kill him in a week.

He thought about Sarah's hip. About the bullet wound that had healed on the outside but left damage on the inside that no amount of points had been able to fix because he'd never had the tools to try.

The portable surgical kit was 300 points. The X-ray was 250. The defibrillator was 150. The autoclave was 100.

All four: 800 points.

That would leave him at 361. Low. Lower than he liked to sit. But the compound had food, water, power, and defenses that hadn't existed two weeks ago. The next clearing run would rebuild the balance. And the medical equipment would change what Helen could do. What anyone with training could do.

A real clinic. Not a first aid kit and a stack of textbooks. A clinic with an X-ray machine and surgical tools and a defibrillator that could restart a heart.

Marcus bought all four.

**[Purchase: Portable Surgical Kit — 300 points]**
**[Purchase: Portable X-Ray Unit — 250 points]**
**[Purchase: Defibrillator (AED) — 150 points]**
**[Purchase: Autoclave — 100 points]**
**[Total Spent: 800 points]**
**[Remaining Points: 361]**

The items materialized in his inventory. He couldn't carry them. Not down a mine shaft, not across seven miles of drainage and game trail. He'd cache them at the mine entrance and come back with Tommy and the truck. If the truck could make it past the washout. If not, he'd carry them in trips. Either way, the equipment was his.

Marcus climbed down from the rubble pile. His legs were stiff and his shoulder ached, the bullet wound from four days ago reminding him that it existed, though Helen would be surprised at how much it had healed. The tunnel behind the collapse was sealed. The Bloater was buried under several tons of limestone, dead, and it could stay there.

He walked out of the mine and into daylight.

The meadow was bright after the darkness of the shaft. Overcast, but bright. The three Runner bodies were where he'd left them, sprawled in the brown grass. The ore cart sat on its side. A bird, a Steller's jay, was sitting on the collapsed equipment shed, watching him with its head tilted.

Marcus sat down in the grass next to the ore cart and materialized the medical equipment.

Four cases appeared on the ground beside him. The surgical kit was in a hard-shell pelican case, olive drab, with latches and a foam interior. The X-ray unit was smaller than he'd expected, a portable digital system with a flat panel detector and a battery-powered emitter. The defibrillator was bright yellow, the kind you saw in airports and office buildings, with pictographic instructions on the front. The autoclave was a stainless steel cylinder about the size of a large pressure cooker.

He opened the surgical kit. Scalpels, hemostats, retractors, suture kits in sealed packets, a set of stainless steel instruments he couldn't name. A laminated reference card showed basic procedures. Everything was clean, sterile, packed in individual trays.

Helen would know what to do with all of it. Helen had eight years at Fort Carson and whatever medical training the Army Reserve gave its logistics officers. It wouldn't be surgeon-level, but it would be more than gauze and hemostatic powder. And the reference card was detailed enough that someone with steady hands and basic anatomy knowledge could follow it.

Marcus repacked the cases. He stacked them against the ore cart, covered them with the tarp from the collapsed shed, and marked the location on his map.

Then he started walking home.

## — Joel —

Joel saw Marcus come down the drainage trail at two-forty. Twenty minutes early. The boy looked tired, which was unusual, and dirty, which was normal, and he was walking with the loose-shouldered gait of someone who'd been carrying tension for hours and had finally let it go.

Joel watched from the gate. Carson was beside him, holding a post level while the concrete set. Carson had been steady today. Two days of axe work on the north ridge had burned off the worst of his gravel-bar anxiety, and the gate project was giving him something to build instead of something to fear.

"He looks rough," Carson said.

"He's fine."

Marcus came through the gate and stopped. He looked at Joel.

"I need Tommy and the truck tomorrow morning," he said. "There's equipment cached at a mine site seven miles northwest."

"Equipment."

"Medical. A surgical kit, an X-ray, a defibrillator, and a sterilizer."

Joel set down the wrench he'd been holding. He looked at Marcus the way he looked at most things Marcus said. With the careful attention of a man who'd stopped being surprised by impossible statements and had moved on to cataloging them.

"From the cache," Joel said.

"From the cache."

"A surgical kit."

"And an X-ray machine."

Carson's post level dipped sideways. He caught it.

Joel studied Marcus for a long moment. The boy was dirty. His boots were caked with something gray-white and organic that Joel recognized. Fungal growth. Heavy fungal growth, the kind you only found in places where infection had been sitting for months.

"What'd you find up there?" Joel asked.

"A Bloater."

The word sat in the air between them. Joel knew what a Bloater was. Not from games. From experience. Twenty years before, in the early chaos, he'd seen one from a distance in Pittsburgh. A FEDRA squad had engaged it with automatic weapons and a grenade launcher. Three soldiers died. The Bloater took eleven minutes to kill.

"You fought a Bloater," Joel said. Not a question.

"I collapsed a mine shaft on it. Then I finished it with the knife."

Joel's mouth opened. Closed. He looked at Marcus's hands. Dirty. Intact. No visible injuries beyond the shoulder wound he'd come home with four days ago.

"Alone."

"Alone."

Joel took a breath. Behind him, Carson was very still, holding the post level with both hands and staring at Marcus like he was seeing him for the first time. Which, in a way, he was. The gravel bar had been seven men with guns. This was something else entirely.

"You're tellin' me you killed a Stage 4 infected by yourself in a mine shaft with a knife."

"And a structural collapse. The knife was just the last part."

Joel ran his hand across his jaw. He looked at the gate. At the compound beyond it. At the mountains where his daughter lived and worked and made food charts and organized patrol schedules.

"Medical equipment," Joel said. "That's what you went up there for."

"No. I went up there for a standard clearing run. The Bloater was a surprise. But the points from killing it were enough to upgrade our medical capabilities. Helen can set up a clinic. A real one."

Joel picked up the wrench. He turned back to the gate post. He tightened a bolt that didn't need tightening.

"Tommy'll have the truck ready at seven," he said.

"Thanks."

Marcus walked toward the residence. Joel watched him go.

Carson cleared his throat. "Did he say a Bloater?"

"Yeah."

"Those are the big ones. The ones with the armor."

"Yeah."

Carson looked at the post level in his hands. He adjusted it. The post was perfectly straight. He adjusted it again.

"Helen's going to want to hear about the medical stuff," Carson said.

"She will."

"That's... that's good. For the community."

"It is."

Carson nodded slowly. He set the level against the post and picked up the next piece of crossbar lumber. His hands were steady.

Joel went back to work. The gate was taking shape. The concrete was setting. The sun was behind the clouds and the air was cold and the dam hummed its constant low note, and somewhere inside the residence, Marcus was telling Sarah about a Bloater in a mine and a surgical kit that had come from a cache that didn't exist.

The compound had a clinic now. Or it would, by tomorrow. An X-ray machine, a defibrillator, surgical tools. Things that belonged in hospitals, not in mountain settlements with fourteen people and a pine table in the kitchen.

Joel tightened the bolt. He thought about Sarah. About the bullet wound in her hip that had healed on the outside and left damage on the inside. About the diagnosis Marcus had found in a textbook at four in the morning, back on the road, back when there was nothing anyone could do about it.

Maybe now there was something someone could do.

Joel didn't say it out loud. He didn't need to. The thought was enough. The possibility was enough.

He picked up the next bolt and kept working.
