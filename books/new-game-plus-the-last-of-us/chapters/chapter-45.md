# Chapter 45: Two Roads

## — Sarah —

They left at dawn, three of them in the flatbed truck with the patched canvas cover and the engine that sounded like it was clearing its throat every time Tommy's splice in the exhaust manifold caught a bump.

Sarah drove. Helen Park sat shotgun with a clipboard and the settlement census data. Paul Crane rode in the back with a rifle and a thermos of Lena's chicory blend, keeping watch on the road behind them.

Glenwood Springs was thirty-two miles northwest. Two hours on roads that hadn't been maintained since 2014. Highway 133 to Carbondale, then west on 82 through the canyon.

"What do we know about their leader?" Helen asked.

"Diane Pryor. Former FEDRA district administrator out of Grand Junction. Left the QZ sometime in 2019 with about thirty people. Built Glenwood Springs up from whatever was there."

"Why'd she leave?"

"That's the question."

The road north was empty. No infected contacts on Marcus's map within twenty-five miles, and they'd be beyond his range within the hour. Sarah felt the absence like a pressure change. For eight years, Marcus's map had been the safety net under everything. Outside its range, they were just people on a road.

The canyon walls rose around them as they turned west onto 82. Red rock and scrub pine. The Colorado River ran brown and fast in the gorge below, swollen with spring snowmelt. A billboard for whitewater rafting hung at an angle from its frame, one corner touching the ground. ADVENTURE AWAITS. The exclamation point had fallen off.

They reached the outskirts of Glenwood Springs just after eight.

---

The town looked different from what Sarah had expected.

Glenwood Springs had been a tourist town before the outbreak. Hot springs, ski access, hotels. Sarah had pictured something like Crystal Falls in its early days. Makeshift shelters, garden beds, a perimeter of scavenged wire.

What she found was a functioning town.

The main street had been cleared of vehicles and debris. Buildings on either side showed signs of repair. Glass in the windows, patched roofs, painted addresses. A water tower on the hill above town flew a blue flag she didn't recognize. The intersection at Grand Avenue had a manned checkpoint with a proper gate. Two people with rifles and what looked like a standardized uniform. Grey shirts, black armbands.

"Uniforms," Helen said quietly. "That's FEDRA-trained."

The gate guards were polite and professional. They asked for names, purpose, and weapons count. Sarah answered, handed over their rifle as a gesture of good faith. Helen's expression suggested she did not agree with this gesture. They were directed to a building three blocks south.

The building was the old City Hall. Clean steps, swept porch, a hand-painted sign that read GLENWOOD SPRINGS COMMUNITY COUNCIL. Below it, smaller: NO TRIBUTE. NO REGISTRATION. SELF-GOVERNED.

Sarah noted the sign. Three phrases that were a direct response to FEDRA's demands. Diane Pryor had built her settlement as an anti-FEDRA statement.

This was going to be complicated.

---

Pryor met them in the council chamber. A conference room on the second floor with a long table, eight chairs, and windows that looked out over Grand Avenue.

She was in her fifties. Short grey hair, reading glasses on a chain around her neck, posture that said military or bureaucratic or both. She wore the same grey shirt as the gate guards, but without the armband. A notebook sat in front of her, closed.

Two other people flanked her. A younger man with sunburned arms who was introduced as "Ben, our agricultural lead," and a woman named Faye who handled security and said nothing at all.

"Mrs. Webb." Pryor stood and offered a handshake. Firm. Practiced.

"Sarah is fine."

"Sarah, then. I know why you're here. Kate Schultz told us about your coalition. Please sit."

They sat. Helen opened her clipboard. Sarah left hers closed. She'd learned years ago that leading with paperwork made people defensive.

"I appreciate you meeting with us," Sarah said.

"I appreciate the advance notice. Your runner arrived three days ago. We don't get many visitors who announce themselves first." She paused. "Usually they just show up with demands."

"We don't have demands."

"I know what Crystal Falls has. Three hundred and fifty people, a working dam, an armored vehicle, and a man who killed two hundred infected in a single afternoon." She leaned back. "That's a lot of capability to show up at my gate without demands."

Sarah let the statement sit for a moment. Pryor was testing. Feeling for what Crystal Falls actually wanted.

"The coalition is a mutual defense and intelligence-sharing network. No tribute, no governance changes, no authority over your internal affairs. Each settlement patrols its own territory and reports unusual infected activity. If someone finds a Beacon, we coordinate a response."

"Define 'coordinate.'"

"We share information. If your patrols spot a cluster that's growing instead of dispersing, you send a runner to Crystal Falls and Basalt. We assess whether it's a Beacon. If it is, we deal with it before it matures into a horde."

"And 'we' means your husband."

"Marcus is the most effective person we have for Beacon kills, yes."

Pryor took off her glasses and set them on the table. "I left Grand Junction because Colonel Briggs ran the QZ like a feudal state. Tribute, registration, mandatory labor. Any settlement that contacted the QZ was given two options: join or be designated hostile. Three years ago, FEDRA sent envoys to your community with the same playbook."

"We turned them down."

"I know. I was still in Grand Junction when Whitcomb reported back. Briggs was furious. He called Crystal Falls a 'rogue settlement.'" She paused. "Six months later, I left. Took everyone who wanted to come. Because if a town of two hundred people could tell FEDRA no and survive, the system was already broken."

Sarah hadn't known that. Crystal Falls' refusal of FEDRA had rippled outward in ways they'd never tracked.

"So you understand why we don't want centralized authority," Sarah said.

"I understand why you say you don't want it. But Crystal Falls has one person who can single-handedly destroy a Beacon and its horde. That's not a coalition. That's a dependency. You're offering protection in exchange for information. The moment we rely on your protection, we lose the ability to refuse anything else you ask for."

Helen shifted beside Sarah. She could feel Helen wanting to respond, to lay out the data, the threat assessments, the Beacon projections. Sarah touched her arm under the table. Not yet.

"You're right," Sarah said.

Pryor blinked.

"If you join the coalition and a Beacon appears in your territory, you'll need Marcus to kill it. And that gives us leverage we didn't earn and you didn't choose. I won't pretend otherwise."

The room was quiet. Ben's sunburned arms were folded. Faye's expression hadn't changed since they sat down.

"Then what exactly are you offering?" Pryor asked.

"Start smaller. No coalition membership. Just an information channel. Your patrols report infected patterns to us. Ours report to you. If either side detects a Beacon, the other is notified. No obligation to act on the information. No mutual defense commitment. No visits from Marcus unless you specifically request one."

"And in return?"

"The same. We share our intel with you. Including what we've learned about how Beacons work, how to identify them early, and what happens when you don't."

Pryor picked up her glasses. Put them back on. Opened her notebook.

"Tell me about the Beacons."

Sarah talked for twenty minutes. Carbondale. Snowmass. The horde. The pattern of signal range and infected accumulation. The timeline from seeding to maturity. Pryor listened, asked sharp questions, took notes in tight handwriting. Ben's arms uncrossed halfway through. Faye asked one question: "How far can the signal travel?"

"We don't know yet," Sarah said. "The Snowmass Beacon pulled infected from at least forty miles. Maybe further."

Faye looked at Pryor. Something passed between them. A conversation in glances that Sarah recognized from her own council. Years of working together compressed into a look.

"I want to think about it," Pryor said. "I'll give you an answer within the week."

Sarah stood. "That's all I'm asking."

---

They ate lunch in the community mess hall. Bean soup, corn bread, and fresh greens from a greenhouse that Ben walked them through on the way. The hot springs, it turned out, provided natural geothermal heating for two greenhouses. Glenwood Springs was growing food year-round.

Helen took notes on everything. Crop yields, building layouts, the water system, the population breakdown. Pryor allowed it without comment. Letting Crystal Falls see what Glenwood Springs had built was its own argument. We don't need you. We're doing fine.

And they were. A hundred and twenty-three people, self-sufficient, well-organized, running on competence and the stubborn belief that nobody should have power over anyone else. Sarah respected it. She also knew it wouldn't survive a Beacon horde.

Paul was waiting at the truck. Nobody had touched it. The rifle was returned. The gate guards waved them through.

On the drive back through the canyon, Helen spoke first.

"She's going to say yes."

"You think?"

"She asked about the signal range. That's not a philosophical question. That's a 'how much time do I have' question. She's scared of the Beacons. She just doesn't want to look like she joined because she was scared."

Sarah shifted gears as the truck climbed the grade. "Then we give her a reason that isn't fear."

"Such as?"

"She's growing food year-round with geothermal heat. We're not. Basalt isn't. Information sharing goes both ways. If she teaches us the greenhouse technique, she's contributing, not just receiving. That changes the power dynamic."

Helen wrote something on her clipboard. "Smart."

"That's why they pay me the big bucks." Nobody got paid anything. Helen snorted anyway.

---

## — Marcus —

They'd left Crystal Falls at the same hour, heading the opposite direction.

Four of them. Marcus, Joel, Dale Briggs, and Ava Huang, who'd transferred from watch rotation to scouting three weeks ago and hadn't stopped asking tactical questions since. Dale carried the long-range radio Tommy had rigged from salvaged parts. Ava carried a pack with three days of food and a notebook she was filling with terrain observations. Joel carried a rifle, a handgun, and the particular silence of a man who preferred forests to meetings.

The plan was simple. Fifty miles north, following the Fryingpan River valley past Basalt, through the Aspen corridor, and into territory nobody from Crystal Falls had scouted. Deep country. Unknown infected density. The scout unit's first real test.

Joel set the pace. Not fast, not slow. The pace of a man who'd been walking through dangerous country for eight years and understood that arriving tired was worse than arriving late.

"Rules," Joel said, two miles out. "Nobody splits off alone. Nobody engages without my say. If I tell you to run, you run. Questions."

"What if we find a Beacon?" Dale asked.

"Then we mark it, count the contacts, and go home. We're scouts, not a kill team."

"What if it finds us?"

Joel looked at Marcus. Marcus shrugged.

"Then we improvise," Joel said.

They followed the river north through country that had been empty for years. Abandoned ranches with collapsed fences. A gas station with a tree growing through the roof. The road was cracked and buckled where frost heaves had done eight winters of damage. Wildflowers grew in the cracks. Purple and yellow.

Marcus ran the system map continuously. Zero contacts for the first fifteen miles. The Snowmass clearing and the horde battle had emptied the near valley more thoroughly than he'd realized.

"Quiet," Ava said.

"Too quiet?" Dale said.

"Just quiet. I've never been this far out."

"Stick close," Joel said.

They camped that night at a Forest Service cabin eighteen miles north of Basalt. No infected within map range. Joel built a small fire in the cabin's stove, keeping the smoke to a minimum. The night sky above the valley was enormous. No light pollution. No settlement glow. Just stars and the black outline of mountains and a coyote working the ridge.

"How far tomorrow?" Dale asked.

"Another twenty-five miles," Marcus said. "We'll be past Aspen by noon. After that, the map is blank."

Joel poked the fire. "I've been out that far."

Marcus looked at him.

"2015. Before we had the vehicle. Tommy and I walked the valley north lookin' for dam sites. Got about forty miles past Aspen, up near the old Minturn turnoff on I-70. There was a resort town up there. Big hotels, lodge, golf course." He paused. "Lot of infected. We turned around."

"How many?"

"Didn't count. Enough that I didn't want to find out."

Marcus filed that. A resort town on I-70, forty miles past Aspen, with a significant infected presence six years ago. If a Beacon had seeded there early, it had been growing since before Crystal Falls existed.

"That's where we're heading," Marcus said.

"Figured."

---

They reached the resort by mid-afternoon the next day.

The approach was wrong from two miles out.

Marcus had seen Beacon signatures before. Carbondale was a tight cluster in a single building. Snowmass was a radial pattern across a complex. Both had clear centers of gravity. Dense core, infected spreading outward.

This was different.

The map showed contacts scattered across a wide area. A resort village, maybe a quarter mile across, with infected distributed through buildings, parking structures, access roads, and the surrounding tree line. No obvious center. No dense core.

And the outer ring had contacts that were moving.

Not wandering. Moving. In patterns. Regular loops, overlapping coverage. Like patrol routes.

"Joel." Marcus described the map layout in detail. Joel couldn't see the system interface, but eight years of working together had built a shared language for this.

"Sentries," Joel said.

"You've seen this before?"

"In people. Never in infected."

They set up an observation point on a ridge three hundred yards south of the village. Ava watched through binoculars while the others stayed low. The resort was mid-sized. A main lodge, two condo buildings, a recreation center with a pool that had turned green and overflowed into the parking lot years ago. Heavy timber, river stone, broken glass. The architecture of mountain luxury slowly returning to mountain.

"I've got movement," Ava said. "Southeast corner. Something walking the tree line."

Marcus checked the map. One of the six outer contacts. Moving along the eastern perimeter in a smooth, consistent path. Not the jerky shuffle of a Runner. Not the cautious creep of a Stalker. Smooth. Purposeful.

"Let me see."

Ava handed over the binoculars.

The infected was at the tree line, maybe two hundred yards away. It looked like a Clicker at first glance. Fungal growth across the face, splitting the skull into thick dark plates. But the movement was wrong. Clickers walked with their heads tilted, constantly echolocating, bumping into obstacles. This one walked straight. Head level. It moved between trees without touching them.

And it was silent. No clicking. No breathing that carried. The thing covered ground without disturbing a branch.

"That's new," Marcus said.

"What is it?" Joel asked.

"I don't know yet."

They watched for twenty minutes. The six outer contacts completed their patrol loops and started again. The pattern was consistent. Each one covered a section of the perimeter, overlapping slightly with the adjacent patrol. If one was killed, the gap wouldn't go unnoticed for long.

The Beacon, wherever it was, had organized a perimeter defense. Not just gathered infected around it. Evolved them. Assigned roles. Built a security system out of bodies and fungus.

"I want to get closer," Marcus said.

"No," Joel said.

"I need to know what those things are."

"You need to not die. We're four people, thirty miles from home, with one radio and enough ammo for maybe twenty infected. If those patrols raise an alarm, we've got twenty-two more comin' from the buildings."

"One of them. Just one. I circle east, intercept the one at the tree line, and pull it away from the perimeter. Quick kill, no noise."

Joel looked at him for a long time. The look that weighed risk against information, survival against knowledge, the thing he wanted to do against the thing he should do.

"Dale stays here with Ava. She covers you with the rifle from the ridge. I'm comin' with you."

"Joel."

"Not optional."

Marcus didn't argue. Eight years of Joel had taught him when the man's mind was set.

---

They circled east through the tree line, staying below the ridgeline. The ground was soft with old pine needles. Quiet footing. Marcus tracked the nearest patrol contact on his map. It was completing its southern loop, turning east.

Three hundred yards. Two hundred. One hundred.

Joel stopped. Raised a fist. Marcus stopped behind him.

The infected came through the trees forty yards ahead. Up close, the differences were obvious. The fungal plating was denser than a standard Clicker's, darker, almost black. The growths weren't random. They followed the skull's contours precisely, like armor that had been fitted rather than grown. Its eyes were sealed over with plates, but it navigated the forest floor without stumbling, without sweeping its head for echolocation. It moved through the terrain like it could feel every root and stone before its foot touched them.

It was listening to the ground.

Marcus understood. The mycelium. The Beacon's root network ran underground, threading through soil and rotting wood and the root systems of living trees. This infected was walking on the network. It felt vibrations through the fungal mat beneath the forest floor. Didn't need eyes. Didn't need echolocation. It felt the forest itself.

Joel raised his rifle. Marcus put a hand on the barrel and pushed it down.

"I need it close," he whispered.

Joel kept the rifle down. His expression said everything his mouth didn't.

Marcus waited. The infected completed its turn and headed north along the tree line, its back to them. He moved. Fast, quiet, closing the forty yards in seconds. Knife in hand. He hit the infected from behind, driving the blade through the back of the skull where the plating was thinnest, into the brain stem.

The thing didn't go down.

It twisted. Faster than any Clicker Marcus had fought. An arm came around and caught his jacket, the strength behind it pulling him off balance. The infected turned to face him with a mouth full of fungal teeth that split apart in segments.

Marcus ripped free, tearing his jacket sleeve, and drove the knife in again. Side of the head. Through the plate, through bone, into the cortex. The infected shuddered. Its grip loosened. It fell.

**[+72 Points]**
**[New Enemy Class Identified: Sentry]**
**[Classification: Stage 3c — Beacon-Integrated Patrol Unit]**
**[Enemy Class Progress: 8/8]**
**[First Kill Bonus: +40 Points]**

**[Tier 5 Progress Updated: 76%]**

Eight of eight.

Marcus stared at the notification. Eight years, six stages of infected, two evolved variants, and now this. The system's checklist for Tier 5 had a line through one of its three requirements.

Joel appeared at his shoulder, rifle up, scanning the trees.

"Quiet?" Joel asked.

Marcus checked the map. The five remaining Sentries continued their patrols. The contacts inside the buildings hadn't moved. No alarm raised. The kill had been clean enough that the mycelium network hadn't registered the loss yet. Or the gap in the patrol route hadn't been noticed.

"Quiet. For now."

"Good. What was it?"

"The system calls it a Sentry. Stage 3c. Beacon-integrated patrol unit." He looked at the body. The dark plating. The sealed eyes. The feet, which he now noticed had fungal growths extending from the soles into the soil, thin tendrils that had snapped when the thing fell. "It was connected to the mycelium network underground. It could feel us through the ground."

Joel processed this the way Joel processed everything. Silence, then the practical question.

"So the Beacon has eyes."

"Not eyes. Feet. It uses these things as sensors. Walking perimeter patrols connected to the root system. If one detects movement, the Beacon knows where."

"And if we'd set off an alarm?"

"Every infected in that village would have known exactly where we were."

Joel looked at the resort below them, at the buildings and the parking structures and the twenty-two contacts inside. He looked at the body on the ground. He looked at the five remaining patrol routes on Marcus's description.

"We're leavin'," he said.

Marcus didn't argue.

---

They pulled back to the ridge, collected Dale and Ava, and moved south until they were well clear of the patrol perimeter. Marcus filed a mental report. Location. Contact count. Twenty-two standard infected inside, six Sentries on patrol. One Beacon, location uncertain but likely in the main lodge, based on the radial pattern. The Beacon had been growing for years, possibly since 2016 or 2017. Its mycelium network extended underground across the entire village and into the surrounding forest. It had evolved purpose-built patrol infected that used the root network as a sensory web.

This wasn't a nest. It was a fortress.

"Can you take it?" Dale asked as they walked.

"Not with four people."

"With more?"

"Maybe. The Sentries change the approach. You can't sneak up on this one. The ground itself is the alarm system. You'd have to cross the mycelium network to reach the Beacon, and the moment you touch it, everything in the village wakes up."

Joel cleaned his rifle as they walked, a habit that didn't require him to slow down. "We burn it."

Marcus looked at him.

"Mycelium's organic. It burns. We set the tree line on fire upwind and let it roll through the village. Kills the network, blinds the Sentries, forces everything into the open. Then we deal with whatever comes out."

"That's a lot of fire in a mountain valley in May."

"Controlled burn. Tommy can rig it. He did wildfire mitigation for the Forest Service before the army." Joel paused. "It's how you fight somethin' that uses the ground. You take the ground away."

Marcus filed that too. Joel's idea was blunt and dangerous and probably the right answer.

"We'll bring it to the council," Marcus said.

"Damn right we will."

They started south. Thirty miles to Crystal Falls. The afternoon sun dropped behind the western ridge and the temperature fell and they walked through country that was quiet and green and full of things growing underneath that nobody could see.

---

## — Sarah —

She was waiting at the gate when they got back.

Marcus looked tired. Joel looked the same as always, which meant he was also tired but would rather collapse than show it. Dale and Ava were quiet in the way people got quiet after seeing something that rearranged how they understood the world.

"How'd it go?" she asked.

"Found the Beacon." Marcus dropped his pack on the ground. "It's worse than Snowmass."

"How much worse?"

"It has guards. Dedicated infected that patrol the perimeter, connected to the fungal network underground. The system classified them as a new enemy type. Sentries. Stage 3c."

"So you got the eighth class."

"Yeah. Eight of eight. Tier 5 progress is at seventy-six percent."

Sarah pulled the pencil from behind her ear. "How many infected total?"

"Twenty-eight confirmed. Probably more inside that we couldn't see from the ridge. The Beacon's been growing for years. It's built into the terrain."

She wrote the number down. Added it to the file in her head. Two Beacons killed, a third located and fortified, an unknown number still out there beyond the map's range.

"Glenwood Springs?" Marcus asked.

"Pryor's smart. She's cautious. She didn't say yes."

"She say no?"

"She said she'd think about it. Which is the same thing she said to FEDRA before she left Grand Junction. So I'm choosing to interpret that positively."

Marcus smiled. The tired kind.

Joel walked past them toward the lodge. "I need coffee and a surface to draw on. I've got a plan for that Beacon and it involves fire."

"Dinner first," Sarah said.

"Coffee first."

"Dinner."

Joel stopped. Turned. "You are your father's daughter, you know that?"

"I'm everyone's daughter. Go eat."

He went.

Marcus and Sarah stood at the gate. The sun was behind the mountains and the valley was blue and cold and quiet. Three hundred and forty-nine people going about their evenings. The smell of cooking from the mess hall. A dog barking near the chicken coop. Captain, probably. Chasing things he'd never catch.

"Two roads," Sarah said.

"What?"

"Us and them. Today was two roads. I talked. You fought. Both trying to solve the same problem from different ends."

"Did we solve it?"

"We'll see. Pryor needs a week. Joel needs a map and more coffee than we have. But the pieces are moving."

Marcus put his arm around her shoulders. She leaned into it. His jacket was torn on the left sleeve. Again.

"I liked that jacket," she said.

"I know."

"You go through jackets faster than Noah goes through crayons."

"That's because Noah doesn't fight Sentries."

"Yet."

They went inside. Tomorrow there would be briefings and plans and council debates and maps spread across the pine table. Joel would argue for fire and Tommy would argue for something more controlled and Sarah would make the schedule and Helen would take notes and the work would continue the way it always continued, one problem at a time, one solution at a time, one day at a time.

But first, dinner. And a new notebook Marcus had bought her from the Mall.

Fourteen points.

"Frivolous," she said.

"You're welcome."
