# Chapter 11: The Shopping List

## — Tommy —

The kid was doing the eye thing again.

Tommy sat with his back against the brick wall of the auto repair shop they'd bedded down in, the Remington across his knees, watching the parking lot and the county road beyond it through the open bay door. Two in the morning, give or take. No watch. No phone. Just the moon and the feel of the air and twenty years of waking up at odd hours in Army barracks that had trained his internal clock to something close to reliable.

Marcus was sitting cross-legged on a sleeping bag ten feet away, facing the wall. His eyes were open. They moved. Left to right, up and down, pausing, flicking, like he was reading a newspaper that Tommy couldn't see. His fingers twitched sometimes. Small motions. Tapping things that weren't there.

He'd been at it for over an hour.

Tommy had seen a lot of strange things in ten days. The world ending was strange. Walking through empty towns where the mail was still in the mailboxes was strange. A twelve-year-old girl eating candy while her father washed blood off his hands in a creek was strange in a way that sat in his stomach like bad water.

But Marcus was a different kind of strange. The kind that didn't fit into any box Tommy had, and Tommy had a lot of boxes.

He'd watched Marcus produce a water bottle from thin air on Day Three. Just reached down beside his pack and picked up a bottle that hadn't been there a second before. Tommy had been three feet away. He'd checked the angle. No pocket, no sleeve, no trick. The bottle appeared.

Joel had seen it too. Joel had chosen not to ask. That was Joel's way. If the strange thing was useful and the strange person wasn't a threat to Sarah, Joel filed it under "deal with later" and kept walking. Joel had a whole filing cabinet of "deal with later."

Tommy was more of an "ask now" person. But he hadn't asked, because every time he started to form the question in his head, another part of his brain pointed out that Marcus had saved Sarah's life, kept them fed for ten days, and hadn't asked for a single thing in return. And in Tommy's experience, people who did that either wanted something big or genuinely didn't want anything at all, and the second type was rare enough that you didn't scare them off with interrogations.

So he watched. The eye thing. The tapping. The way Marcus sometimes smiled at whatever he was seeing, a quick, private grin that disappeared as fast as it came, like a kid in a candy store who'd just found the good shelf.

Marcus was on that shelf right now. Whatever invisible thing he was reading, it was making him happy. Or at least excited, which in the last ten days was close enough to happy to count.

Tommy checked the parking lot. Empty. Checked the road. Empty. Checked the sky for smoke or light or any of the other signs that meant trouble. Nothing. Just stars and the faint yellow glow of the moon through thin clouds and the sound of Joel snoring softly in the office at the back of the shop, where he'd dragged a couch cushion onto the floor and fallen asleep with one hand on Sarah's shoulder and the other on the SIG.

Tommy looked back at Marcus. The kid was grinning again.

"What the hell are you lookin' at?" Tommy said. Quiet. Not hostile. Just curious.

Marcus blinked. The grin vanished. He turned his head and seemed to need a second to remember where he was, and who was talking, and that there were other people in the room who couldn't see the newspaper.

"Nothing," Marcus said.

"Uh-huh."

"Really."

"You been staring at nothing for an hour and smilin' about it. Must be some real good nothing."

Marcus looked at him. In the dark, with just the moonlight through the bay door, his face was hard to read. But Tommy had been reading faces in bad light for fifteen years and what he saw wasn't guilt or deception. It was the look of someone sitting on a secret that was too big to keep and too dangerous to share.

"I can't explain it," Marcus said. "Not yet."

"That's what you said last time."

"I know."

"You gonna keep sayin' that?"

"Probably, yeah."

Tommy shifted the Remington on his knees. The barrel was cold. He'd cleaned it after the crossroads, the way he'd been trained, the way you always cleaned your weapon after you fired it whether you wanted to or not. He'd been thinking about the crossroads all night. About the sound his shot made hitting the big man's shoulder. About Sarah at the window. About Joel's face.

But that was done. That was filed. Right now, two in the morning in an auto repair shop on a county road in the Hill Country, the thing that mattered was the kid sitting ten feet away who could produce supplies from nothing and read invisible menus in the dark and somehow knew exactly where the infected were before anyone else did.

"Can I ask you something else?" Tommy said.

"Sure."

"Colorado. The dam idea. You brought that up back at the farmhouse. Before any of us said a word about long-term plans. You already had a destination in mind."

"Yeah."

"Why a dam?"

Marcus was quiet for a moment. Then he turned fully toward Tommy, sitting cross-legged, and the look on his face shifted. Less guarded. More engaged. Like someone had asked him the right question for the first time in days.

"Because electricity changes everything," Marcus said.

## — Marcus —

He'd been browsing the Mall for ninety minutes and his brain was on fire.

Not the bad kind. The good kind. The kind he used to get at three in the morning when a game revealed a new tech tree or a crafting system opened up and suddenly the thing he thought was a survival game was actually a base-builder with survival elements, and the whole strategic landscape shifted under his feet like tectonic plates.

The Mall had tiers. He'd known that since the farmhouse. Tier 1 was survival basics. Tier 2 was practical hardware. He'd been living in Tier 2 for a week, buying ammo and food and water purification tablets, spending points on the daily necessities of keeping four people alive on an eight-hundred-mile walk.

But tonight, with 647 points in the bank and nothing trying to kill him for the first time in forty-eight hours, he'd scrolled past the stuff he knew and into the stuff he didn't.

**[MALL — Tier 2: Infrastructure & Construction]**

**[Portable Generator (3.5kW, gasoline): 180 points]**
**[Solar Panel Kit (200W, with charge controller): 250 points]**
**[Lumber Bundle (pressure-treated, 50 boards): 85 points]**
**[Concrete Mix (20 bags, 80lb each): 120 points]**
**[Hand Tools Set (carpentry, full kit): 45 points]**
**[Roofing Materials (asphalt shingles, 10 sq): 60 points]**
**[Chain Link Fencing (100ft roll + posts): 95 points]**
**[Barbed Wire (500ft spool): 30 points]**

He read the list three times. Then he scrolled down.

**[Plumbing Kit (PVC pipe, fittings, basic fixtures): 75 points]**
**[Well Pump (hand-operated, deep well): 110 points]**
**[Well Pump (electric, submersible): 200 points]**
**[Septic System Kit (tank + drain field): 350 points]**
**[Water Heater (propane, 40 gal): 150 points]**

There was a section for electrical supplies. Wiring, outlets, junction boxes, breaker panels. A section for agricultural equipment. Seeds by the pound, irrigation kits, hand plows, a rototiller that cost 300 points.

He kept scrolling. Tier 2 went deeper than he'd realized. Way deeper. It wasn't just guns and food. It was everything you'd find at a Home Depot and a Tractor Supply combined, priced in a currency he earned by killing monsters.

"That's the thing," Marcus said to Tommy, keeping his voice low enough not to wake Joel or Sarah. "A dam gives you power. Reliable, renewable power. No gasoline to scavenge, no solar panels to maintain. You put a turbine in moving water and it just... works. Day and night. Rain or shine."

Tommy leaned forward. "You know how a hydroelectric dam works?"

"The basics. Water flows through turbines, turbines spin generators, generators produce electricity. The dam controls the water flow. Bigger dam, more flow, more power."

"That's the Cliff's Notes version." Tommy's voice had changed. Not warmer, exactly, but more awake. Like a part of him that had been sleeping for ten days had just sat up. "I've worked on dam infrastructure before. Small scale. Nothing like Hoover or Grand Coulee. But the principle's the same."

Marcus looked at him. "Military?"

"Army Corps of Engineers, briefly. Then a stint with a private contractor doing maintenance on municipal water systems in Wyoming. I know how a penstock works. I know how to read a turbine governor. I know what kind of bearings fail first and why you need to keep the intake screens clear of debris."

This was new information. Marcus filed it immediately. In the game, Tommy ran Jackson's dam. That was the reason Jackson had power. The reason it had lights and refrigeration and electric fences. The reason it was the closest thing to civilization in the TLOU universe. Tommy's knowledge was the foundation Jackson was built on.

And Tommy was sitting ten feet away, telling him about penstock maintenance.

"How hard is it to get a dam running?" Marcus asked. "If you found one that was intact but shut down."

Tommy tilted his head. He was thinking now. Not casual conversation thinking, but real thinking, the kind where you went back through everything you knew and organized it.

"Depends on the dam. Small hydroelectric, run-of-river type, those are simpler. The turbine's usually a Francis or a Kaplan design. If the mechanical components aren't corroded and the control systems are still functional, you could get one running in... maybe a few weeks. With the right tools. With someone who knows what they're looking at."

"What would you need? Tools, materials, parts."

"First thing, you'd need to inspect the turbines. That means access to the turbine room, which means the dam has to be structurally sound. No cracks in the spillway, no erosion on the abutments. Then you'd check the generator windings, the bearings, the governor. Replace the oil in the bearing housings. Check the penstock for blockages." Tommy paused. "You'd need grease. Lots of grease. Bearing grease, specifically. And wrenches. Big ones. Pipe wrenches, adjustable wrenches, torque wrenches. A multimeter to check the generator output. Wire to run from the dam to wherever you're setting up."

Marcus was scrolling through the Mall as Tommy talked. Pipe wrenches: 15 points. Bearing grease, industrial: 20 points. Multimeter: 25 points. Electrical wire, 500-foot spool: 40 points.

He could afford all of it. Not today, not without draining his reserves, but the costs weren't insane. Tier 2 prices. The dam tools weren't luxury items in the system's economy. They were just hardware.

"What about the dam itself?" Marcus asked. "If you could pick any dam in Colorado, what would you look for?"

"Small to medium. Something built for a rural electric co-op, not a major utility. Those are simpler, fewer things to break. You want one with a reservoir, not just run-of-river, because a reservoir gives you water storage and flood control. Fish too, probably. And you want it in a valley with defensible terrain around it. Mountains, ridgelines, natural chokepoints. One or two roads in, not six."

"You've thought about this before."

Tommy was quiet for a second. "Everyone thinks about it. Where they'd go if they had a choice. I keep comin' back to dams because... they work. Electricity is the line between surviving and living. You can survive without it. People did for thousands of years. But living, actual living, hot water and lights and refrigeration and tools... that takes power."

Marcus nodded. He was doing math in his head. The kind of math he used to do in Civilization games, where you balanced your economy against your military against your infrastructure and tried to hit the sweet spot where everything fed into everything else.

647 points. He'd been spending them on survival. Food, water, ammo, medicine. The daily burn was maybe 20-30 points per day for four people, depending on what they needed. At that rate, he'd be broke in three weeks.

But he'd also been earning. The nest at the feed store had netted 178 points. The farmhouse clearing was 56. Random Runners on the road, another 40 or so. The income was irregular and dangerous, but it was real. Every infected he killed was money in the bank. And the further they got from Austin, the more infected they'd encounter in places nobody had cleared yet.

The math said something interesting. If he could average 50-75 points per day from clearing runs, and keep the daily spend under 30, he'd be net positive. By the time they reached Colorado, call it six to eight weeks of walking, he could have 1500 to 2000 points. Maybe more, if they hit a good nest or found a cluster of Clickers.

1500 points bought a generator, a full carpentry kit, fencing materials, and enough building supplies to fortify a structure. 2000 points bought all that plus a solar panel kit, a well pump, and the dam tools Tommy was describing.

It wasn't enough for a town. But it was enough for a start.

"I think we can do it," Marcus said.

Tommy looked at him. "Do what?"

"Build something real. Not just a camp. Not just walls and a roof. Actual infrastructure. Power, water, food production, defenses. A place people would want to live."

"With what? We got a hunting rifle, thirty rounds, and a bag of Jolly Ranchers."

"I have... resources."

"The invisible resources."

"Yeah."

Tommy let out a breath through his nose. Not a sigh, exactly. More like the sound a man makes when he's deciding whether to ask the question he's been holding for ten days or let it sit for another ten.

He let it sit.

"If you say you have resources," Tommy said slowly, "and those resources include the kind of tools I just described... then yeah. A small dam in the Colorado Rockies is doable. But it's not a one-person job. It's not a four-person job either. You'd need people. Workers. A community."

"People will come. When we have lights and clean water and walls, people will come."

"That's optimistic."

"It's Texas. There are a million people between here and Colorado right now, all walking in different directions, all looking for the same thing. Safety. Food. A reason to stop running. If we build it, they'll come."

Tommy stared at him for a long moment. Then the corner of his mouth twitched. Not quite a smile, but close. "You just quoted Field of Dreams at the end of the world."

"Seemed appropriate."

"You're a strange kid, Marcus."

"Yeah. I know."

---

The morning came grey and cool, the first hint that October was getting closer and the Texas heat was starting to lose its grip. Joel woke first, or maybe he'd been awake for hours, it was hard to tell with Joel. Sarah woke second, stretching on her sleeping bag with the careful, measured motion of someone whose hip remembered being shot even when the rest of her body was fine.

They ate breakfast in the repair shop. Canned peaches and beef jerky and instant coffee that Marcus heated on a small propane stove he'd bought from the Mall two days ago. The coffee was terrible. Nobody complained.

...

They packed up and moved by seven. The county road cut northwest through low hills, following a creek that had carved a shallow valley through limestone. Cottonwood trees lined the banks. A redtail hawk circled overhead, riding a thermal, not caring about the apocalypse or the four people walking single file below it.

Joel was in front. Tommy behind him. Sarah in the middle. Marcus at the rear, checking the map every ten minutes out of habit.

The road was empty.

---

## — Marcus —

They stopped midmorning at a ranch gate where a stock tank sat behind a low stone wall. The tank was full. Spring-fed, Marcus guessed, because the windmill above it was still turning, slow and creaky, pumping water up from somewhere underground that didn't know the world had ended.

Joel checked the water. Tasted it. Nodded. They filled their bottles and sat on the stone wall in a line, four people on a fence like birds on a wire, drinking water that tasted like iron and limestone and nothing else.

"We should talk about Colorado," Marcus said.

Joel looked at him sideways. "We been talkin' about Colorado for a week."

"We've been talking about going to Colorado. I mean talk about what we do when we get there."

Sarah pulled her feet up onto the wall and sat cross-legged. She'd been quiet all morning. Not the bad kind of quiet, the kind from yesterday at the creek. Just regular quiet. Thinking quiet. The bag of Jolly Ranchers was in her jacket pocket and she took one out, unwrapped it, and put it in her mouth. Cherry.

"Tommy and I were talking last night," Marcus continued. "About dams."

"Dams," Joel repeated.

"Hydroelectric dams. Small ones. Rural ones. The kind that power a town of a few hundred people."

"I know what a dam is."

"Joel ran a crew that built a shopping center in Cedar Park," Tommy said. "He knows construction."

"That's different from a dam."

"Not as different as you'd think," Tommy said. He shifted on the wall, turning to face the group. The posture change was subtle but it mattered. He wasn't just sitting anymore. He was presenting. Briefing. "A dam's a structure like any other. Concrete, steel, engineering. The turbine room's the specialized part. Everything else is foundation work, waterproofing, civil engineering. Joel's built foundations."

Joel looked at his brother. The expression was hard to read. Not dismissive, exactly. More like the face of a man who was being recruited for a project he hadn't applied for.

"What exactly are you proposin'?" Joel asked.

Marcus took a breath. "We find a small hydroelectric dam in the Colorado Rockies. Something built in the forties or fifties for a rural co-op. Intact, with a reservoir. We get it running. That gives us electricity. With electricity, we have lights, refrigeration, heating, power tools. We build a settlement around the dam. Fortify it. Grow food. And we stay."

"Stay," Joel said the word like he was tasting it.

"Stay. Stop walking. Stop running. Build something permanent."

"With four people."

"For now. More will come."

"You keep sayin' that like it's a fact. People comin'. Why would they?"

"Because we'll have lights," Sarah said.

Everyone looked at her. She was chewing the Jolly Rancher, staring at the stock tank, not looking at any of them.

"Lights," she said again. "At night. When everything else is dark, if someone sees lights, they walk toward them. That's what people do."

The simplicity of it sat between them for a moment. Marcus hadn't thought of it that way. He'd been thinking in game terms, in systems and resources and strategic advantage. Sarah was thinking about something more basic. More human.

"She's right," Tommy said. "Jackson Hole... back before the outbreak, they had a hydroelectric plant. Tiny thing. Powered maybe two hundred homes. But at night, driving through the mountains, you could see the glow from thirty miles away. Like a beacon."

"This ain't Jackson Hole," Joel said.

"No. But the principle's the same." Tommy looked at Marcus. "You said you have resources. For argument's sake, let's say I believe that. What kind of dam are we looking for, specifically?"

Marcus pulled up the map in his head. The system's 25-mile range was useless for Colorado from here, but he'd been studying the Mall's infrastructure section and cross-referencing it with what he remembered from geography and from the game.

"Something in the western Rockies. Below ten thousand feet so we're not dealing with extreme winter conditions. Near a pass or a valley with agricultural potential. Flat land for farming, water for irrigation, and the dam for power. Defensible terrain. Mountains on at least two sides."

"You want a lot," Tommy said.

"I want a place worth defending."

"There are a handful of small dams on the Western Slope," Tommy said. "Crystal River drainage, Gunnison area, maybe up near the Roaring Fork Valley. Bureau of Reclamation built a bunch in the mid-century. Some of them are barely bigger than a two-story house. You wouldn't power a city, but a few hundred homes? Yeah."

"What about further north?" Marcus asked. "Toward Wyoming?"

Tommy thought about it. "There's more up that way. The Wind River range, the Absarokas. Colder, though. Harder winters. More remote." He paused. "There's a place I worked, years ago. Private dam on a ranch outside Marble. Francis turbine, maybe 500 kilowatts. The rancher used it to power his operation and sold the excess back to the grid. Small reservoir, good valley, mountains on three sides."

"Is it still standing?"

"Was when I left Wyoming. That was 2010. Three years ago." Tommy shrugged. "Dams don't fall down on their own. If nobody's blown it up, it's still there."

"500 kilowatts is enough?"

"For a starter community? More than enough. That's lights, refrigeration, heating, and workshop power for a hundred people, easy. You'd run it at maybe thirty percent capacity at first. Plenty of headroom to grow."

Joel had been listening with his arms crossed and his jaw set in the expression he wore when other people were making plans that involved him without asking first. But he hadn't interrupted. He hadn't walked away. He was still sitting on the stone wall, which for Joel was practically enthusiastic participation.

"Let's say we find this dam," Joel said. "Let's say it works. How do you keep it?"

"What do you mean?"

"I mean, you got a dam and lights and water and all the rest. Congratulations. How long before someone with more guns shows up and takes it from you?"

"We fortify. Build walls, set up watch posts, control the access roads."

"With four people."

"For now."

"You keep sayin' that."

"Because it's true. But even with four, we can do a lot." Marcus looked at Joel directly. "You built houses. You know construction. Tommy knows dams and infrastructure. I have resources. Sarah's smarter than all three of us combined."

"Leave me out of it," Sarah said, but the corner of her mouth moved.

"The first month is the hardest," Marcus continued. "After that, it gets easier. Every person who joins makes it easier. A carpenter. A farmer. A nurse. One person with each of those skills and we're a town."

Joel uncrossed his arms. He picked up a piece of limestone from the wall and turned it over in his fingers, examining it the way he examined everything, with the slow, deliberate attention of a man who'd spent his life building things and could read materials by touch.

"There's a difference between a plan and a fantasy," Joel said. "This sounds like a fantasy."

"Three weeks ago, you were watching Monday Night Football and paying a mortgage," Tommy said. "Everything sounds like a fantasy now."

Joel looked at Tommy. Something passed between them that Marcus couldn't fully read. Brotherhood. History. The kind of shorthand that two people develop over decades of knowing each other, where a look and a half-sentence carries the weight of a full conversation.

Joel dropped the limestone chip. "Fine. We get to Colorado. We find this dam. We look at it. I'm not promisin' anything beyond lookin'."

"That's all I'm asking."

"No it isn't. But it's all you're gettin'."

## — Sarah —

She walked behind Tommy for the rest of the morning, eating Jolly Ranchers and thinking about lights.

Not the lights Marcus was talking about. Not electricity and generators and dams and all the planning that the three of them did, which sounded like the conversations her dad used to have with his contractor friends about permits and load-bearing walls, except now the walls were for keeping out things that wanted to eat you.

She was thinking about the lights in her old house. The lamp in the living room that buzzed if you turned it on too fast. The green glow of the microwave clock at 3 AM when she got up for water. The porch light her dad left on when he worked late, so she'd see it through her bedroom window and know he was coming home.

Those lights were gone. Austin was gone. Bombed, burned, swallowed by whatever the world was turning into. The porch light was rubble. The microwave clock was melted slag. The lamp was ash.

But Marcus was talking about making new ones.

She didn't understand everything they were planning. Turbines and penstocks and kilowatts were words that landed in her head without sticking. But she understood the core of it, the simple version that she'd said out loud without really thinking about it. Lights in the dark. People walking toward them.

Her dad hadn't said yes. He'd said he'd look. Which, for Joel Miller, was practically the same thing. Her dad didn't look at things he'd already decided against. He looked at things he was going to say yes to but needed time to pretend he was still thinking about it.

She'd watched him do it with the truck. The F-150 he'd bought when she was nine. He'd spent two weeks telling Tommy it was too expensive, the mileage was bad, he didn't need a new vehicle. Then he drove it home on a Tuesday and acted surprised when Sarah said it was the same truck from the dealership they'd walked through.

"It was a good deal," he'd said.

"Uh-huh," she'd said.

He'd look at the dam. Then he'd start building.

She unwrapped another Jolly Rancher. Blue raspberry. The fake kind that tasted like nothing in nature and everything like summer and gas stations and being twelve.

She was almost out.

## — Marcus —

That night, camped in a hay barn that smelled like dust and old alfalfa, Marcus sat with his back against a bale and did the math.

647 points. The number had been sitting in the corner of his vision for two days, not growing, just waiting. He hadn't killed anything since the crossroads, and the crossroads hadn't given him anything because the system didn't pay for people. Fair enough. He didn't want it to.

He opened the Mall and started a list. Not a shopping list. A building list.

**[SETTLEMENT STARTUP — ESTIMATED COSTS]**

**[Portable Generator (backup power): 180]**
**[Carpentry Tool Kit: 45]**
**[Pipe Wrenches + Bearing Grease + Multimeter (dam repair): 60]**
**[Electrical Wire (500ft): 40]**
**[Fencing (100ft chain link + barbed wire): 125]**
**[Lumber (50 boards): 85]**
**[Concrete Mix (20 bags): 120]**
**[Seeds + Farming Tools: 80]**

**[Total: 735 points]**

He stared at the number. 735. He had 647. He was 88 points short of a starter kit for civilization.

88 points was maybe five Runners. Two Stalkers. One lucky day.

The realization landed quietly. Not like a bomb. More like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was there. He was 88 points away from not just surviving, but building. From being a guy with a knife and a gamer interface walking through Texas, to being the guy who puts a roof over people's heads and turns the lights on.

He could close the gap in a single good clearing run. One infested barn, one abandoned house with three Runners in it, and he'd have enough.

And that was just the starter kit. The bare minimum. The dam tools and the first round of construction materials. Beyond that, the list went on. Solar panels for 250. A well pump for 200. Eventually, the Tier 3 stuff. Medical equipment. Electrical infrastructure. Armored vehicles. The system scaled with ambition, and for the first time since he'd woken up in this world, his ambition had somewhere to go besides "don't die today."

He closed the Mall. Opened the map. The 25-mile radius showed clear around them. No infected, no hostiles. Just empty Texas countryside, rolling and dark under the stars.

Tomorrow they'd walk. The next day, they'd walk more. Six to eight weeks to Colorado, give or take, depending on terrain and trouble and how many detours the world threw at them.

But for the first time, the walking felt different. It wasn't just moving away from something. It was moving toward something. A dam in a valley with mountains on three sides and a turbine that Tommy knew how to fix.

Marcus bought one thing before he closed the interface. A small purchase. Barely worth noting.

**[Purchase: Regional Atlas — Western United States (pre-outbreak, detailed): 12 points]**
**[Points Remaining: 635]**

The atlas materialized beside his pack. A thick, spiral-bound book with a faded blue cover. He opened it to Colorado and found the Western Slope. The Crystal River. The Roaring Fork Valley. The Gunnison drainage. Small blue lines on a map that represented moving water, and moving water meant power, and power meant everything.

He marked three possible locations with a pen. Circled them. Drew a line from Austin, Texas to western Colorado. Eight hundred miles. Give or take.

He closed the atlas and put it in his pack and lay down on the hay barn floor and stared at the ceiling beams and thought about a dam he'd never seen in a state he'd never been to and the look on Tommy's face when he'd talked about turbine governors and bearing housings. That look. Like he'd remembered who he was, or at least who he could be.

Joel would come around. He'd look at the dam and see the foundations and the concrete and the structural problems and his contractor brain would start solving them before his mouth caught up. He'd complain the whole time. He'd do it anyway.

Sarah would name it. She'd come up with a name for the settlement the way she came up with a name for everything, casually, like she'd always known what it was called and was just waiting for everyone else to figure it out.

And Marcus would build it. Point by point. Purchase by purchase. One cleared nest, one dead Runner, one small blue square on a system interface at a time.

Somewhere in Colorado, a dam was sitting in a valley, waiting.

He fell asleep thinking about lights.
