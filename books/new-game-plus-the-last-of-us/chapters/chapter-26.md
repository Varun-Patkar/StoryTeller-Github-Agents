# Chapter 26: The Other Half

## — Sarah —

Day fifty-four started with an argument about oatmeal.

Sarah heard it from the garden beds behind the residence, where she'd been since six AM pulling rocks out of the soil Lena wanted to expand into a fourth raised bed. The soil up here was thin and full of granite chips. Every shovelful needed sifting. Her hands were dirty, her hip ached the way it always did in the mornings, and the argument carrying through the kitchen window was not the kind of problem she could fix with a trowel.

Carson's voice, low and tight. "I'm just saying I've been on gate detail since dawn and the portions aren't equal. Gene got twice what I got."

Gene's voice, reedy and defensive. "I got the same as everyone. One packet, one cup. That's what she set out."

"Yours was bigger."

"They're MRE packets. They come in one size."

"They come in different menus and different menus have different amounts. The chicken and rice is twice the volume of the vegetable crumble. You took the chicken."

Sarah set the trowel down and wiped her hands on her jeans. Lena looked up from the row she was raking.

"I can handle it," Lena said.

"I know. But this isn't about food."

It wasn't. Carson had been wound tight since the gravel bar. Two days of avoiding Marcus, two days of swinging an axe on the north ridge like the trees owed him something, and now the tension was leaking sideways into a complaint about oatmeal packets that were, in fact, the same size.

Sarah walked into the kitchen.

Carson was standing at the counter. Gene was sitting at Frank's table, eating his breakfast with the careful, measured bites of a man who knew his blood pressure was being monitored and didn't want to give it a reason to spike. Frank was at the far end of the table, sanding something small, probably a handle for something nobody had asked for yet. He didn't look up.

"What's the problem?" Sarah asked.

"Nothing," Carson said. Too fast.

"It sounded like something."

Carson looked at her. He was a big man, quiet most of the time, careful with words. The kind of person who carried lumber without being asked and ate whatever was put in front of him. He wasn't a complainer. Which meant this complaint was a symptom, not a cause.

"The food system's not fair," he said. "Some people get better meals. Some people eat first. There's no structure to it."

Sarah looked at the kitchen counter. Fourteen MRE packets were laid out in two rows, the way she'd arranged them last night before bed. Each one labeled with a name written on masking tape in her handwriting. Chicken and rice, beef stew, vegetable crumble, pasta primavera, and two unfortunate packets of something called "cheese tortellini" that tasted like neither cheese nor tortellini.

"I rotate the menus every three days," Sarah said. "Nobody gets the same thing twice in a row. The rotation's posted on the wall next to the work schedule."

She pointed. It was there. A grid, handwritten, names down the left side, days across the top, meal assignments in each cell. She'd made it on Day 50, the same day she'd taken over food coordination from the informal "grab whatever's on the counter" system that had preceded it.

Carson looked at the grid. He hadn't seen it before. Or he'd seen it and hadn't registered it, which amounted to the same thing.

"Oh," he said.

"You had chicken and rice on Tuesday. Gene had vegetable crumble on Tuesday. Today it's reversed. Tomorrow you both get beef stew because there's only one menu left that neither of you has had this cycle."

Gene looked at Carson over his oatmeal. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

"The menus vary in volume by about twenty percent," Sarah continued. "That's an MRE design issue, not a fairness issue. But if it matters, I'll weigh the portions and supplement the smaller ones with crackers from the bulk box. Fair?"

Carson's shoulders dropped. Not a lot. Half an inch, maybe. But enough.

"Yeah," he said. "Fair. Sorry."

"Don't apologize. Tell me when something bugs you. That's what the council's for."

Carson nodded. He picked up his packet, the vegetable crumble, and went outside. Sarah heard him cross the porch and head toward the north ridge, where his axe was waiting.

Gene finished his oatmeal.

"It wasn't about the food," he said.

"I know."

"He's scared of your boy."

Sarah sat down at the table across from Gene. Frank's sanding paused for half a second, then resumed. He was listening. Frank always listened. He just had the good sense to do it while his hands were busy.

"Everyone's adjusting," Sarah said. "It takes time."

"I'm not scared of him," Gene said. "I've been alive seventy-one years. I've been scared of plenty of things. A young man who kills monsters and buys me blood pressure pills isn't one of them."

"I appreciate that."

"But Carson hasn't been alive seventy-one years. Carson's been alive thirty-something and he's been in the apocalypse for two months and the man who feeds him just fought seven armed people like it was a light jog. That kind of thing sits different when you're thirty than when you're seventy."

Sarah didn't have a response to that. Gene was right. The fear was rational. Marcus had done something that no ordinary person could do, and now the people who depended on him were recalculating what it meant to depend on someone who wasn't ordinary.

"He burned the coffee again this morning," Frank said from the end of the table. He didn't look up from his sanding.

"He didn't burn it," Sarah said. "That was you."

"I know. But if anyone asks, it was him. A man who burns coffee is a man people can live with."

Sarah looked at Frank. The shop teacher was sixty-two years old, he'd been at the compound for three days, and he'd already figured out the same thing Sarah had.

Let Marcus be normal. Let the ordinary bury the extraordinary.

"Thank you, Frank," she said.

"For what? I'm just sanding a drawer pull."

____

Sarah spent the next two hours reorganizing the food system.

Not because it needed reorganizing. It worked fine. The rotation was fair, the portions were consistent, and the transition to garden-grown food was on Lena's timeline, not anyone else's. But Sarah had learned something from the oatmeal argument: the system worked, but nobody knew it worked, and invisible systems bred invisible resentments.

She pulled the rotation chart off the wall and rewrote it on a larger piece of paper from Frank's workshop. Bigger letters, cleaner columns, a header that said MEAL ROTATION — QUESTIONS TO SARAH in block capitals. She added a column for dietary notes. Gene's low-sodium restriction. Ava's aversion to the cheese tortellini, which Sarah privately shared. Mia's allergy to tree nuts, which Lena had mentioned on Day 49.

She posted it at eye level next to the work schedule. Then she wrote a second chart: PANTRY INVENTORY, listing every food item in storage with a running count. Thirty-seven MRE packets. Twelve cans of vegetables (scavenged from Marble). One bag of rice. Three bags of dried beans. Six jars of peanut butter. Salt, sugar, coffee (ground, and yes, frequently burned).

Helen found her taping the inventory to the wall.

"That's my job," Helen said. She wasn't offended. She was assessing.

"The master inventory is yours. This is the public-facing version. People should see what we have. It kills rumors."

Helen considered that. She pulled her notebook from her jacket pocket, opened it, compared the numbers on the wall to the numbers in her notes.

"Your count on MREs is off by two," Helen said. "Thirty-nine, not thirty-seven. Marcus bought a resupply yesterday."

Sarah corrected the number. "How do you know he bought a resupply?"

"Because I counted thirty-seven Tuesday night, and this morning there were thirty-nine, and nobody brought food in from outside."

"The cache," Sarah said. The cover story she'd crafted on the dam walkway.

Helen looked at her with the expression she used on everything. Level, patient, cataloging. "Sure. The cache."

She didn't believe it. Sarah knew she didn't believe it. Helen was too smart and too experienced to believe that a survivalist cache in the Colorado mountains produced MREs, antibiotics, electrical wire, and solar panels on demand. But Helen was also professional enough to accept the official story and file the real question for a time when asking it would be productive.

"The patrol schedule," Sarah said, changing the subject because staying on it was pointless. "Donna's three-shift rotation is working for the watch posts, but we don't have a schedule for the patrols Joel wants. He mentioned running two-person sweeps along the perimeter twice a day."

"He mentioned it to me too. Yesterday, on the south approach."

"Can you help me draft it? You know the perimeter better than anyone except Joel."

Helen's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Is this a council assignment or a request?"

"Both."

"Good enough."

They sat down at Frank's table with Helen's notebook and Sarah's journal. Helen mapped the compound's perimeter from memory, marking the five positions: tree stand (north, covering the road), boulder (east, covering the valley), secondary OP (south, covering the river bank), west draw (currently unmonitored), and the dam walkway (central, but not a defensive position).

"Two patrols a day," Helen said. "Dawn and dusk. Two-person teams. One circuit takes about forty minutes at a walk. An hour if you're checking the trip wires and the brush line."

"Who do we have?"

Helen counted on her fingers. "Joel, Tommy, Marcus, Donna, me, Carson. That's six people with combat or security experience. Mateo's willing but untrained. Ray and Lena aren't suited for it. Frank will tell you he's too old, and he's right. Gene is off the table."

"Ava?"

"I haven't assessed her. She's been keeping to herself."

Sarah made a note. "I'll talk to her. She's seventeen, she's fit, and she's been surviving on her own since Aspen. She can handle a perimeter walk."

Helen nodded. "Six confirmed, two possible. That gives us enough for two-person patrols without burning anyone out. But we need to cross-train. Right now, Joel and Marcus are the only ones who've fought infected at close range. If something comes through the perimeter and they're not here, we need people who know what to do."

"That's Joel's domain."

"I know. I'll bring it up at Monday's meeting." Helen paused. "Tactfully."

Sarah almost laughed. Helen Park, being tactful with Joel Miller. That was a conversation she wanted to watch from a safe distance.

They drafted the patrol schedule in twenty minutes. Dawn patrol: two people, full circuit, starting at the tree stand and ending at the dam. Dusk patrol: same route, reverse direction. Assignments rotated weekly. Helen would take the first dawn shift with Carson. Donna would pair with Tommy for dusk. Joel and Marcus were left off the regular rotation because Joel refused to follow a schedule he didn't write and Marcus was more useful doing clearing runs than walking a perimeter he could monitor from his map.

Sarah wrote it up cleanly and posted it next to the meal rotation and the work schedule. The kitchen wall was becoming a bulletin board. Three charts, one weekly plan, and Frank's table below them. The administrative center of a fourteen-person settlement.

It looked official. It looked like something a real community would have.

"You're good at this," Helen said.

"I'm good at lists."

"That's not what I mean." Helen closed her notebook. "I've been in units with officers who had twenty years of experience and couldn't organize a patrol rotation this clean. You're thirteen."

"I'll be fourteen in November."

"My point stands."

## — Marcus —

Marcus went south at noon.

The clearing run was routine. Eight Runners at the Crystal River Trailhead, four miles downstream, clustered in and around a parking area with three abandoned cars and a collapsed information kiosk. The trailhead sign still read CRYSTAL RIVER TRAIL — 4.2 MI — MODERATE in cheerful green-and-white lettering. Below it, a Runner was eating a squirrel.

Marcus worked through them methodically. Knife for the first three, the ones spread out near the cars. Hatchet for the cluster of four inside the kiosk, where the close quarters made the longer reach useful. The eighth was an outlier, standing at the trailhead gate, facing uphill, swaying in the slight breeze. It didn't turn until Marcus was ten feet away, which gave him time to notice the hiking boots, the REI jacket, the daypack still strapped to its back. A weekend hiker. Someone who'd driven out here for a Saturday trail run and never drove back.

He killed it with the knife. Quick, clean, through the base of the skull.

**[Runners Cleared: 8]**
**[Points Earned: 94]**
**[Streak Bonus: +12]**
**[Total Points: 587]**

Ninety-four points. Decent. Not great. Standard Runners were worth less per kill than they used to be. The system seemed to scale rewards based on threat level relative to the player, and Marcus had outgrown basic infected. He'd need to find bigger nests, tougher variants, or go deeper into territory he hadn't cleared yet.

But not today. Today was about maintenance, not progress.

He searched the trailhead parking lot. One car had a case of bottled water in the trunk, still sealed. Another had a first aid kit, basic but usable. The third had a child's car seat in the back, empty, and Marcus closed that door without looking at anything else.

He walked back to the compound with the water and the first aid kit, taking the river trail instead of the road. The afternoon was cold and clear. October in the Rockies. The aspens were turning, gold patches spreading across the hillsides like paint spilled on green canvas. A hawk circled the ridge above the dam. The river ran clear and fast over smooth stones.

He got back at two-thirty.

The compound was busy.

Not busy the way it had been before, with people working in their own corners and checking over their shoulders. Busy in a directed way. Carson was at the access road with Tommy, setting the first post for Joel's gate. Frank was building a lean-to at the boulder, using scrap lumber and a tarp. Helen and Donna were walking the west draw together, Helen pointing at things and Donna writing in a small notebook she'd started carrying after Helen started carrying hers.

Lena was in the expanded garden beds with Ava. The seventeen-year-old was kneeling in the dirt, pulling rocks from the soil the same way Sarah had been that morning. She was wearing borrowed clothes and a pair of work gloves two sizes too big, and she was doing the job without being asked.

Mateo was on the dam walkway, cleaning the railings with a wire brush. Nobody had asked him to do that either. He'd found a task and was doing it. Fifteen years old, abandoned by his group at Independence Pass, and he was cleaning rust off railings because it was something that needed doing.

Gene was on the porch, reading a paperback in the afternoon sun. His blood pressure cuff was on the table beside him. Mia was sitting on the bottom step, drawing in the dirt with a stick.

Everyone was working. Everyone had a place. And nobody had looked up when Marcus walked in from the south.

Not because they were avoiding him. Because they were doing their jobs.

He found Sarah at Frank's table in the kitchen, writing in her journal. The wall above her had three new charts: the meal rotation, the pantry inventory, and a patrol schedule he hadn't seen before.

"When did that go up?" he asked, nodding at the patrol chart.

"This morning. Helen and I drafted it." She looked up from the journal. "How was the run?"

"Eight Runners. Ninety-four points. Found some water and a first aid kit."

"Good. Add the water to the pantry. I'll update the inventory."

Marcus set the case of water on the counter. Sarah made a note in her journal, then stood up and corrected the wall chart with a pencil. BOTTLED WATER: 0 became BOTTLED WATER: 24.

"Helen's count on MREs was off," Sarah said. "I had thirty-seven, she had thirty-nine. I updated it."

"Thirty-nine is right. I bought two this morning."

"From the cache."

"From the cache."

The word sat between them like a shared joke, except it wasn't funny, exactly. It was just theirs.

Marcus looked at the kitchen wall. The work schedule from Monday. The meal rotation. The patrol chart. The pantry inventory. Sarah's handwriting on all of them, clean and organized.

"You've been busy," he said.

"Carson started a fight about oatmeal this morning."

"About oatmeal."

"About oatmeal. Except it wasn't about oatmeal. It was about the gravel bar and the fact that the person who feeds him can shrug off a bullet."

Marcus sat down. His shoulder ached. Not badly. The wound was closing faster than it should, the skin knitting under the bandage in a way that Helen would notice the next time she changed the dressing.

"How'd you handle it?"

"I showed him the rotation chart. Explained the system. He apologized. Then Gene told me Carson's scared of you, and Frank offered to take the blame for burning the coffee."

"Frank burned the coffee."

"I know Frank burned the coffee. Frank knows Frank burned the coffee. But Frank also knows that a man who burns coffee is a man people can live with. His words."

Marcus rubbed the back of his neck. "So the plan is working. Be normal. Be boring."

"The plan's working because I'm the one implementing it. You can't make yourself normal, Marcus. You tried eating MREs on the dam walkway yesterday and Helen watched you pick up a cracker with your injured arm like you hadn't been shot twelve hours earlier. You're not good at normal."

"Thanks."

"But I am." Sarah closed the journal. "I'm the person who makes the food chart and argues about oatmeal portions and pins schedules to the wall. I'm the person who asks Gene about his blood pressure and Lena about her garlic and Mateo if he needs a warmer blanket. I'm ordinary. People can come to me with ordinary problems and get ordinary solutions."

"You're not ordinary."

"I know. But I'm better at faking it than you are."

Marcus looked at her across the table. Frank's table. Pine, pencil gutters, trestle base. The table where fourteen people ate dinner and bumped elbows and complained about cheese tortellini.

"You're running this place," he said.

It wasn't a question. It was a realization that had been forming for days and had just finished crystallizing. Sarah didn't manage food distribution. She managed the community. The food was one system. The patrols were another. The work schedule was a third. She'd built a framework that made fourteen people feel like they belonged to something organized, something fair, something human. And she'd done it with a journal, a pencil, and the kind of practical empathy that no system could buy.

"We're running it," Sarah said. "You get the points. I get the people. That's the deal."

"When did we make that deal?"

"About ten seconds ago. Do you accept?"

Marcus looked at the kitchen wall. The charts. The schedules. Sarah's handwriting. Below them, Frank's table, solid and real, built by a man who believed that people who sat down together and looked each other in the eye could figure things out.

"Yeah," he said. "I accept."

## — Sarah —

Sarah went to talk to Ava at four o'clock.

She found her in the expanded garden beds, still pulling rocks. Her borrowed work gloves were crusted with dirt and her knees were muddy. She looked up when Sarah sat down on the edge of the raised bed.

"You've been out here for hours," Sarah said.

"Lena said the soil needed clearing."

"It does. But you could take a break."

Ava pulled another rock from the dirt and tossed it into the pile. She was thin, with dark hair that she kept in a braid and eyes that didn't settle on anything for long. She'd been at the compound for four days and hadn't volunteered a word about the commune in Aspen or how she'd ended up alone on the road.

"I don't want to go inside," Ava said.

"Because of last night?"

Ava didn't answer. She pulled another rock.

"You know he's not going to hurt you," Sarah said.

"I know. Everyone keeps saying that."

"But?"

Ava sat back on her heels. She looked at Sarah with the directness of someone who'd been alone long enough to stop softening things.

"The commune in Aspen had a guy like that," she said. "Not the same. Not as strong. But he was the one who got things done. He brought food. He fought off the infected. He made the decisions. And everyone loved him for it. Right up until he decided that making decisions meant making all the decisions. About the food. About the work. About who stayed and who left. About everything."

Sarah listened.

"It took three months before people started disagreeing with him. It took four months before he stopped letting them. By month five, it wasn't a commune. It was his camp and we lived in it because the alternative was outside." Ava pulled a last rock from the soil and held it. Smooth, gray, the size of her fist. "I left at month six. So did three others. The rest stayed because they were more afraid of outside than they were of him."

"Marcus isn't that person," Sarah said.

"I believe you. But I believed it last time too."

Sarah didn't have a rebuttal for that. Ava's fear wasn't irrational. It was earned. It came from experience, and experience didn't care about arguments.

"Do you want to do patrol shifts?" Sarah asked.

The question surprised Ava. She'd expected more reassurance, probably. More explaining. Instead Sarah was offering her a job.

"Patrol?"

"Perimeter walks. Dawn and dusk, two-person teams. Helen drafted the schedule. We need more people in the rotation."

"I don't have a gun."

"You don't need one for patrol. You need eyes, ears, and legs that work. If you see something, you report it. That's all."

Ava considered it. She turned the rock over in her hand.

"Who would I be paired with?"

"I was thinking Donna. She's steady. She won't push you to talk."

"Okay," Ava said. "Yeah. I can do that."

Sarah added Ava to the patrol rotation in her journal. Seven names now. Enough for a real schedule, with rest days and backups. She'd update the wall chart after dinner.

She stood up and brushed dirt off her jeans. Ava went back to pulling rocks. But something in the way she held her shoulders had shifted. Looser. Less braced.

A job. A place in the rotation. A small piece of belonging that wasn't dependent on one person's generosity.

That was what the commune guy had never understood. People didn't need a protector. They needed a structure they could trust, run by rules they could see, with a place for everyone that didn't vanish when the strong person changed his mind.

Sarah walked back to the kitchen and added a fourth chart to the wall.

PATROL ROTATION — DAWN & DUSK — EFFECTIVE DAY 55

Seven names. Two teams per shift. Rest days marked. Questions to Sarah or Helen.

The wall was getting crowded. She'd need a bigger wall soon.

## — Marcus —

That evening, the compound settled into something Marcus hadn't felt before.

Dinner at Frank's table was the same controlled chaos. Elbows bumping, chairs scraping, Gene telling a story about a fishing trip in 1987 that nobody had asked to hear but everyone was listening to anyway. Frank was explaining to Mateo how to plane a board without tearing the grain. Lena was feeding Mia crackers and negotiating with Ray about whether the solar panels needed cleaning. Tommy and Carson were arguing about gate post depth, a conversation so mundane and so specific that it could only have happened between two people who cared about doing something right.

Helen ate and listened and wrote nothing in her notebook.

Joel sat at the far end, eating, watching. The neutral face. But softer around the edges than yesterday. Softer because the compound was humming around him and nobody was looking at Marcus with the careful eyes they'd had last night.

They were looking at Sarah.

Not with fear. With the ordinary attention you give to the person who knows where the food is, who wrote the schedule, who organized the patrol you're walking tomorrow morning. Sarah moved through the kitchen refilling water cups and answering questions and telling Mateo that yes, the cheese tortellini really was that bad, and yes, she'd tried to get Marcus to buy better ones, and no, the "cache" apparently didn't stock gourmet options.

Mateo laughed. It was the first time Marcus had heard the kid laugh since he'd arrived.

After dinner, Marcus walked to the dam walkway. Sarah followed, carrying two cups of coffee. One of them was Frank's brutal brew. The other was a cup she'd made herself, weaker, drinkable.

She handed him the drinkable one and kept the other.

"You gave yourself the bad coffee," Marcus said.

"I'm making a point about shared sacrifice."

"That's terrible coffee as a leadership metaphor."

"Drink your coffee."

They sat on the concrete rail, legs over the edge. The river below was dark, reflecting stars. The dam hummed. The compound lights were on behind them, yellow and warm against the October cold. Somewhere in the maintenance building, Frank was teaching Mateo a card game. The sound of cards being shuffled carried on the still air.

"Joel came to me today," Sarah said. "After lunch. He said the gate post is set and he wants razor wire for the top. He asked me, not you."

"He always asks me for purchases."

"He asked me. I told him I'd put it on the council agenda for Monday and we'd discuss it against the other priorities."

Marcus sipped his coffee. "And he was okay with that?"

"He said 'Fine' in the voice that means he's not fine but he respects the process." Sarah drank her coffee without flinching. Frank's brew didn't seem to bother her. "Tommy asked me about the wire for the outbuildings. Helen asked me about flashlights for the night patrol. Carson asked me where to put the lumber scraps from the gate."

"They used to ask me."

"Some of them will still ask you. But the everyday stuff, the questions that don't need a system or a clearing run or a hundred points, those come to me now. That's how it should be."

Marcus didn't argue. She was right. The community didn't need him for food charts and patrol schedules and oatmeal disputes. It needed him for the things only he could do. Points. Clearing runs. The system. The impossible things.

Everything else was Sarah's.

"Partners," he said.

"Partners," she said.

The word settled between them like a fact. Not new. Not sudden. Something that had been true for a while and was only now being said out loud.

"I talked to Ava today," Sarah said. "She's joining the patrol rotation."

"Good."

"She told me about the commune in Aspen. A man who started as a protector and ended as a dictator. That's her frame of reference. That's what she's watching for."

Marcus stared at the river. A trout rose to something on the surface, a brief ring of disturbance that spread and vanished.

"And you told her I'm not that."

"I told her we have a council, a schedule, and charts on the wall. I told her the structure doesn't depend on one person's mood."

"Does it?"

"Not anymore." Sarah finished her coffee. She set the cup beside her on the rail. "Marcus. You built this place. You found the dam, you bought the tools, you cleared the infected, you kept everyone alive. That's real and nobody's going to forget it. But a settlement that runs because one person is strong isn't a settlement. It's a camp. Camps fall apart when the strong person gets tired or hurt or makes a bad call."

"I made a bad call on the gravel bar?"

"You made the right call. But the right call scared people, and scared people make bad decisions. So we build something that doesn't scare them. Rules they can see. Roles they can count on. A person they can talk to about oatmeal without worrying she'll bench-press a truck."

Marcus laughed. It surprised him. He hadn't laughed since before the gravel bar.

"I can't bench-press a truck," he said.

"Yet."

"Yet," he agreed, and the word was funny and also not funny at all, because they both knew the system wasn't done with him.

Sarah picked up both cups and stood. "Council meeting Monday. I've got four agenda items. Joel's razor wire, Tommy's conduit run, Helen's flashlight request, and a proposal to formalize my role."

"Your role?"

"Community coordinator. I want it on the record. Not because I need a title, but because Ava needs to see that authority comes from the council, not from one person."

Marcus looked up at her. The compound lights caught the side of her face, the line of her jaw that was Joel's, the steadiness in her eyes that was entirely her own.

"I'll second it," he said.

"I know you will."

She went inside. Marcus sat on the rail alone, listening to the compound settle. Cards shuffling. A door closing. Donna's boots on gravel, heading to the watch position. The dam humming its low, constant note.

He checked the settlement screen.

**[Settlement: Crystal River Dam]**
**[Population: 14]**
**[Power: Dam (168 kW) + Solar (1 kW backup)]**
**[Water: River (filtered)]**
**[Food: Mall-supplied (transition to agriculture in progress)]**
**[Defenses: Perimeter watch (3-shift rotation), patrol rotation (dawn/dusk, 2-person teams), natural bottleneck, observation post, tree stand, south perimeter tripwire (active), secondary OP (active), access road gate (under construction)]**
**[Governance: 5-seat council (established Day 51)]**
**[Morale: Stable]**

**[Points: 587]**

Morale: Stable.

Not "Shaken." Stable. Two days ago, the compound had watched him fight like something that wasn't entirely human, and the morale had cracked. Now it was back. Not because Marcus had done anything. Because Sarah had made food charts and patrol schedules and talked to a scared girl about pulling rocks from the soil.

He'd protected them with the hatchet and the system.

She'd held them together with a journal and a pencil.

"Partners," he thought.

The dam hummed. The stars were out. Somewhere in the kitchen, Frank was teaching Mateo how to bluff at poker, and Frank was losing, because Frank was the kind of man who believed that teaching someone to win was better than winning yourself.

Marcus finished his coffee and went inside.
