# Chapter 38: Vows

## — Marcus —

June 2020. Crystal Falls, Colorado.

Three years since Captain Whitcomb's FEDRA convoy had rolled up Highway 133 and asked Crystal Falls to bend the knee. The answer had been no then, and it was still no. Colonel Briggs had sent two more delegations since, each smaller and more polite than the last. They'd settled into a trade relationship. Food and medical supplies for scrap metal and diesel. No tribute. No registration. Crystal Falls was nobody's subject.

The settlement had 331 people. Sarah counted every Tuesday. New cabins climbed the valley slope in neat rows that David Chen had laid out with an engineer's eye for drainage and sight lines. The clinic had three rooms now. The school had thirty-four students. Lena's orchard was bearing fruit for the first time, small sour apples that she insisted would improve with another season. The armory had a proper door. Joel had built it himself because Joel didn't trust anyone else to build a door that mattered.

Marcus stood in the maintenance building bathroom and tried to button a shirt.

White cotton, slightly yellowed at the collar where the bleach hadn't quite reached. Frank Garvey had tailored it from two dress shirts found on a scavenging run to Glenwood Springs six months ago and pressed it with a clothes iron heated on the woodstove. The best shirt in Crystal Falls. Probably the only dress shirt in Crystal Falls.

His hands were shaking.

Not from fear. Not from the adrenaline that came before a clearing run or a fight. Just nerves. Plain, stupid, ordinary nerves. The kind that didn't care if you could hear heartbeats from two hundred meters or heal from a bullet wound in a day.

He'd fought Bloaters. He'd stared down a FEDRA convoy alone. He'd killed more infected than he could count and spent seven years building a town from a broken dam and a bag of Mall-purchased tools.

He couldn't button a shirt.

"You're doin' it wrong."

Joel stood in the doorway. He was wearing what passed for his formal clothes. Clean jeans, a flannel with no stains, boots he'd actually polished. His hair was combed. Marcus had never seen Joel with combed hair.

"I'm not doing it wrong. My fingers aren't working."

Joel walked over. Looked at the buttons. Looked at Marcus's hands. He didn't say anything for a few seconds.

"You know what I was doin' the morning Sarah was born?" Joel asked.

"What?"

"Throwin' up in a hospital bathroom."

Marcus looked at him. Joel's face was doing the thing it did when he was sharing something personal and pretending it was casual. Flat expression, eyes on the shirt buttons instead of Marcus's face.

"Her mama was in labor for fourteen hours," Joel said. "I was fine for the first twelve. Then the doctor said there was a complication and I needed to wait outside, and I went to the bathroom and threw up in the sink." He reached over and buttoned the top button that Marcus had skipped. "Nerves don't mean you're not ready. They mean it matters."

"Joel."

"Yeah."

"Thank you."

Joel straightened the collar. Gave it a tug to make it lie flat. Contractor's hands, precise and practical.

"You make her happy," Joel said. Not a question. A statement he'd arrived at over years of watching. "That's the only thing that matters today."

He turned and walked out. Marcus heard his boots on the stairs. Steady, unhurried. The walk of a man who'd made peace with something that had taken him a long time.

Marcus looked at himself in the mirror. The shirt was buttoned. It looked decent. He looked nervous, which was honest.

He heard the compound getting ready. Lena directing volunteers in the kitchen, her voice carrying that particular Oklahoma authority she used when something needed to be right. Raymond arguing with Frank about table placement on the dam walkway. Tommy testing the string lights he'd spent two days wiring, the faint buzz of each bulb clicking on in sequence, one after another along the lines.

And Sarah's heartbeat, steady at sixty-two, in the residence across the courtyard. The same rate it always ran. The woman who'd told him four years ago "I've known for a year, idiot" was apparently not nervous at all.

Marcus closed his eyes. Opened them. Went downstairs.

---

The dam walkway had been transformed.

Frank had built a wooden arch from stripped pine and sanded it smooth enough that the grain showed like watercolor. Lena had threaded wildflowers through it. Lupines and columbines she'd been cultivating in the greenhouse since March, saving the best blooms for today. The arch stood at the center of the walkway, facing east across the reservoir, with the mountains behind it and the dam's concrete wall dropping away on one side.

Tommy's string lights ran in two lines overhead, zigzagging from the arch to the powerhouse roof. Sixty-three bulbs. Marcus could count them by the individual electrical hums. They weren't lit yet. That was for the evening.

Chairs had been borrowed from every building in Crystal Falls and arranged in rough rows. Folding chairs, kitchen chairs, wooden benches, a piano stool that Gene insisted on bringing because it was the right height for his back. Three hundred and thirty-one people and not enough chairs for all of them, so the back rows were standing room. Nobody complained.

Marcus stood near the arch and watched people arrive. Keiko came early with her patrol team, all of them in clean clothes with rifles stacked discreetly by the powerhouse door. Old habits. Helen had a clipboard. Of course Helen had a clipboard. She was noting attendance and probably tallying something related to meal portions for afterward. Dr. Reyes arrived with her med kit, because Dr. Reyes went nowhere without her med kit. Dale Briggs had shaved for the occasion and looked like a different person.

Carson brought the goats. Not on purpose. Margaret had chewed through her fence tie again and three goats followed Carson up the walkway before anyone could stop them. He chased them back twice before giving up. Margaret settled under the third row of chairs and chewed on someone's shoelace. Seven years old and still the settlement's most persistent problem.

Marcus watched it all and felt something he didn't have a system notification for. Not a stat boost. Not a perk. Just the quiet, complicated feeling of a life that had somehow turned out to be real.

## — Joel —

Joel knocked on the residence door at five thirty. The evening light was turning gold on the valley walls. The first guests were already filling the chairs on the dam walkway. He could hear the murmur of three hundred conversations, the sound of a community dressed in its best and trying to remember what occasions like this used to feel like.

"It's open," Sarah called.

He went in. Sarah's room. The east-facing bedroom she'd asked for back on Day 12 when they'd been walking through Texas with nothing but a sports wagon and a dead man's backpack. She'd gotten her room. She'd gotten the east window, the bookshelf that Frank had built against the wall, and a border collie mix named Captain that she'd found during a clearing run two years ago. Captain was currently lying on the bed, looking at Joel with the expression dogs used for people who were not their favorite person.

Sarah stood by the window. She was wearing a dress that Helen and Lena had put together from fabric found on a trading run to Basalt Station. White, simple, with a hem that stopped above her boots because this was still the apocalypse and nobody was walking across a dam in heels. Her hair was pulled back and Lena had woven small blue columbines into the braid.

She looked like her mother.

That was the thing that hit Joel first and hardest. The shape of the jaw, the way she stood with her weight slightly forward, the half-smile that meant she was thinking about three things at once. He'd seen that face on another woman in another life, in a kitchen in Austin with morning light coming through the window, and the memory landed in his chest like a fist.

"You look..." Joel started.

"If you cry, I'm telling everyone," Sarah said.

"I ain't cryin'."

"Your voice is doing the thing."

It was. The thing where it went rough at the edges, where the Texas got thicker because the feelings were too close to the surface. He cleared his throat.

"You look beautiful, baby girl."

Sarah's composure cracked for exactly one second. A blink that lasted longer than it should have. A swallow. Then she was back. She smoothed the dress and looked at him.

"Did Marcus throw up?"

"Almost."

"Good. If he wasn't nervous, I'd be worried."

Joel stood in the doorway and looked at his daughter. The girl who'd been twelve on Outbreak Night, bleeding on a highway overpass while he screamed and Tommy dragged him away. She'd walked eight hundred miles through the ruins of Texas and New Mexico and Colorado with a bullet wound that would never fully heal. She'd helped build a town from a broken dam and empty land. She'd managed three hundred people's lives on clipboards and handwritten charts and the kind of quiet authority that didn't need to raise its voice.

She was getting married. To the stranger who'd appeared out of nowhere and saved her life. The man Joel had spent years not trusting, then not understanding, then grudgingly respecting, and finally accepting. Marcus was impossible. His supplies came from thin air. His abilities broke the rules of the world everyone else lived in. His knowledge of things he shouldn't know sat between them like furniture nobody moved.

But he stayed. That was the thing Joel kept coming back to. In a world where everyone either died or left, Marcus stayed. He stayed when it was hard. He stayed when people were afraid of him. He stayed when Joel threatened him on a back porch in Texas. He stayed, and he built, and he waited four years because Sarah asked him to and waiting was the right thing to do.

"Ready?" Joel asked.

Sarah picked up a small bouquet from the desk. Columbines and a few sprigs of rosemary from Lena's herb garden. Simple. She held them in one hand and linked her other arm through Joel's.

"Ready."

They walked out of the residence and into the evening light.

## — Sarah —

The string lights came on when she stepped onto the dam walkway.

Tommy's timing. He'd been waiting at the powerhouse breaker for the signal, and Donna had signaled from the walkway entrance when Sarah and Joel appeared. Sixty-three bulbs, warm yellow, running in twin lines above the rows of chairs and the standing crowd behind them.

Three hundred and thirty-one people. Her people. She knew most of their names. She knew who was allergic to what, who couldn't stand night watch, who needed reading glasses and didn't have them, who argued about laundry schedules and who stole extra bread at dinner and who cried quietly in their cabin at night when they thought no one could hear.

They were all here. Standing in the golden light on a concrete dam in Colorado, dressed in scavenged finery, waiting to watch her marry a man who'd appeared out of thin air on the worst night of their lives and carried her to safety.

Sarah tightened her grip on Joel's arm. Not because she was nervous. Because she wanted to remember what this felt like. Her father's arm under her hand. The lights overhead. The mountains turning purple in the east. The way three hundred people went quiet at the same time.

They walked between the rows. Sarah saw faces. Helen, clipboard absent for once, smiling in a way that looked unfamiliar on her. Dr. Reyes with her med kit under her chair and tears already forming. Raymond, standing in the back row with flour still on his forearms because he'd been baking until twenty minutes ago. Carson, who'd clearly lost the battle with the goats. Margaret was under a chair in the third row, chewing contentedly on something. Mateo, twenty-one now and a squad leader, wearing a clean shirt buttoned to the collar like someone had told him to look respectable.

Keiko stood at the end of the second row with her arms folded and her eyes scanning the tree line, because Keiko was Keiko even at a wedding.

Frank Garvey stood near his pine arch and looked like he might burst from pride. He'd built the arch, built the council table, built half the furniture in the settlement. Every major moment in Crystal Falls had a piece of Frank's work in it somewhere.

And Marcus. Standing under the arch in his white shirt with the slightly yellow collar, hands clasped in front of him, watching her walk toward him.

He looked terrified. And happy. And like a man who couldn't quite believe any of this was real, which was funny, because that was exactly how he'd looked on Outbreak Night when he'd knelt over her bleeding body and told her she was going to be okay.

She'd believed him then. Twelve years old, shot in the hip, lying on asphalt with her father's screams in her ears. A stranger's hands pressing gauze against her wound. She'd believed him because his voice didn't shake even though his hands did.

His hands were shaking now, too.

Joel walked her to the arch. He stopped. Looked at Marcus. The two men exchanged a look that carried seven years of history. Trust earned in increments, arguments in kitchens, fights in abandoned buildings, quiet conversations on porches at dawn.

Joel lifted Sarah's hand from his arm and placed it in Marcus's.

"Take care of her," Joel said.

"Every day," Marcus said.

Joel nodded once. Stepped back. Sat in the front row beside Tommy, who handed him a handkerchief without looking at him. Tommy had come prepared.

Tommy stood from his seat and walked to the arch. He was officiating. They'd discussed who should do it, and it had been Sarah's idea. Tommy was the one who'd backed Marcus's plan for Colorado when Joel wanted to run for a QZ. Tommy had fixed the turbine. Tommy had introduced the group to the valley. If anyone was going to say the words, it should be the man who'd helped bring them all here.

Tommy cleared his throat. He was wearing his cleanest flannel, which wasn't very clean, and boots that had been polished with motor oil because that was all they had.

"We don't have a government," Tommy said. "We don't have a church or a courthouse or a marriage license or any of the things that used to make this official. What we've got is three hundred people who showed up to watch two stubborn idiots finally make it formal."

Laughter from the chairs. Raymond's was the loudest.

"I've known Marcus since the night the world ended. He showed up out of nowhere, saved my niece's life, and then asked us to walk eight hundred miles to a broken dam in Colorado. And I said yes, because I looked at this kid and thought, 'He's either the smartest person alive or the craziest.' Turns out he's both."

More laughter. Marcus shook his head slightly.

"And Sarah." Tommy looked at her. His voice softened. "I watched you grow up out here. I watched you go from a twelve-year-old in a sports wagon to the person who runs this entire town on clipboards and willpower. You're the toughest Miller, and I include your daddy and me in that count."

Joel looked at the ground. His jaw worked once.

"So." Tommy turned back to Marcus and Sarah. "You wrote vows?"

Marcus nodded. He turned to Sarah. Took both her hands.

"Seven years ago," he said, "I was running through Austin in the dark. I didn't know where I was going. I didn't have a plan. I just knew that somewhere out there, there was a girl who wasn't going to die. Not if I could get there in time."

His voice was steady. Sarah noticed. His hands shook, but his voice was clear.

"I found you on the ground by the highway. Your dad was screaming. Tommy was pulling him away. And you were bleeding, and I had nothing except bandages I'd bought five minutes earlier with points I'd earned killing my first Runner."

He paused. The dam was quiet. Just the creek below and the hum of the turbine and the string lights buzzing softly above.

"I knelt next to you and told you it was going to be okay. I didn't know if that was true. I just knew I'd make it true or die trying."

Sarah's grip tightened on his hands.

"Everything I've done since that night," he said, "every wall, every clearing run, every point I've spent. It started with you. On a highway in Austin, in the dark, with the world ending around us."

He stopped. Took a breath. Let it out.

"You asked me once, back in Texas, for a room with an east-facing window. A bookshelf. A dog. That was the first time I knew what I was building toward. Not a settlement. Not a community. A home. Your home. A place where you could wake up to the sunrise and read a book and not be afraid."

Sarah's vision blurred. She blinked it clear. She wasn't going to cry before her own vows.

"I love you," Marcus said. "I loved you before I knew what to call it, and I'll love you through every chapter that comes next. Whatever happens. Whatever's out there. I'm not going anywhere."

The dam was very quiet. Sixty-three bulbs hummed. Someone in the back row sniffed. Probably Helen.

Sarah looked at him. This strange, impossible man who'd fallen out of the sky on the worst night of her life with a magic shopping system and a head full of knowledge he wouldn't explain. Who'd carried her through burning streets and back roads and eight hundred miles of nothing. Who'd built a town and a council and a life, and waited four years because she'd asked him to, and never once acted like waiting was hard.

She'd written her vows on a piece of notebook paper three days ago, sitting in her east-facing room with Captain asleep on the bed. She'd written two pages, crossed them out, written one page, crossed that out, and ended up with two sentences. Because she was Sarah Miller, and two sentences was enough.

"You carried me when I couldn't walk," she said. "I'll carry everything else."

Marcus laughed. A short, surprised sound, halfway between a laugh and something that might have been a sob if he'd let it go any further. Sarah smiled at him. The real smile, the one she'd gotten from her mother, the one Joel sometimes looked away from because it hurt in the good way.

Tommy wiped his eyes with the back of his hand. Cleared his throat.

"Then by the authority of absolutely nobody except three hundred people who love you and a town you built from nothing..." He put one hand on Marcus's shoulder and one on Sarah's. "You're married. Kiss her before I start cryin' in front of the whole settlement."

Marcus kissed her. Sarah kissed him back.

The dam erupted. Three hundred and thirty-one people clapped and cheered and whistled. Mia Haywood, thirteen now and completely shameless, screamed "FINALLY!" from the fourth row. Margaret bleated from under her chair. Captain barked from somewhere near the residence, upset about the noise or thrilled by it, impossible to tell with that dog.

## — Marcus —

The reception lasted until the stars came out.

Raymond's kitchen crew had outdone themselves. Venison roasted over coals, bread still warm from the clay oven Lena had built last fall, apple cider from the orchard's first real harvest, green beans from the garden, and three pies that Lena had been hiding in the greenhouse for a week. She'd used the last of the saved sugar. When Marcus tried to thank her, she waved him off and said the sugar wasn't doing anything useful sitting in a jar.

Tables had been set up on the flat ground below the dam. Tommy's string lights extended down on poles Carson had hammered into the earth that afternoon. The evening was warm for Colorado. Clear sky, no wind. The kind of night that felt ordered specifically for the occasion.

People ate. People talked. For a few hours, Crystal Falls wasn't a settlement managing resources and watching for threats. It was just a town at a party.

Helen danced. Marcus had never seen Helen dance. She was terrible at it, methodical and slightly off-beat, and she looked happier than he'd ever seen her.

Frank danced with Donna, slow and easy, the way people danced when they'd been doing it together for decades and didn't need the music to match.

Mateo danced with Ana Gutierrez, both of them pretending it was casual and fooling nobody.

Joel brought out his guitar after dinner. He'd played at the harvest festival, and a few times since at community gatherings, but never for long. Tonight he played for an hour. Old songs. Country and folk, the kind of music that sounded right under string lights in the mountains. "Wish You Were Here" again, because it was Joel's song and the crowd expected it. Then something slower that Marcus didn't recognize, something Joel played with his eyes closed and his fingers moving from memory.

The last song was a lullaby. Marcus had heard fragments of it before. Late at night, when Joel sat on the porch and thought nobody was listening. A lullaby he'd probably sung to Sarah when she was small, before Austin, before everything.

Sarah was sitting next to Marcus at the head table, her hand on his. She went still when the melody started. She didn't say anything. Just listened. Marcus watched her watch her father play a song from a world that didn't exist anymore, and something about the moment was so ordinary and so impossible at the same time that he had to look at the mountains instead.

The guitar faded. People clapped. Joel nodded once and set the guitar against his chair.

---

They danced. Once. Neither of them was good at it. They'd danced at the harvest festival in 2016, the night they'd both known and neither had said anything. That had been awkward and perfect. This was less awkward. More perfect.

The song came from an old iPod that still held a charge on the solar panel dock. The battery was at twelve percent and the speaker was a Bluetooth unit patched together from three broken ones by Frank. The sound quality was terrible. Neither of them cared.

"You shook through your entire vows," Sarah said.

"My hands. Not my voice."

"Your voice cracked on 'I love you.'"

"It did not."

"Helen was taking notes. She'll confirm."

Marcus pulled her closer. She smelled like columbines and rosemary and the clean soap Dr. Reyes had started making from rendered fat and lye. An apocalypse smell. The only perfume available.

"Thank you for the two-sentence vows," he said. "I wrote three pages and cut it down."

"I know. I heard."

"You could've warned me you were going for brevity."

"And miss watching you talk for five minutes while I stood there looking good? No chance."

He laughed. She smiled. The string lights hummed overhead. The mountains were dark shapes against a sky full of stars. The turbine ran. The creek ran. Three hundred people milled around them, eating pie and talking and being alive in a world that had tried hard to kill all of them.

Sarah rested her head against his shoulder.

"Room with an east-facing window," she said.

"Bookshelf."

"Dog."

"Got all three."

"You forgot the husband."

"I didn't ask for a husband on Day 12. That was a late addition."

"Best addition."

"Don't push it."

The iPod died at eleven percent because the battery gauge had always been optimistic. They kept moving without music, slowly, in the quiet, while the town settled into the sounds of an evening winding down.

## — Joel —

Joel sat on the powerhouse steps with the guitar across his knees and watched his daughter dance with her husband.

His daughter. Alive. Married. Happy.

Seven years ago, he'd held her on an overpass in Austin and screamed until Tommy pulled him away. He'd thought she was dead. He'd carried that belief for miles before a stranger caught up with them and said the words that changed everything.

She wasn't dead. She was dancing. Barefoot now, boots abandoned under a table. Laughing at something Marcus said. The string lights made her hair glow and the columbines in her braid had started wilting, petals dropping onto her shoulders.

Tommy sat down next to him.

"Nice ceremony," Tommy said.

"You talked too long."

"I talked the right amount."

"You cried."

"You cried first."

Joel didn't deny it. He ran his thumb along the guitar strings. A soft chord, barely audible over the creek.

"He's a good man," Tommy said. Not asking Joel to agree. Just saying it the way you say things that are true.

"Yeah," Joel said. "He is."

"She's happy."

"Yeah."

They sat. The compound was emptying slowly. Parents carrying sleeping children to cabins. Couples walking arm in arm. Dale and Carson arguing about who'd drunk the last of the cider. Ordinary sounds. A community going to bed after a good day. There weren't enough of those. Good days. They came in bursts between the hard ones, and you had to pay attention or you'd miss them entirely.

Joel looked at the string lights. Sixty-three bulbs. Tommy's wiring, Frank's poles, Carson's labor. A community effort to put lights above a wedding on a dam in the mountains. Seven years ago, the four of them had stood on a ridge and looked at this dam and Sarah had said "Home." First time any of them had used that word since Austin.

Now it was home. Full of people and problems and goats and council meetings and Monday schedules and whatever Gene's latest complaint about the shower door was. Home, in the way that mattered. Not a place you stayed because there was nowhere else. A place you stayed because leaving would be worse.

"I almost lost her," Joel said quietly. He didn't need to say when. Tommy knew.

"But you didn't."

"Because of him."

Tommy was quiet for a moment. "Because of a lot of things. Him, and you, and her being too stubborn to die. She's a Miller. We don't go easy."

Joel almost smiled. The Miller stubbornness. Sarah had it worse than either of them.

He watched Marcus and Sarah sway in silence, no music, just two people moving slowly under string lights in a world that had ended and kept going anyway. His daughter's head on her husband's shoulder. His daughter's laughter, quiet now, just for the two of them.

Joel set the guitar down. Leaned back against the powerhouse wall. The concrete was warm from the day's sun.

"Good day," he said.

"Good day," Tommy agreed.

They stayed on the steps until the last lights went out.
