# Chapter 23: Growing Pains

## — Marcus —

Day fifty-one. Two hundred and twenty-seven points.

Marcus stared at the number the way a man with a maxed-out credit card stares at his bank statement. He'd been lower, technically. Back in Austin, back when thirteen points felt like a fortune and a hunting knife was the most expensive thing he'd ever bought. But he hadn't been feeding eleven people back then.

The Mall was open in front of him, projected on the inside of his eyelids in that blue-white interface only he could see. Food supplies, medicine, building materials, tools. Everything had a price. Everything added up.

Gene's lisinopril refill was due in eleven weeks. Sarah wanted lime for the garden beds. Tommy had a list of copper fittings for the outbuilding wiring that would cost sixty points minimum. And Marcus was sitting on two-twenty-seven like it was a savings account instead of the difference between prepared and screwed.

He needed a clearing run. Today.

The compound was already awake when he came out of the residence at dawn. Tommy and Ray were in the maintenance building, arguing about junction boxes. Marcus could hear them through the open door, two electricians speaking a language of amperage and load calculations that sounded like a foreign film without subtitles.

"You can't run fourteen-gauge to the outbuildings and expect it to handle a space heater," Ray was saying. "Not at this distance. You'll get voltage drop before you get heat."

"I ran fourteen-gauge at Fort Hood for three years and never had a problem."

"Fort Hood had a substation. You've got a turbine from 1936."

Marcus kept walking. They'd figure it out. They always did, eventually, after they'd exhausted every possible thing to disagree about first.

Carson was on the north ridge with Joel. Marcus could see them through the trees, two shapes moving through the brush line with axes and a crosscut saw. Joel had wanted a hundred yards of cleared sightline on the north approach. Carson had the build for it. The man was quiet, methodical, and could swing an axe for hours without complaining. Joel appreciated that. Joel appreciated anyone who worked without talking.

Donna was on the boulder at the valley entrance, rifle across her knees, exactly where Sarah usually sat. She'd slotted into the watch rotation like she'd been doing it for years. Ex-park ranger. She knew how to sit still and watch things.

Marcus stopped by the boulder on his way out.

"Heading north," he said. "Clearing run. Back by afternoon."

Donna looked at him. "How far north?"

"Five, six miles. There's a cluster of contacts past the old fishing camp."

"You want backup?"

"I'm good."

"Marcus." She said it the way people said things when they'd been alive long enough to know that "I'm good" was usually a lie. "You're going alone to fight infected. At least tell Joel."

"Joel's busy. And I work better solo for this."

Donna's expression said she had opinions about that. She kept them to herself.

Marcus headed north along the river trail.

...

The fishing lodge was five miles upstream, tucked into a bend where Carbonate Creek met the Crystal River. A wooden building with a wraparound porch, a stone chimney, and a sign that read CRYSTAL RIVER OUTFITTERS in faded green paint. There were three trucks in the gravel lot, all dead. A kayak rack with two kayaks still strapped to it. A plastic cooler on the porch with the lid open, empty except for rainwater.

Marcus checked his map. Seven contacts inside the building. All orange. Runners.

Seven was a lot for a solo run. More than he'd usually take without Joel or Tommy backing him up. But seven Runners at ten to fifteen points each, plus streak bonuses, plus the nest breaker achievement if he cleared the whole building. That was a hundred points minimum. Maybe one-thirty if there were bonus modifiers.

He needed every one of them.

The Glock was loaded. Fifteen rounds. The hunting knife was on his belt. He'd bought a hatchet two weeks ago for eight points, a compact thing with a fiberglass handle that fit in his belt loop. Three weapons, seven infected, one building.

He went in through the back door.

The lodge's interior was what you'd expect from a place that catered to fly fishermen with money. Knotty pine walls, mounted trout above the fireplace, leather furniture gone dark with mildew. A reception desk with a guest book still open to a page dated September 25, 2013. Two days before the world ended. Someone named Patterson had checked in for a week of guided fishing.

The Pattersons hadn't checked out.

The first Runner was in the kitchen. Female, mid-forties, wearing a fleece vest and hiking boots. She'd been standing in front of the industrial refrigerator like she was trying to decide what to eat, which would've been funny if the fungal growth on her face hadn't been the size of a fist.

Marcus took her from behind. Knife through the base of the skull. Quick, quiet, practiced. She dropped without a sound.

The second lunged. Marcus sidestepped, caught it by the collar of its vest, and drove the knife up under its chin.

Three down, four to go. He could hear them upstairs. Thumping. The floorboards creaking under weight that moved in that jerky, purposeless way that Runners had when they weren't chasing anything.

The stairs were narrow. Pine, with a runner carpet that had gone black with mold. Marcus went up slowly, knife in one hand, hatchet in the other, Glock holstered because gunshots in a confined space would bring all four at once and he wanted them in pieces, not a mob.

The fourth was in the hallway. Fast, younger than the others, and it came at him the second he cleared the top step. Marcus caught it with the hatchet but the angle was wrong and the blade glanced off the thing's shoulder instead of its skull. It grabbed his jacket. He shoved it into the wall, pinned it with his forearm, and finished it with the knife.

**[Runner eliminated — 14 points]**

The noise brought the fifth from a bedroom on the left. Marcus stepped over the body and met it in the doorway. Clean kill. Knife through the eye socket.

Two left. He could hear them in the last room at the end of the hall. A door with a brass number plate: Room 6. The door was closed.

Marcus listened. Two sets of footsteps. One normal Runner pace, shuffling and aimless. The other was different. Heavier. Faster. A pattern that wasn't quite right.

He checked the map. Two contacts. One orange. One dark orange, almost red.

He hadn't seen dark orange before.

Marcus opened the door and went in fast, hatchet high. The first Runner was by the window, an older man in pajamas. Easy. The hatchet took it in the side of the head and it went down.

The second one was in the corner. And it was wrong.

Bigger than a normal Runner. Male, maybe thirty, in a flannel shirt that had split at the seams because the body underneath had swollen with fungal growth. Not a Clicker. No mushroom plating on the face, no echolocation clicking. It could still see. But the eyes were different. Redder. Wilder. And when it moved, it moved fast.

Very fast.

It crossed the room in two strides. Marcus barely got the knife up in time. The thing hit him like a linebacker, driving him back into the hallway wall. His head cracked against the pine paneling. Stars. The infected's hands were on his shoulders, fingers digging in through his jacket, and its mouth was open and snapping six inches from his face.

Marcus jammed the hatchet handle sideways into its mouth like a bit. The thing bit down on fiberglass and kept pushing. Its breath was hot and smelled like rotting meat and something sweet and fungal underneath.

He got his right hand free. The knife found its way between the thing's ribs. Once. Twice. The infected didn't slow down. It wasn't feeling pain the way the others did. Marcus twisted the blade and pulled it out and drove it into the side of its neck. Something popped. The thing's grip loosened. He pushed it sideways, pulled the hatchet free, and buried it in the top of its skull.

It went down. Finally.

Marcus stood in the hallway, breathing hard, blood on his jacket, blood on his hands, blood on the wall. His head was ringing from hitting the paneling. His left shoulder ached where the thing had grabbed him.

Four hundred and forty-three. Better. Not comfortable, but better.

He looked at the Enhanced Runner on the floor. The new contact type. The dark orange on his map. Stronger, faster, tougher than a standard Runner. Not as dangerous as a Stalker or a Clicker, but a clear step up from the shuffling, mindless things he'd been farming since Austin.

"They're getting stronger," he said to the empty hallway. Nobody answered. The lodge was quiet now except for the wind through a broken window and the distant sound of the creek.

The infected were evolving. He'd known that since the first Stalker at Buckhorn school. But knowing it and standing in a hallway with a new variant's blood on his hands were different things.

He wiped the knife on the carpet and headed back to the dam.

## — Joel —

Joel heard the gunshot at noon.

One shot. From the valley entrance. The signal.

He set down the axe and picked up the rifle. Carson did the same with the shotgun. They moved through the brush line to the access road without talking. Joel's tree stand was empty. Donna was on the boulder, rifle up, scope to her eye.

"Three contacts," Donna said. "Coming from the north this time. Up the river trail."

North. Not south. That was new. Everyone so far had come from the south, from the highway, from the populated valleys. North was wilderness. National forest, old mining roads, not much else.

Joel climbed into the tree stand and settled the rifle on the branch rest. Through the scope, he could see them. Three people, single file on the river trail, moving carefully. Two adults and what looked like a teenager. They had packs but no visible weapons. The lead figure was a man, tall, wearing a canvas jacket and a watch cap. The woman behind him was shorter, dark-haired, carrying a frame pack that looked military surplus. The teenager was between them, which was the smart position, protected front and back.

"Where's Marcus?" Joel asked.

"Clearing run. North. Said he'd be back by afternoon."

Joel's jaw tightened. Of course Marcus was gone when people showed up. The boy had a talent for being somewhere else when the front door knocked.

"Tommy," Joel called toward the powerhouse.

Tommy appeared in the doorway with a wrench in one hand. "I heard the shot. How many?"

"Three. Coming from the north. I'll handle it."

Tommy looked at him. "You sure?"

"I've done three screenings. I know the questions."

"Joel, you also scared the hell out of Ava."

"Ava was hiding things. I asked until she wasn't."

Tommy raised his hands in surrender and went back inside. Joel climbed down from the stand, handed the overwatch rifle to Donna, and walked down the trail to meet the newcomers.

He met them at the creek crossing, a hundred yards north of the compound. The man in the watch cap stopped when he saw Joel and put his hands out, palms open. Smart. The woman did the same. The teenager, a boy of maybe fifteen or sixteen, just stared.

"We're not armed," the man said. "We saw the lights two nights ago. From up on the pass."

Joel studied them. The man was maybe fifty. Lean, weathered, the kind of face that spent time outdoors before the world gave everyone a reason to be outdoors. The woman was younger, thirties, with a calm directness in her eyes that reminded Joel of Donna. The boy had dark circles under his eyes and a healing cut on his forehead.

"Names," Joel said.

"I'm Frank Garvey. This is Helen Park. The boy is Mateo. Helen and I aren't together. We met on the road four days ago. Mateo was with a group that fell apart near Independence Pass."

Joel looked at Mateo. "That true?"

The boy nodded. "There were eight of us. Some wanted to go to Denver. Some wanted to stay in the mountains. We split up. I went with the mountain group. Then they left me."

"Left you how?"

"I woke up and they were gone. Took most of the food."

Joel processed that. People leaving a kid behind wasn't unusual anymore. It wasn't even the worst thing he'd heard this week.

"What'd you do before?" Joel asked Frank.

"High school shop teacher. Woodworking, metalwork, small engine repair."

Joel almost smiled. Almost. A shop teacher was more useful right now than a surgeon. Surgeons needed equipment. Shop teachers needed a workbench and some hand tools.

"And you?" he asked Helen.

"Army Reserve. Supply and logistics. Eight years. Got out in 2010."

"Where were you stationed?"

"Fort Carson, mostly. Some time at Peterson."

Colorado bases. She knew the area.

Joel ran through the rest of the screening questions. Where they'd been since the outbreak. Who they'd been with. What they'd seen. The answers were consistent, detailed in the right places and vague in the places where trauma made things vague. Nobody's story was clean anymore. Everybody had gaps.

"Weapons surrendered on arrival," Joel said. "One-week probation. Separate housing in the maintenance building. You eat with everyone else. You contribute when you're able. Questions?"

Frank shook his head.

Helen said, "Who's in charge?"

Joel paused. It was the first time anyone had asked that question directly.

"We're workin' on it," he said.

## — Sarah —

Fourteen people.

Sarah counted them at dinner that night, sitting on the porch steps with her journal while everyone else ate inside. Fourteen. Marcus, Joel, Tommy, herself. Ray, Lena, Mia. Donna, Gene, Carson, Ava. And now Frank, Helen, and Mateo.

Fourteen people, and they'd been at the dam for nine days.

Inside, the noise was something new. Not loud, exactly. But layered. Multiple conversations happening at once. Lena talking to Donna about the garden beds. Tommy explaining the solar panel system to Frank, who kept asking questions that Tommy actually seemed to enjoy answering. Gene reading aloud from the farming book to anyone who'd listen. Carson eating quietly, as always, but sitting next to Helen, who was also eating quietly, which Sarah suspected was the beginning of something.

Ava was at the end of the table, picking at her food. Mateo was two seats down, doing the same thing. Neither of them was talking to anyone, but Sarah noticed they were both listening to the same conversation. Tommy's solar panel explanation. Two teenagers pretending not to be interested in the same thing.

Mia was on the floor by the fireplace, arranging pinecones in a line. She still hadn't spoken. But this morning, Sarah had seen her standing at the edge of the garden beds, watching Lena dig. And when Lena had offered her a small trowel, Mia had taken it and dug a hole. Didn't plant anything. Just dug a hole and then patted the dirt back into place. But she'd taken the trowel. That was new.

Marcus came in from the kitchen with a plate and sat down next to Sarah on the steps.

"Fourteen," she said.

"I can count."

"You sound like Joel."

Marcus looked at her like she'd accused him of a crime. Sarah didn't smile, but her eyes did.

"The new ones seem solid," she said. "Frank knows his way around tools. Helen has military logistics experience. Mateo's just a kid, but he's been on his own for a while. He's tough."

"Joel screened them?"

"Ran the whole process. Didn't scare anyone. He's getting better at it."

Marcus ate for a while. He looked tired. There was a cut on his left hand he hadn't mentioned, and a bruise on his forehead near the hairline. The clearing run had been rough. He'd told her about the Enhanced Runner in about six sentences, which meant it had been worse than six sentences.

"We need to talk about how this works," Marcus said.

"How what works?"

"This." He gestured at the residence, the people inside, the noise. "Fourteen people. Nine days ago it was four. In another week it could be twenty. We can't keep running this like a campsite where I buy everything and Joel decides who gets in."

Sarah closed her journal. "What are you thinking?"

"Roles. Structure. A council, maybe. People who are responsible for specific things. Tommy runs power and infrastructure. Someone runs food production. Someone handles security and screening. Someone manages housing and supplies."

"You're describing a government."

"I'm describing a chore chart with authority."

Sarah looked at the people inside. Donna, who'd volunteered for every watch shift without being asked. Ray, who'd rewired the outbuildings and was already talking about running a line to the garage for a welding setup. Lena, who knew more about mountain agriculture than anyone else in the valley. Helen, who'd spent eight years managing military supply chains.

"Talk to Joel first," Sarah said. "He'll hate it. But he needs to hear it from you before you bring it up with everyone else."

"Why will he hate it?"

"Because committees slow things down, and Joel believes speed saves lives. He's not wrong. But he's also running security for fourteen people by himself with a tree stand and a rifle, and that's not going to work at twenty."

...

Marcus found Joel on the dam walkway. Same spot as last night. Same railing, same posture, looking at the water.

"Three more today," Marcus said.

"I know. I screened 'em."

"How'd it go?"

"Fine. The shop teacher talks too much. The Army woman talks just enough. The boy's been through something he's not ready to say."

"Joel, we need to talk about structure."

Joel didn't turn from the railing. "Figured that was comin'."

"Fourteen people. We're going to be twenty before the month is out. I can't keep buying everything out of the Mall and making every decision. You can't keep being the only person who decides who gets in."

"Worked so far."

"It worked at four. It worked at seven. At fourteen it's already creaking. What happens at thirty?"

Joel was quiet. The river below the dam was louder tonight. Rain upstream, maybe, swelling the flow.

"What are you proposin'?"

"A council. Five people. Me, you, Tommy, Sarah, and someone from the newcomers. Donna, probably. She's ex-park ranger, she's practical, and she's already doing the work."

"A council." Joel said it like he was tasting something he wasn't sure he liked.

"Not a committee. Not a debate society. Five people who are each responsible for a domain. I handle resources and external threats. You handle security and screening. Tommy handles power and infrastructure. Sarah handles food and community logistics. Donna handles the watch rotation and perimeter."

"And when two of us disagree?"

"We vote. Majority wins."

"And if the majority is wrong?"

"Then we fix it. Same as we've been doing."

Joel turned from the railing. He looked at Marcus for a long time. The look he used when he was measuring someone against a standard only he could see.

"I've been in groups that did the council thing," Joel said. "Boston. The QZ had a residents' committee. You know what they did? They argued about water rationing while FEDRA shot people in the street. Committees talk. They don't act."

"This isn't a committee," Marcus said. "This is five people who already do the work, agreeing to keep doing the work with clear lines so we don't step on each other."

"And if someone outside the council wants a say?"

"Then they bring it to the council. Anyone can bring an issue. We hear it, we decide, we move."

Joel stared at the water again. An owl called from the tree line. Two notes, silence.

"Tommy'll say yes," Joel said.

"I know."

"Sarah already told you to do this."

"She told me to talk to you first."

Joel's mouth did the not-quite-smile thing. "Smart girl." He pushed off the railing. "Fine. But I keep final say on security. Nobody comes in without my sign-off. Not the council's. Mine."

"That's what the security domain means. Your call, your rules."

"And if we vote and I'm outvoted on somethin' that gets people killed?"

"Then I'll buy you a drink from the Mall and you can tell me you told me so."

Joel looked at him. "The Mall sells whiskey?"

"Tennessee bourbon. Twelve points a bottle."

Joel considered this for exactly two seconds. "Council's fine. First meeting's tomorrow. And buy the bourbon."

Marcus bought the bourbon.

_Helen walked the entire perimeter with Donna this afternoon. Took notes. Actual notes, in a pocket notebook, with measurements and compass bearings. She's going to be useful._

_Ava and Mateo sat at opposite ends of the dinner table and pretended not to look at each other. Teenagers are the same in every apocalypse._

_Mia dug a hole in the garden today. Then filled it back in. Then dug it again. Lena pretended not to notice. I pretended not to notice. But I noticed._

_Fourteen people. A council. A garden. A watch rotation. Bourbon on the dam walkway._

_We're not a camp anymore. I don't know what we are. But we have a government now, and that's either the beginning of a town or the beginning of an argument._

_I guess we'll find out._
