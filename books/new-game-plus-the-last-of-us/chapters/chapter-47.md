# Chapter 47: The Cost

## — Marcus —

He heard the engines at 5:47 AM.

Marcus was on the dam walkway, checking the turbine output readings on the clipboard Tommy had bolted to the railing. A morning habit. The dam hummed through his boots, 168 kilowatts of stable power lighting three hundred and forty-nine lives. The Crystal River ran below, cold and fast with late-July snowmelt. A hawk circled above the eastern ridge, riding the thermals.

The engines were south. Many of them. Diesel, heavy, the rumble of large vehicles on bad road. His enhanced hearing separated the sounds: at least four distinct engine signatures, maybe six, spaced in a convoy pattern. Eight miles out, maybe ten. Moving north on Highway 133.

Nobody drove convoys anymore.

He opened the system map. Fourteen contacts on the approach road. Not infected. The system classified them differently. Orange dots for infected. These were grey. Human. Moving in formation.

**[Unknown — Human Group]**
**[Size: Estimated 180–220]**
**[Classification: Armed Military]**
**[Distance: 9.2 miles, approaching]**
**[Estimated Arrival: 47 minutes]**

Two hundred soldiers. Marcus watched the grey dots crawl north along the highway and felt something cold land in his chest that wasn't fear and wasn't surprise. It was the feeling of a thing he'd been expecting finally arriving.

Colonel Briggs.

He was off the dam walkway and crossing the compound in thirty seconds. Joel's cabin was dark. Marcus knocked twice. The door opened on the second knock. Joel slept like a man who expected bad news.

"FEDRA. Two hundred soldiers. Armored vehicles. Less than an hour."

Joel didn't ask how Marcus knew. Hadn't asked in eight years.

"Wake the militia. Ring the bell."

---

The bell was a piece of railroad track hung from a pine frame outside the mess hall. Frank Garvey had welded the mount. The sound carried across the compound and echoed off the valley walls, low and ugly and impossible to sleep through.

Crystal Falls woke up.

Keiko Tanaka was at the armory in four minutes, issuing rifles and ammunition to the militia members who came running in various states of dressed. Helen Park appeared with the radio, already hailing Basalt on the emergency frequency. Tommy was at the generator building, running a status check on power and communications. Donna Pruitt took the south watchtower.

Marcus found Sarah at the main lodge. She had Noah by the hand and a clipboard in the other. Already making lists. Evacuation routes, non-combatant shelters, medical station locations. Dr. Reyes was behind her, pulling trauma kits from the clinic supply.

"How many?" Sarah asked.

"Two hundred, give or take. Armored vehicles."

"Briggs?"

"Has to be."

Sarah looked at Noah. Then at the clipboard. Then at Marcus. "I'll get the civilians into the mine tunnel. Dr. Reyes sets up triage in the maintenance building. Helen?"

"Already calling Basalt."

"Good." Sarah knelt in front of Noah. "Hey. Remember the drill we practiced?"

Noah nodded. His eyes were wide but his chin was set. Six years old, born into a world that did this to children.

"Go with Martha. Stay in the tunnel until I come get you. Okay?"

"Okay."

Martha Cope, Harlan's wife, had been visiting Crystal Falls for a coalition planning meeting. The timing was terrible. Or lucky, depending on how you looked at it. Harlan was back at Willow Creek with a radio.

Marcus watched his son walk away. Then he turned to the gate.

...

Forty-seven minutes was enough time to do two things: position the militia and send one radio call.

Joel did the first. Keiko did the second.

Joel deployed thirty-two militia members to the valley bottleneck. The narrow section where the Crystal River ran between a granite cliff on the west and a steep forested slope on the east. The barricade they'd built over the past year was a wall of railroad ties, sandbags, and scavenged steel plate, reinforced with concrete that Carson and David Chen had poured last winter. Not military-grade. But thick, positioned where the road squeezed to one lane, with firing positions for twenty.

Joel put Keiko in command of the barricade. Twelve militia on the wall. Eight in the tree line on the eastern slope as flankers. Six in reserve at the dam compound. Four runners for message relay, because radios failed in firefights and human voices didn't.

Dale Briggs took the left end of the barricade. Paul Crane took the right. Carson held the center, behind the thickest section of steel plate, feeding ammunition.

Keiko's radio call to Basalt was short: "Crystal Falls under imminent military attack. FEDRA Grand Junction. Two hundred soldiers, armored vehicles. Request immediate support."

Kate Schultz's response came through static: "Moving now. Thirty minutes."

Basalt was twelve miles away. Thirty minutes was optimistic.

## — Marcus —

He walked south to meet them.

Joel had argued against it. Not long. He recognized the tactical value of a forward position. But he'd looked at Marcus the way he did when the math included the possibility of Marcus not coming back.

"If they shoot you..."

"Bullet-resistant, Joel."

"Resistant ain't proof."

"I need to try talking first."

Joel had handed him a radio. "You've got five minutes of talkin'. After that, you get behind the wall."

Marcus walked down the access road past the outer perimeter markers, past the tripwire system Helen and Joel had built three years ago, past the point where the valley opened enough to see the highway approach from the south. He stopped at the bridge over Crystal River. A good spot. Open ground behind him, clear sightlines forward, the river on both sides.

He heard them before he saw them. Engines growing louder, the crunch of tires on cracked asphalt, the diesel rattle of heavy vehicles in low gear. Then they came around the bend.

Three Humvees in the lead. Two had roof-mounted M2 machine guns. The third had a Mark 19 grenade launcher. Behind them, four troop transport trucks, canvas-covered, heavy with bodies. Behind those, two more Humvees bringing up the rear. The exhaust stained the morning air grey.

The convoy stopped two hundred yards from the bridge. The lead Humvee's door opened.

Colonel Nathan Briggs was in his fifties. Tall, lean, the build of a career officer who'd stayed fit through discipline and the kind of food rationing FEDRA imposed on everyone, including its commanders. Buzz cut gone grey at the temples. BDU uniform maintained with the care of a man who believed the uniform still meant something. He wore a sidearm and carried nothing else.

Two soldiers flanked him. A dozen more fanned out behind the vehicles, taking positions along the road.

Briggs walked to within fifty yards. Close enough to talk. Far enough to run.

"You're Webb," Briggs said.

"And you're Colonel Briggs. We met through your envoys four years ago."

"Whitcomb told me you were reasonable."

"I was. I offered fair trade between equals. Your envoys left peacefully. You could have taken the deal."

Briggs looked past Marcus at the valley. At the road narrowing to the bottleneck. At the barricade barely visible beyond it.

"Things have changed since then. You've built a coalition. Basalt. Glenwood Springs. Willow Creek. You've pulled four settlements out of federal authority and organized them under your leadership."

"Under no one's leadership. Each settlement governs itself."

"You clear their infected. You supply their tools. You built the network. That makes you the authority, whether you call yourself that or not."

"What do you want, Colonel?"

Briggs straightened. The career officer speaking. "Crystal Falls will register with FEDRA Grand Junction as a federal settlement. The coalition will be dissolved. Your militia will be placed under federal command. In return, you receive military protection, supply chain access, and representation in QZ governance."

"And if we say no?"

"You've already said no once. I came to make sure you understood the consequences of saying it again."

Marcus looked at the Humvees. The mounted guns. The soldiers positioning themselves along the road. Two hundred men and women who'd been fed FEDRA rations and drilled with FEDRA weapons and told they were the last legitimate government on earth.

"Colonel, you have eight hundred people in Grand Junction. You brought two hundred here. That's a quarter of your population and probably your entire combat force. Your food situation is bad. Whitcomb's heart rate told me that four years ago. Your QZ has been losing people to settlements like mine ever since. Diane Pryor took thirty of your people when she left."

Briggs's jaw tightened. "Pryor is a deserter."

"Pryor is a woman who watched you run a QZ like a prison and decided she could do better. She was right." Marcus kept his voice even. Not aggressive. Not pleading. Just laying out facts. "You don't have the resources for this fight. And if you start it, you won't have a QZ to go back to."

"I have three armored vehicles, two hundred trained soldiers, and federal authority."

"And I have a wall, thirty-two people who'll die before they let you through it, and me."

The morning was quiet except for engines idling and the river running under the bridge. A meadowlark called from somewhere in the tree line. Bad timing for birdsong.

Briggs studied Marcus the way a tactician studied terrain. Looking for weaknesses. Angles. The thing that didn't fit.

"You're one man, Webb."

"I am. But your scouts have filed reports on me. You know what I did to that horde three months ago. You know what happened to the raiders on the south gravel bar. You know I walked alone into a mine and killed a Bloater with a hunting knife." Marcus let it hang. "Ask yourself if this is worth it."

Briggs was quiet for ten seconds. Twenty. The soldiers behind the Humvees shifted their weight. One of them coughed.

"I didn't come here to ask," Briggs said. "I came to tell."

He turned and walked back to his Humvee.

Marcus keyed the radio. "Joel. Talking's done."

"Get behind the wall."

Marcus ran.

## — Joel —

The first shot came from the Humvee's mounted gun.

The M2 Browning cycled at 450 to 600 rounds per minute. Joel knew this because he'd fired one exactly once, in a life before the outbreak, at a range outside Austin where a buddy from border patrol had let him try. That had been a fun afternoon. This was not.

The heavy rounds hit the barricade's steel plates. The sound was enormous. Not the crack of rifle fire but a sustained hammering that rattled teeth and made the railroad ties jump in their sandbag brackets. Splinters of wood and fragments of metal filled the air behind the wall.

"Hold position!" Keiko yelled. Her voice carried over the gunfire through force of will alone. "Nobody fires until they're inside two hundred yards!"

Joel was at the eastern tree line with eight flankers, positioned on the slope above the road. From there he could see the entire approach. The three lead Humvees advanced in a staggered line, the mounted guns raking the barricade with suppressing fire while FEDRA soldiers dismounted from the transports and spread into the tree line on both sides of the road.

Professional. Briggs's people knew what they were doing. Fire and maneuver. Fix the defenders with heavy weapons, advance infantry on the flanks, overwhelm the position. Textbook.

Joel had never gone to war. But he'd spent eight years training a militia, studying manuals Marcus bought from the Mall, and thinking about exactly this scenario. The terrain was the advantage. The valley bottleneck forced the Humvees into single file. The steep slopes on both sides made flanking slow and exposed. And Marcus was somewhere between the wall and the Humvees, which meant everything between those two points was about to get very bad for anyone wearing FEDRA grey.

"Dale. Left flank. Count the infantry in the tree line."

Dale Briggs lay prone behind a fallen log, binoculars up. "Twelve... fifteen. Moving in three-man teams. Slow on the slope."

"Let 'em get close. Seventy yards. Then we hit their lead team and roll them back downhill."

"Copy."

On the barricade, Keiko's twelve held. The M2 fire was terrifying but the steel plates stopped everything it threw. Carson fed ammunition from behind the center section, his hands steady, doing the thing he was best at: not panicking. Paul Crane had his rifle up, watching the gap between the two lead Humvees for infantry.

The first FEDRA soldiers reached engagement range.

"Barricade, fire!" Keiko called.

Twelve rifles opened up. The sound crackled off the granite cliff and filled the valley. FEDRA soldiers went down. Two, then three, then one who'd been climbing a boulder to get elevation. The advance stalled.

But the Humvees kept coming.

The lead vehicle was forty yards from the barricade. The M2 gunner adjusted aim, concentrating fire on the sandbags where Keiko's line was thinnest. Railroad ties splintered. A sandbag burst and sprayed sand across the firing position. Rico, one of the Denver refugees who'd volunteered six months ago, took a fragment in the shoulder and dropped back.

"Somebody deal with that truck!" Carson yelled.

Joel keyed his radio. "Marcus. Lead Humvee. Now."

## — Marcus —

He was already moving.

The M2 rounds hit him twice as he crossed the open ground between the barricade and the lead Humvee. Left shoulder. Right thigh. The enhanced durability turned what should have been through-and-through wounds into deep bruises that spread across his skin in dark blotches. It hurt. Not the polite sting of a cut or the dull ache of exhaustion. A sharp, angry pain that said his body was absorbing energy it was never meant to handle, even after the upgrades.

He kept running.

The M2 gunner saw him coming. The turret swiveled. Marcus could hear the mechanism turning, the slight grinding of a mount that hadn't been properly oiled in months. He felt the road surface through the soles of his boots. The heavy vehicle's engine. The turret motor. The gunner's weight shifting.

He reached the Humvee before the turret finished tracking. Enhanced speed covered sixty yards in under four seconds. He hit the vehicle's front quarter panel with his shoulder and felt the frame buckle. Not shatter. Not flip. Just buckle, the way a shopping cart crumples into a wall. The front axle shifted two inches sideways. Enough to jam the steering.

The gunner fired. Three rounds, close range, into Marcus's back as he came around the side of the vehicle. He felt them like punches from someone twice his size. His vision went white at the edges. Something inside his ribcage cracked. Not a bone. One of the cartilage connections between rib and sternum. It would heal in hours. He needed it to hold for minutes.

Marcus grabbed the M2's barrel. The metal was hot enough to burn normal hands. He twisted it sideways until the mounting bracket snapped. Then he pulled the gunner out of the turret by his vest and set him on the road. Not gently, but alive.

The driver bailed out. Marcus let him go.

Second Humvee. Fifty yards back. The grenade launcher.

The Mark 19 fired before Marcus reached it. The round hit the road surface between him and the vehicle and detonated. The blast picked Marcus up and threw him eight feet sideways into the drainage ditch. His ears went flat. The world became a ringing silence filled with dirt and the taste of blood in his teeth.

He got up. Slower this time. The accelerated healing was working on his eardrums and the lacerations across his forearms, but it was working on everything at once and the system wasn't built for this kind of simultaneous damage.

**[Health: 61%]**
**[Accelerated Healing Active — Rate: 3.2% per minute]**
**[Warning: Multiple concurrent injuries detected]**

Marcus charged the second Humvee. The Mark 19 fired again but the angle was wrong. The gunner couldn't depress the launcher far enough to hit a target closing from twenty yards. The round sailed over Marcus's head and hit the tree line behind him. Wood cracked and burned.

He reached the vehicle. This time he went straight for the hood, driving his fingers into the gap between the hood and the fender, and tore it open. The engine block sat exposed. Marcus grabbed a fistful of wiring and pulled. Coolant hoses, spark plug cables, the main wiring harness came free in his hand. The engine died.

The gunner dropped through the top hatch and ran. The driver followed.

Third Humvee. Rear guard. Already reversing.

Marcus didn't chase it. His ribs were screaming. His health was at 64% and climbing but everything ached in the specific way that said stop.

He turned back to the barricade.

## — Joel —

The infantry on the eastern flank reached seventy yards.

"Now," Joel said.

His eight flankers fired from above. The downhill angle turned the tree line into a shooting gallery. FEDRA's flank teams had been climbing the slope in three-man groups, focused on reaching the barricade from the side. They hadn't expected fire from above.

Four soldiers went down in the first volley. The rest scattered, breaking formation, sliding on pine needles and loose rock. One group tried to return fire uphill but the angle was terrible and Joel's people had cover behind boulders and deadfall.

"Push them back. Don't follow past the road."

The flankers advanced downhill in pairs, keeping fire on the retreating FEDRA soldiers. The left flank broke. Soldiers ran for the road, for the transports, for anything solid between them and the rifles above.

Then Joel heard the sound he'd been waiting for.

Engines. From the south. But lighter than FEDRA's Humvees. A pickup truck and a van, coming fast up the highway, and the radio crackled with Kate Schultz's voice: "Crystal Falls, Basalt inbound. Fifteen fighters. Where do you need us?"

"Eastern road, south of the transports. Block the retreat."

Schultz didn't hesitate. The Basalt fighters dismounted behind the FEDRA convoy and fanned across the road. Not attacking. Blocking. Making it clear that running south wasn't an option.

FEDRA's advance had stalled at the barricade. The two lead Humvees were disabled. The third was reversing into its own troop transports. Infantry on both flanks had been pushed back. And now their retreat route had fifteen rifles across it.

Briggs was in the third Humvee. Joel could see him through the windshield. The colonel was on his radio, shouting at someone, and Joel could see the exact moment the man's tactical assessment caught up with his ambition. The moment when the math stopped working.

White fabric appeared from the Humvee's window. Not a flag. A t-shirt. Someone's undershirt, held out on a rifle barrel.

"Cease fire!" Keiko shouted. "Cease fire!"

The shooting stopped. The valley went quiet except for engines and the river and a man somewhere on the road making a sound that was probably screaming but might have been crying. Hard to tell from the slope.

Joel came down from the tree line. Kept his rifle up. Walked to the barricade.

"Report," he said.

Keiko turned to face him. Her expression was flat and focused. Still operating on adrenaline. "Three wounded. Rico took a fragment, Paula got grazed on the arm, and..."

She stopped.

Joel looked where she was looking.

Dale Briggs was on the ground behind the left section of the barricade. He was on his back with his eyes open and a hole in his neck where a round had come through a gap in the railroad ties. His rifle was still in his hands. His binoculars lay on the ground beside him, one lens cracked.

Joel knelt beside him. Checked for a pulse he already knew wasn't there. Dale's skin was warm. The blood was still spreading into the dirt beneath his head, slow and dark, soaking into ground he'd helped defend for three years.

Dale Briggs. Militia volunteer from Day 103. The man who'd spotted fungal trace on a spruce tree during the first patrol exercise and made the threat concrete for everyone. Who'd walked thirty miles north with Marcus and Joel to scout a Beacon fortress and kept his nerve when the Sentries came. Who sat in the mess hall every morning at the same table, by the window, eating whatever was served without complaint.

Gone. A bullet through the neck.

Joel closed Dale's eyes with two fingers. Took the rifle from his hands. Set it against the barricade wall.

"Who else?" Joel asked.

"Hector Molina. He was on the wall when the grenade hit the tree line." Keiko's voice was steady but her hands were shaking. "Shrapnel. He was dead before anyone got to him."

Two. Two militia dead. Three more wounded. Joel looked at the road, at the disabled Humvees, at the white shirt still hanging from the third vehicle's window.

A cost. The first real cost.

"Hold the line," Joel said. "Nobody moves until I say."

He picked up Dale's binoculars. One lens was cracked straight across, a clean line through the center of the glass. He put them in his jacket pocket and walked toward the road.

## — Sarah —

She heard the shooting stop and came out of the maintenance building.

The wounded came first. Rico on a stretcher, shoulder wrapped in gauze that was already red. Paula walking on her own, arm pressed to her body, a cut across her bicep that Dr. Reyes was already cleaning. A third man, one of the Denver refugees, limping with a leg wound that turned out to be a ricochet bruise.

Then the dead.

They brought Dale on a stretcher made from two rifles and a jacket. Carson and Paul carried him. They set him down on the grass outside the clinic and covered him with a blanket from the supply closet. Keiko brought Hector Molina, a quiet man who'd joined the militia four months ago. His wife was pregnant with their first child.

Sarah stood over both of them. She held her clipboard to her chest and breathed through her nose and counted to ten because if she didn't she was going to start crying, and three hundred and forty-seven people needed her not to cry right now.

Three hundred and forty-seven. Not forty-nine. Two fewer than yesterday.

Marcus came through the gate ten minutes later. His shirt was torn, his arms were cut, and he moved like a man who'd been thrown into a ditch by an explosion, which was apparently what had happened. Dr. Reyes tried to examine him. He waved her off. He walked to the stretchers, looked at the blankets, and stood there.

"Dale and Hector," Sarah said.

Marcus nodded. Didn't speak.

Joel came through the gate behind him. He walked past the stretchers without looking, went to the lodge, and came back with a bottle of bourbon he'd been saving. He poured some into a tin cup and set it on the ground between the two bodies.

"They held the wall," Joel said.

Nobody said anything else for a while.

...

Out on the road, Briggs's soldiers surrendered. A hundred and seventy-three of them. Fifty-one wounded, twelve dead. The rest put down their weapons when Keiko and the Basalt fighters walked the line and made the terms plain: lay down arms, receive medical treatment, return to Grand Junction on foot. Crystal Falls would keep the vehicles and weapons. FEDRA's claim to regional authority was finished.

Briggs was escorted to the lodge under guard. He sat in the same chair where Whitcomb had sat four years ago and drank water from a clean mug.

Marcus sat across from him. Sarah stood by the door.

"You lost," Marcus said.

Briggs said nothing.

"You sent two hundred soldiers to take a settlement of three hundred and fifty people, and you lost in forty minutes. Your vehicles, your heavy weapons, twelve of your soldiers. We lost two."

"I had my reasons."

"I know your reasons. The coalition undermines your authority. The settlements you used to tax are feeding themselves without you. Pryor proved your system was broken by walking out of it. And now there's a corridor of four communities across western Colorado that doesn't need FEDRA for anything."

Briggs stared at the table. At the mug. At his hands, which were clean because he'd stayed in the Humvee while his soldiers died on the road.

"Grand Junction has six hundred people who need food, medicine, and someone to tell them the future isn't just more of the same," Marcus said. "You can go back and try to hold what's left together with the soldiers you have left. Or you can join the coalition as an equal partner. Same deal we offered four years ago. Trade. Mutual defense. Self-governance. No tribute."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you walk home with your wounded and we see you again in a year when you're desperate enough to try something else. Or desperate enough to ask for help."

Briggs looked at Sarah. She met his eyes and said nothing. She'd learned that from Joel. Saying nothing until the other person filled the silence themselves.

"I'll think about it," Briggs said.

"Take your time. You've got a long walk."

They let Briggs and his soldiers leave at sundown. Disarmed, fed, and given water for the road. A hundred and seventy-three people walking south on Highway 133 with the evening shadows stretching ahead of them.

Briggs walked at the front. He didn't look back.

---

Sarah found Marcus on the dam walkway after dark.

He was sitting on the concrete railing with his legs hanging over the edge, looking at the river. The cuts on his arms had already closed. The cracked cartilage in his ribs ached but held. His shirt was ruined.

"Noah's asleep," she said. "Martha read him a story. He asked if the bad people were gone."

"What did you say?"

"I said yes."

She sat beside him. The dam hummed beneath them. Three hundred and forty-seven people were eating dinner or sitting in their houses or holding their children or doing all the ordinary things people do after the worst day of their lives. Keiko was running double watches. Helen was logging the battle in the coalition ledger.

"Dale," Marcus said.

"I know."

"He was on the left side. The gap in the railroad ties. I knew about that gap. Joel filed a report last month asking for another steel plate on the left section. I hadn't gotten to it."

"Don't."

"If I'd..."

"Don't." Sarah took his hand. "Dale died because a man named Briggs decided that power mattered more than people. Not because of a gap in a wall."

Marcus looked at the river. The water was black in the dark, reflecting the compound lights.

"Two people," he said. "Eight years of building this place and today we lost two people in forty minutes."

"We saved three hundred and forty-seven."

"That math doesn't help."

"I know. But it's still true."

They sat together on the dam. The river ran. The lights of Crystal Falls glowed behind them. Somewhere in the mess hall, someone was talking too loud about the battle, retelling a moment with the energy of a person who'd survived something terrible and needed to hear themselves say it out loud.

Somewhere else, Hector Molina's wife was sitting in their cabin with her hand on her belly and an empty chair across the table.

Sarah leaned against Marcus's shoulder. He put his arm around her. They didn't speak again for a long time.

Tomorrow there would be funerals.
