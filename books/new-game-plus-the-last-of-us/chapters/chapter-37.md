# Chapter 37: Envoys

## — Marcus —

April 2017. Crystal Falls, Colorado.

Three weeks with enhanced hearing, and Marcus still couldn't tune out the goats.

He sat on the north ridge with a thermos of chicory and the system map open in front of him. Six fifteen in the morning. The valley was quiet in the way that meant two hundred and thirty-one people were still mostly asleep. He could hear the turbine, the creek below the dam, a woodpecker working on the dead pine near the orchard, and Margaret the goat chewing on something metallic near the garden fence.

He'd learned to sort it. That was the trick. Not blocking sounds out, because you couldn't block them, but filing them. Turbine: normal. Creek: normal. Woodpecker: normal. Margaret: annoying but not dangerous. Gene's back door opening at six seventeen: inevitable.

The map showed green dots at the settlement and clear terrain for fifteen miles in every direction. Two orange dots far to the northwest, probably elk based on the movement pattern. Nothing hostile. Nothing unusual.

Marcus drank his chicory. It still tasted terrible. Three and a half years of dandelion root and wild chicory and he hadn't gotten used to it. He missed coffee. Real coffee, from a bag, ground in a machine, poured into a mug that said something stupid on it. He'd had a mug that said "World's Okayest Gamer" in the old world. His old world. The one with electricity and grocery stores and a game called The Last of Us that he'd cried at on his couch.

He closed the map. Sat with the morning for a while.

Crystal Falls was three weeks past the council disclosure about his Tier 4 enhancements. The reaction had been roughly what Sarah predicted. Joel took it without blinking, Tommy asked three practical questions about limitations, Helen filed it in whatever mental category she kept for things she didn't fully understand but would plan around, and Dr. Reyes asked if she could run blood tests. Marcus let her. The results came back normal, or at least normal for him. His resting heart rate had dropped to forty-one beats per minute. Dr. Reyes wrote "anomalous" on the chart and moved on.

The two settlements south of Carbondale, Basalt Station and Willow Creek, had sent representatives twice in the last month. Both wanted help with infected clearing. Both offered trade in exchange for Crystal Falls running sweeps through their perimeters. The council had approved both requests. Keiko's militia team had done the first sweep last week. Eleven Runners, two Stalkers. Clean work, no injuries, and a handshake agreement for regular patrols.

This was new territory. Not the sweeps. They'd been clearing the Redstone corridor for a year. But the requests. The acknowledgment that Crystal Falls was the strongest thing in the region and other settlements knew it.

Marcus heard a truck engine.

He set the thermos down. Listened.

Not a truck. Trucks. Plural. Coming from the south on Highway 133, moving at maybe thirty miles an hour. Diesel engines. Two of them. No, three. Three vehicles, heavy, the kind of engine note that meant armor or payload or both.

He opened the map. Three red dots, six miles south and closing. The system had tagged them before he'd consciously identified the sound.

**[Alert: Unknown armed group detected]**
**[3 vehicles, estimated 12-15 personnel]**
**[Bearing: South on Highway 133]**
**[ETA at current speed: 12 minutes]**

Marcus stood. Left the thermos on the rock. Started jogging toward the gate.

---

Joel was already at the armory when Marcus arrived. Because Joel was always at the armory before anyone else, and because Joel had heard Marcus jogging and that wasn't a thing Marcus did at six thirty in the morning.

"Three vehicles from the south," Marcus said. "Armed. Twelve to fifteen people. About ten minutes out."

Joel pulled his rifle off the rack. Checked the magazine. His motions had the unhurried precision of a man who'd done this so many times the muscles didn't consult his brain anymore.

"Military?"

"Diesel engines, heavy frames. Could be military. Could be a convoy with armored trucks."

"Reckon we'll find out." Joel moved to the door and whistled twice. Sharp, carrying. The morning watch signal for Donna's team.

Within five minutes, the settlement shifted. Not panic. Not anymore. Crystal Falls had protocols for this. Helen's protocols, refined over a year of drills and two real incidents. Donna's watch team moved to the wall positions. Keiko's patrol squad, four of them on morning rotation, jogged to the gate with rifles. Joel positioned Dale and Ana on the flanks with clear sight lines to the road.

Tommy came out of the powerhouse wiping grease off his hands. "What's happenin'?"

"Company," Joel said. "From the south."

Tommy looked at Marcus. "How many?"

"Three vehicles. Probably twelve to fifteen. A few minutes out."

Tommy nodded once and went to get his rifle.

Sarah appeared at the dining hall door with her clipboard. She'd been doing the morning meal count. She looked at the gate, at Joel's rifle, at the militia moving to positions, and put the clipboard under her arm.

"What do we know?" she asked Marcus.

"Not much. Armed group, three vehicles, coming up 133. Could be anything."

"Could be Basalt or Willow Creek sending a delegation."

"In three diesel trucks with a dozen people? No."

She looked at the gate for a second. Then she looked at Marcus.

"You handle it," she said. "I'll keep the compound calm."

She went back inside. Marcus heard her voice through the wall, even and practical, telling the breakfast crew that they had visitors coming and everything was fine.

Marcus walked to the gate. Joel fell in beside him.

"Don't shoot first," Marcus said.

"Wasn't plannin' to." Joel paused. "But I'm bringin' the rifle."

"Wouldn't expect anything else."

They stood at the gate and waited. The morning was cold. Marcus could hear the vehicles clearly now, the rumble of diesel growing louder, punctuated by the grind of tires on broken asphalt. He could smell exhaust. Not civilian exhaust. The particular oily burn of military diesel.

The first vehicle came around the bend in the valley road at six forty-two. An up-armored Humvee, olive drab, with a mounted .50 cal on the roof ring. The gunner was in place, not aiming at anything specific but present. The second vehicle was identical. The third was a cargo truck, canvas-covered, riding lower on its springs than an empty truck would.

FEDRA.

Marcus had known they'd come eventually. Crystal Falls had electricity, agriculture, a growing population, and a reputation. You couldn't be the only functioning town in a hundred miles and stay invisible forever.

The Humvees stopped a hundred yards from the gate. The engines idled. Nobody got out.

Marcus looked at Joel. Joel's face was doing the thing it did when he was running probabilities. Eyes narrow, jaw forward slightly. Not tense. Calculating.

"QZ plates," Joel said. "Grand Junction, maybe. They've been the closest active zone for two years."

"Yeah."

"Twelve people for three trucks is light. Enough for security, not enough for a raid."

"So it's a conversation."

"Could be a conversation that turns into somethin' else."

Marcus nodded. "I'll go out. You stay at the gate with the team."

Joel looked at him. The look said a lot of things Joel wasn't going to say out loud.

"If it goes wrong, I heal fast now," Marcus said. "You don't."

Joel didn't argue. He stepped back to the gate, rifle across his chest, and positioned himself where the FEDRA people could see him clearly. A visible reminder that the settlement had teeth.

Marcus walked out alone.

---

He crossed the hundred yards at a normal pace. No hurry. No tension in his posture. Just a man walking toward three military vehicles on a Colorado morning with his hands visible and his senses tracking every heartbeat in every truck.

The front Humvee's passenger door opened. A woman stepped out. Mid-forties, trim, wearing a FEDRA uniform with captain's bars and a sidearm she didn't touch. Her hair was cropped short, going gray at the temples. She moved like someone who'd spent a career giving orders and having them followed.

From the second Humvee, a man in civilian clothes. Fifties, thin, wire-rimmed glasses, carrying a leather binder. He looked like an accountant who'd survived the end of the world by being too useful to leave behind.

Six soldiers climbed out of the vehicles after them. Rifles, body armor, helmets. They formed a loose line behind the captain. Not aggressive. Posturing.

The captain walked forward. Stopped ten feet from Marcus. Her eyes moved past him to the gate, the wall, the rooftops of Crystal Falls visible beyond. She was counting. Assessing. Marcus could hear her heart rate. Seventy-eight beats per minute. Elevated but controlled. She was nervous, but she'd been nervous before and knew how to work through it.

"Morning," Marcus said.

"Good morning." She had a flat, professional voice. Midwest, maybe. Trained out of any accent by years of military service. "I'm Captain Whitcomb, Federal Disaster Response Agency, Grand Junction garrison. This is Administrator Calloway."

The man in glasses nodded. His heart rate was eighty-four. More nervous than the captain. His hands held the binder against his chest like a shield.

"Marcus," Marcus said. He didn't offer a last name. He didn't offer a title. "Welcome to Crystal Falls."

Whitcomb looked at the settlement again. Her expression shifted slightly. Marcus caught it because he could see the micro-movements now, the way her eyes widened a fraction when they tracked from the reinforced gate to the rooftops to the greenhouse complex on the hillside.

She hadn't expected this. Whatever intelligence she'd been given about the settlement, what she was seeing didn't match.

"You have power," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Hydroelectric. The dam's been running since Day 43."

"Day 43."

"Of the outbreak. We got it operational about six weeks in."

Calloway wrote something in the binder. His pen scratched against cheap paper. Marcus could hear the specific frequency of ballpoint on notebook stock. The same sound Sarah's pen made.

"We've been hearing about this place for a while," Whitcomb said. "Reports from travelers passing through our zone. A settlement with electricity, farmland, two hundred-plus population. Self-governing." The last word carried a slight emphasis. Like "self-governing" was a category that needed evaluation.

"That's right," Marcus said.

"How many people?"

"Two hundred and thirty-one."

Whitcomb's eyebrows moved a fraction. Two hundred and thirty-one was more than she'd expected. Marcus heard her heart rate tick up to eighty-two.

"That's a significant population for an independent settlement," Calloway said. His voice was reedy, careful. The voice of a man who chose his words for legal precision. "Under the Federal Emergency Continuity Act, settlements above fifty persons are expected to register with the nearest FEDRA garrison and enter into a cooperative framework."

"I'm not familiar with that act," Marcus said. Which was true. It was also irrelevant.

"It was established in 2014," Calloway continued. "Under executive authority. The framework provides for mutual defense, resource sharing, and administrative coordination between FEDRA-recognized settlements and the governing authority."

Marcus let the silence sit for two seconds. A trick he'd picked up from watching Joel. Silence made people fill space, and the space they filled was usually more honest than the words they'd planned.

Calloway filled the space. "In practical terms, registered settlements contribute a percentage of food production and labor to the regional garrison. In exchange, they receive military protection, medical supplies, and priority access to FEDRA supply chains."

"What percentage?" Marcus asked.

"Fifteen percent of surplus food production. Labor contributions are negotiated individually."

"And what does military protection look like?"

Whitcomb took this one. "A FEDRA patrol would include your settlement in its circuit. Regular visits. Threat assessment. Coordinated response to infected incursions or hostile groups."

Marcus looked past her at the six soldiers. Young. Early twenties, most of them. Their rifles were clean, but their body armor was worn. Patched in places. The Humvees had rust on the wheel wells. A cracked windshield on the second vehicle. The .50 cal mount on the lead Humvee had a stripped bolt that someone had replaced with a zip tie.

This wasn't a garrison operating from strength. This was a garrison stretching what it had.

He listened to the soldiers' heartbeats. All elevated. Ninety to a hundred BPM. Young men standing in front of a settlement they'd been told might be hostile, trying to look like they had the situation handled.

"Captain Whitcomb," Marcus said. "How many people does the Grand Junction QZ have?"

She paused. A half-second. Deciding what to share.

"Approximately eight hundred."

"And how's your food situation?"

Another pause. Longer.

"Adequate."

Her heart rate jumped to ninety-one on that word. She was lying. Or at least stretching the truth far enough that her body registered the effort.

"I'll be direct," Marcus said. "We've been here for three and a half years. We built this from nothing. No FEDRA support, no government framework, no supply chain. We generate our own power, grow our own food, run our own patrols, and clear our own infected. We've taken in every refugee who showed up at our gate, and we've never turned anyone away."

Calloway opened his mouth. Marcus kept talking.

"We're not hostile to FEDRA. We're not competing with you. But we're not in a position where we need protection, and we're not going to hand over fifteen percent of our food production to a garrison eighty miles away."

Whitcomb's jaw shifted. Not a reaction she'd have let show if she'd known it was visible. The look of someone running numbers and not liking the answer.

"Mr. Marcus," Calloway said, "refusal to register carries certain implications under the Continuity Act."

"Such as?"

"Non-registered settlements forfeit protection status. They're classified as independent and potentially non-compliant. FEDRA resources would not be allocated to assist them."

"That's fine. We haven't needed FEDRA resources so far."

"There are also enforcement provisions," Calloway said. His voice stayed even, but his heart rate hit ninety. He was reading from a script now, not a binder, and the script had a part in it he didn't enjoy saying. "For settlements that interfere with FEDRA operations or harbor personnel absent without leave."

Marcus looked at him. Looked at Whitcomb. Looked at the soldiers.

"Are you threatening us, Administrator?"

Calloway adjusted his glasses. "I'm explaining the legal framework."

"Here's what I see," Marcus said. He kept his voice conversational. The way you talk about weather or crop yields or what's for dinner. "I see three vehicles with twelve soldiers. Good soldiers. Young, well-trained, professional. But they're tired. Their gear is worn. The lead Humvee has a stripped bolt on the gun mount held together with a zip tie. I see a garrison that controls a zone eighty miles from here and is probably responsible for fifty, sixty miles of territory around it. And I see two people who drove a long way to offer protection to a settlement that doesn't need it, in exchange for food that you probably do need."

He let that sit.

Whitcomb's expression didn't change, but her heart rate did. Ninety-three. She was a professional hearing a professional, and both of them knew the math didn't work in her favor.

"I'm not looking for a fight," Marcus said. "And I don't think you are either. So here's what I'll offer instead. Crystal Falls will trade with Grand Junction. Food, medical supplies, materials, whatever makes sense. Fair exchange. No tribute. No registration. No outside authority over our governance. Two communities helping each other because that's what should happen when people are still alive."

Whitcomb studied him. Her eyes moved to the gate again, where Joel stood with his rifle and eight militia members visible behind the wall. Then back to Marcus.

"That's not what I was authorized to offer," she said.

"I understand. Take it back to your commanding officer. We'll be here."

Calloway started to say something. Whitcomb raised a hand, barely a motion, and he stopped. She was the one running this. The administrator was the script. She was the judgment.

"You said you handle your own infected clearing," she said.

"We do. We've been running regular sweeps for over a year. We also clear for two settlements south of Carbondale."

"We've been losing ground south of Glenwood Springs. A cluster of Stalkers in the old resort area. We've sent two teams. Neither one cleared it."

Marcus filed that. Stalkers were hard for conventional military. They hid, they watched, they waited. FEDRA trained for direct engagement, not ambush predators that coordinated attacks from the dark.

"We could take a look," Marcus said. "If you're interested in that kind of arrangement."

"I might be."

They stood there for a moment. Two people on a gravel road in Colorado, figuring out whether the next decade would be cooperation or conflict. The morning sun was above the ridge. A meadowlark was singing in the trees behind the Humvees. One of the soldiers shifted his weight from his left foot to his right. The sound of the kid's boot scraping on gravel was loud in the silence.

"I'll take your proposal back," Whitcomb said. "Colonel Briggs will want to hear it directly."

"He's welcome to visit. Through the gate, not past it."

Whitcomb almost smiled. The corner of her mouth moved a fraction of an inch. Then she turned, nodded to Calloway, and walked back to the Humvee.

The soldiers loaded up. Doors closed. The engines started in sequence, one, two, three. The convoy backed up, turned on the narrow road, and headed south. Marcus listened to the diesel rumble fade until it dropped below the sound of the creek and the turbine and Margaret chewing on the fence.

He stood on the road for another minute. Then he walked back to the gate.

## — Joel —

Joel was waiting inside the gate with his rifle still across his chest.

"Well?" he said.

"Grand Junction QZ. Captain named Whitcomb, a bureaucrat named Calloway. They wanted us to register under some federal continuity act and pay tribute. Fifteen percent of food production."

"What'd you tell 'em?"

"No."

Joel nodded. That was the right answer and he'd known it would be. FEDRA took and took and called it governance. He'd seen it in Austin before the city fell. He'd heard the same story from every refugee who'd walked through Crystal Falls' gate from a collapsed QZ. FEDRA kept order until it couldn't, skimmed resources until people starved, and pulled out when things got ugly enough. The Houston QZ. The Denver QZ. Same pattern every time. Somebody with bars on their collar deciding they knew best and a lot of ordinary people paying for it.

"How many soldiers?" Joel asked.

"Twelve. Young. Their gear's been patched more than once. The lead Humvee has a stripped bolt on the gun mount held on with a zip tie."

A zip tie on a weapons mount. Joel processed that. It told you everything you needed to know about a garrison's supply situation. You didn't jury-rig a Ma Deuce unless you were out of real parts and out of options for getting more.

"They ain't strong," Joel said.

"No."

"But they know about us now. Whoever sent 'em knows what they saw. Two hundred people, power, farms, militia at the wall in five minutes."

"A Colonel Briggs. Whitcomb said she'd take our counter-offer back to him."

Joel leaned against the armory wall. The rifle hung from his shoulder, strap worn smooth from three years of daily carry. He'd replaced the strap twice. Never the rifle.

"You know what happens next," Joel said.

"They come back with a better offer. Or they come back with more trucks."

"And if it's more trucks?"

Marcus looked at him. Joel looked back. Two men who'd been through enough together that they didn't bother with the polite version of things.

"Then they find out what Tier 4 means," Marcus said.

Joel didn't smile. But he didn't argue, either. He pushed off the wall and headed toward the workshop, where Carson was already starting the morning's first project. The sound of a handsaw on pine carried across the compound. Steady, rhythmic. The sound of somebody building something in a world that mostly tore things apart.

## — Marcus —

The council met at noon. All seven seats filled. Tommy, Helen, Dr. Reyes, Donna, Sarah, Joel, and Marcus.

Sarah ran the meeting. She'd already gotten the full account from Marcus and spent the morning working through implications while he'd stood on a road and Joel had sharpened knives in the armory.

"First," she said. "The good news. They came to talk, not to fight. That tells us something about their strength."

"Tells us they can't take us by force," Helen said. "Not with twelve soldiers."

"Tells us they think they might need to try eventually," Tommy said. He was leaning back in his chair, arms folded. The posture of a man who'd served in one military and was now looking at another one from the outside.

"They have eight hundred people," Marcus said. "But their gear is running down and their food situation is worse than they let on. Whitcomb's answer on food supply didn't hold up under basic questioning."

"So they're hungry," Sarah said. "That makes them either a trading partner or a threat."

"Both," Joel said. "Hungry people with guns are always both."

"The Continuity Act language is worth noting," Sarah said. She'd written it down from Marcus's account, word for word, in the margin of the agenda page. "Enforcement provisions. Non-compliance classification. They're using legal scaffolding to justify what's basically a protection racket."

"Every QZ runs the same playbook," Tommy said. "FEDRA was never about governance. It was about control. The law talk is just how they dress it up."

"But they also mentioned a Stalker cluster south of Glenwood Springs," Marcus said. "They've sent two teams and failed to clear it. I offered to help."

Helen looked up from her notes. "Why?"

"Because it costs us very little and it shows them what we can do. If they see our militia clear something their soldiers couldn't, they'll think twice about the alternative."

The table was quiet for a moment. The kind of quiet that happened when everyone was thinking the same thing and waiting for someone else to say it.

Sarah said it. "Crystal Falls just became a political entity. Not a settlement. Not a community project. A power that other powers have to account for. We need to start acting like it."

"What's that mean, practically?" Donna asked.

"It means we need a framework for how we deal with outsiders. Basalt and Willow Creek already come to us for clearing operations. Now FEDRA knows about us. Eventually the Fireflies will hear about us too, if they haven't already. We can't just improvise with each group. We need principles."

Marcus nodded. "We trade fairly. We help with infected clearing when asked. We don't pay tribute to anyone. We don't accept outside authority over our governance. And we don't start fights."

"But we finish them," Joel added.

Sarah wrote it down. Five lines on the clipboard. The foreign policy of Crystal Falls, Colorado, drafted in a dining hall that smelled like chicory and pine shavings by seven people sitting around a table Frank Garvey had built from scrap lumber on Day 52.

"I'll write it up properly for next Monday's community meeting," Sarah said. "Everyone should know that FEDRA came and what we told them. No rumors. Just facts."

She moved to the next agenda item. The Glenwood Springs Stalker cluster. Whether to send Keiko's team now as a goodwill gesture, or wait for a formal request from Whitcomb. The council discussed it for eight minutes and decided to wait. Extending help before it was asked for would look eager. Letting Whitcomb ask would establish the dynamic they wanted: Crystal Falls as a partner, not a subordinate.

The meeting lasted fifty-three minutes. Three over the old average. The problems were growing.

Marcus stood on the dining hall porch afterward and listened to the compound. The school bell, faint and tinny. Raymond's voice in the kitchen, organizing lunch prep. Keiko calling out drill formations at the south gate. Carson's saw. The turbine's hum, steady as always. The creek. Margaret, still alive, still chewing on something she shouldn't be.

Two hundred and thirty-one people. And now a FEDRA garrison eighty miles south that knew exactly what they'd built.

**[Quest Updated: Expand Influence]**
**[Objective: Establish diplomatic relations with external factions]**
**[Progress: 1/3 — FEDRA Grand Junction: Contact established]**
**[Points: 1,801]**

Marcus closed the notification. Looked south, toward the road the Humvees had taken. The exhaust smell was gone. The meadowlark had moved on. Just the valley and the mountains and the ordinary noise of a town getting ready for lunch.

The old world had come knocking. Crystal Falls had answered the door and said no thank you. Whether the old world took no for an answer was a question for another day.

He went inside. Raymond was making bean soup. It smelled better than the chicory.
