# Chapter 46: The Corridor

## — Tommy —

He left before sunrise.

Joel was at the gate, leaning against the post with a coffee mug that had a chip in the rim. The same mug he'd been drinking from since 2017. Tommy had offered to buy him a new one from the Mall. Joel had looked at him like he'd suggested shaving Captain the dog.

"Radio check every four hours," Joel said.

"I know."

"If they're hostile, you don't try to talk your way out. You leave."

"Joel."

"You leave."

Tommy took the mug from his brother's hand and drank the last two inches of coffee. It was bitter and barely warm. "I'll be back by tomorrow night. It's forty miles, not the Oregon Trail."

Joel took the mug back. Looked at the empty inside. Looked at Tommy.

"Take the long-range rifle."

"I'm bringin' Frank and Ana. We'll be fine."

"Take the rifle anyway."

Tommy took the rifle.

The truck — a different one from Sarah's flatbed, a Chevy Silverado with a cracked windshield and a winch Tommy had installed himself — grumbled to life on the second try. Frank Garvey rode shotgun. Ana Gutierrez sat in the bed with a pack and a radio and the particular alertness of someone on their third scout rotation. They pulled out of the gate at five-fifteen, heading east on the old highway toward the Fryingpan River drainage.

The road was empty. Early June in the Colorado Rockies. Snowmelt in the creeks, wildflowers in the ditches, sky going from grey to blue overhead. Tommy drove with the window down. The air smelled like pine and cold water and nothing burning.

Forty miles to Willow Creek. A settlement he'd heard about secondhand from a trader who'd passed through Basalt two months ago. Forty people, maybe fewer. A ranching community in a narrow valley east of the Sawatch Range. They'd survived by being remote and hard to reach, which was also why they were struggling. Isolation kept the infected out. It also kept everything else out.

"What do we know about who's in charge?" Frank asked.

"Name's Harlan Cope. The trader said he's older. Rancher. Ran cattle before the outbreak. Kept his property and most of his neighbors alive through the first winter by slaughtering his herd and salting the meat."

"Practical."

"That's one word for it. He killed his entire livelihood to keep twenty people fed. The trader said he hasn't smiled since."

Frank nodded. He understood that kind of sacrifice. He'd walked away from a teaching career in Aspen to join a group of strangers at a dam because someone had left the lights on. Everyone who'd survived this long had given up something they couldn't get back.

Tommy shifted gears as the road climbed toward the pass. They'd be over the top by seven if the road held.

...

The road held.

## — Marcus —

Noah was drawing at the kitchen table when Marcus came down.

The drawings had changed over the past year. When Noah first arrived, age five, starving and silent, his drawings were all one color. Black crayon. Circles and lines that didn't connect to anything recognizable. Dr. Reyes had said it was normal. Processing.

Now, at six and a half, Noah drew in color. He drew the dam, the chickens, Captain the dog, the truck, the council table, Sarah's garden. He drew people with circle heads and stick arms and smiles that took up half their faces. He drew Marcus with a knife and Joel with a guitar and Sarah with a clipboard. He drew himself between them, always between them, anchored to the family by the physical space he occupied on the page.

This morning he was drawing the mountains.

"Those the Elk Range?" Marcus asked.

Noah shook his head. "Those are the ones Tommy went to."

"The Sawatch?"

"The ones with the snow still on."

Close enough. Marcus poured water from the pitcher and sat down across from his son. The kitchen was quiet. Sarah had left an hour ago for the council prep meeting. The house smelled like the oatmeal she'd made before leaving, the bowl she'd left covered on the counter for Marcus with a note that said EAT.

He ate. He watched Noah draw. He opened the system interface.

**[Status: Marcus Webb]**
**[Level: 47]**
**[Points: 7,504]**
**[Tier 5 Progress: 76%]**

**[Requirements:]**
**[— Enemy Classes Eliminated: 8/8 ✓]**
**[— Settlement Population: 349/500]**
**[— ████████████████: ██/██]**

Eight of eight. Done. The Sentry kill two days ago had closed that line for good. Marcus stared at the green checkmark next to it and felt the particular satisfaction of finishing something that had taken eight years without knowing it was being tracked.

The second requirement was the problem.

Three hundred and forty-nine people. He needed five hundred. A gap of one hundred and fifty-one. Crystal Falls had grown from four people to three hundred and forty-nine over eight years, but the growth rate had slowed. The easy absorptions were done. Every QZ refugee within two hundred miles who was going to find them had already found them. The Denver caravan had been the last major influx, and that was over a year ago.

One hundred and fifty-one people. Not bodies. People with skills, needs, families, mouths to feed, disputes to settle, houses to build, patrols to walk. Every new resident meant more food, more water, more firewood, more of everything. Sarah managed the logistics and she was the best there was, but even she couldn't create supply out of nothing.

That was Marcus's job. And the system's.

He ran the math. If the coalition brought Glenwood Springs (123 people) and Willow Creek (~40 people) into the network, and if some of those people eventually migrated to Crystal Falls the way Basalt residents had started doing, the population could reach five hundred in two to three years. Maybe faster if word spread. Maybe slower if a Beacon horde hit before then.

The third requirement was the real problem.

Redacted. Blacked out. The system had shown him three conditions for Tier 5 and hidden one of them behind a wall of black rectangles. Marcus had poked at it, queried it, tried every system command he could think of. Nothing. The requirement existed. He couldn't read it. He couldn't plan for it. He couldn't even guess whether he was making progress on it or not.

"That's going to be something terrible, isn't it," he thought. The system had a pattern. It rewarded diverse action, punished passivity, and consistently pushed him toward harder, broader challenges. The first two requirements — kill everything, grow the community — were straightforward. The third one would be the one that cost something.

Noah looked up from his drawing. "Dad?"

"Yeah?"

"Can I have more green?"

Marcus reached into the crayon box and handed him the green. Noah went back to drawing mountains. Marcus closed the system interface and finished his oatmeal.

Some mornings the apocalypse was just a man eating breakfast with his kid.

## — Tommy —

Willow Creek wasn't a town. It was a collection of buildings that happened to be near each other.

Tommy counted them from the ridge above the valley. A main ranch house, two-story, stone and timber. A barn that had been converted into something — housing, maybe, based on the curtains in the hayloft windows. Three smaller cabins along the creek. A greenhouse made from old window frames and PVC pipe. A fenced area with goats. A vegetable garden. A hand-painted sign on the access road that read WILLOW CREEK — FRIENDLY.

The sign had bullet holes in it.

"Friendly," Ana said from the truck bed.

"The bullet holes are old," Tommy said. He'd been looking at bullet holes for eight years. These were weathered. Rain-rusted. At least a year, probably two.

"Still."

"Stay with the truck. Frank and I walk in. If we're not back in thirty minutes, drive home."

"Tommy."

"It's the protocol."

Ana didn't argue. She pulled the long-range rifle from behind the seat and settled into position on the truck bed, sighting down the valley. Professional. Joel would have approved.

Tommy and Frank walked down the access road with their hands visible. Two hundred yards. A hundred. The front gate was a cattle gate wired shut with baling wire and a padlock that looked like it had been oiled recently. Someone was maintaining it.

A man appeared on the ranch house porch. Sixties. Tall, stooped, with a sun-weathered face and a bolt-action rifle held loosely at his side. He wore a flannel shirt that had been washed so many times the pattern was more suggestion than color. His boots were muddy. His eyes were not friendly, but they were not hostile either. They were the eyes of a man who had stopped expecting things a long time ago.

"Help you?" he said.

"Harlan Cope?"

"Depends on who's askin'."

"Tommy Miller. Crystal Falls settlement. We're about forty miles west, near Marble."

Cope didn't move. Didn't lower the rifle. Didn't raise it either.

"I heard about Crystal Falls," he said. "The place with the dam and the lights."

"That's us."

"Heard you've got a man who can kill a hundred infected in an afternoon."

"That's also us."

Cope studied them for a long time. The kind of study that measured whether someone was worth the calories of a conversation. Then he leaned the rifle against the porch railing and sat down on the top step.

"Come on up, then. Martha's got tea."

Martha was his wife. She brought out three mugs of something brewed from dried mint and honey. The honey was thin and dark, from a hive they'd found in a dead oak two miles upstream. It was the best thing Tommy had tasted in weeks.

They sat on the porch. Below them, the valley unfolded. The creek ran through the center, bordered by willows — the settlement's namesake. A few people moved between the buildings. Tommy counted eight. The trader had said forty. Either the rest were out working or the number had dropped.

"How many do you have?" Tommy asked.

"Thirty-seven." Cope said it without emotion. The number was a fact. "Forty-one in January. Lost four. Two to infection, one to a hunting accident, one just walked south and didn't come back."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. It's the math. We lose two or three a year. We gain one or two if we're lucky. The line goes down."

Tommy understood the math. Crystal Falls had Marcus and the system to offset it. Willow Creek had goats and mint tea and a man who'd slaughtered his own cattle to keep people alive and hadn't smiled since.

"Mr. Cope. We're buildin' a coalition. Crystal Falls, Basalt Station, and hopefully Glenwood Springs. Shared defense, shared information, messenger routes between settlements. No tribute. No authority over your people. You run Willow Creek. We run Crystal Falls. But when a threat comes — infected or otherwise — we face it together."

Cope sipped his tea. Looked at the valley. Looked at the goats.

"What kind of threats?"

Tommy told him about the Beacons. The signal nodes. The Sentries. The horde that had hit Crystal Falls three weeks ago. He told it plainly, the way Joel would have — no softening, no sales pitch, just the facts of what was out there and what it meant for forty miles of mountain valley between here and the next settlement.

Cope listened. Martha listened from inside the screen door. When Tommy finished, Cope set his mug on the step.

"We found one," he said.

Tommy stopped.

"Two months ago. East of here, in the old ski resort near the pass. A building full of infected that weren't actin' right. Moving together. Watching the doors. We lost two people finding out what was in there. Didn't go back."

"That's a Beacon."

"Didn't know the name. Just knew it was bad."

"Did the infected from that building spread? Move toward your settlement?"

"Not yet. But we've been seeing more Runners on the east road. Three last week. Used to be one a month."

Tommy filed it. A fourth Beacon. East of Willow Creek, near a ski resort on a mountain pass. The Beacon network wasn't just in the Roaring Fork Valley. It extended east, across the Sawatch Range, into territory nobody from Crystal Falls had scouted.

"Mr. Cope, that's exactly the kind of information that saves lives. If you'd had a way to tell us about that two months ago, we could've sent a team."

"I don't have a way to tell you anything. Forty miles is a long way when you've got thirty-seven people and no vehicle."

Tommy reached into his pack and pulled out the radio. One of three Tommy had rigged from salvaged parts. Battery-powered, crystal-tuned to Crystal Falls' frequency, range of about sixty miles in mountain terrain if you got to high ground.

"This is a start," he said.

Cope took the radio. Turned it over in his hands. It was heavy and ugly and built from pieces of things that used to be other things. But it worked. Tommy had tested all three before leaving.

"We check in every morning at oh-seven-hundred," Tommy said. "You report infected contacts, unusual movement, anything that changes. We do the same. If you spot a Beacon or anything that looks like one, you call. We come."

"And if I call and nobody answers?"

"Then somethin' bad happened to us too, and you should start walkin' west."

Cope almost smiled. Almost.

Martha came out and refilled the mugs. Frank had been talking to a man by the barn — a former mechanic named Luis who'd been trying to fix a generator with no parts for six months. Frank was already sketching a wiring diagram on a piece of cardboard.

"The mutual defense part," Cope said. "What does that look like?"

"You get hit, you call. We send people. We get hit, we call. You send what you can. Nobody's expectin' Willow Creek to field an army. But even a few extra rifles at a barricade change the math."

"And the trade?"

"We've got medical supplies, tools, and a doctor. You've got goats and honey and a Beacon location we didn't know about. Seems like a fair start."

Cope looked at Martha. She nodded. A single nod, the kind that came from thirty-plus years of marriage where most conversations happened without words.

"All right," Cope said. "We're in."

Tommy shook his hand. The grip was rough and strong and held on a beat longer than necessary. The grip of a man who'd been carrying something alone for eight years and had just been told he didn't have to anymore.

"One more thing," Cope said. "That Beacon east of here. If your man can kill it, I'd appreciate it. My people won't go near the east road anymore. It's cuttin' us off from half our grazing land."

"I'll bring it to the council."

"Appreciate it."

Frank finished his wiring diagram and gave it to Luis with a list of parts that Crystal Falls could supply. Luis looked at the diagram like it was a love letter. Tommy made a note to bring Frank on every diplomatic mission from now on. The man built trust with cardboard and a pencil faster than anyone Tommy had ever met.

They left at noon. The drive back was quiet. Ana cleaned the rifle in the truck bed. Frank dozed with his head against the window. Tommy drove and thought about the fourth Beacon and the line that goes down and a man who hadn't smiled in eight years.

...

Forty miles of mountain road. Three hours. The gate at Crystal Falls appeared around the last bend and Tommy felt the thing he always felt coming home. Not relief exactly. More like the tension leaving a cable that had been holding too much weight.

## — Marcus —

The first messenger arrived from Basalt at two in the afternoon.

A kid. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. He'd run seven miles from Basalt Station on foot, carrying a leather pouch with a folded piece of paper inside. Helen met him at the gate, gave him water and a chair in the shade, and brought the message to Sarah's office.

Sarah read it aloud at the council table. Marcus, Joel, Helen, and Donna were present. Tommy wasn't back yet.

"Kate Schultz reports no infected contacts within Basalt's patrol radius for the past six days. Their eastern patrol spotted old tracks — possibly Runner — on the road toward Snowmass, but nothing fresh. She's requesting a copy of our Beacon identification criteria so her patrols know what to look for."

Helen wrote the date and contents in the coalition log she'd started that morning. A leather-bound ledger she'd found in the Marble Mercantile during the early scavenging days. Neat handwriting. Time stamps. Origin settlement. Contents. Action items.

"I'll draft the Beacon criteria document tonight," Marcus said. "Visual signs, map patterns, behavioral indicators. Keep it simple enough that a patrol team with no system access can use it."

"I'll write a cover letter," Sarah said. "Tone matters. This is the first official communication in the coalition. It should read like partners, not like headquarters issuing orders."

Joel, who had been cleaning a pistol at the far end of the table, looked up. "Tell her about the Sentries."

"We should," Marcus agreed. "If Basalt's patrols encounter a Beacon with Sentries, they need to know not to approach. The mycelium network changes the rules."

"Include retreat protocols," Joel said. "Minimum safe distance. How to move without triggering the ground sensors. What to do if a patrol goes quiet."

Helen wrote it all down. Action items: Beacon criteria document (Marcus), cover letter (Sarah), Sentry protocols and retreat procedures (Joel). Due by end of day tomorrow.

The runner from Basalt ate lunch in the mess hall, slept for an hour in the guest quarters, and jogged back with a reply confirming receipt and requesting a follow-up meeting. The system worked. Slow, human-powered, dependent on a teenager with good lungs, but it worked.

At four-thirty, Glenwood Springs sent a runner of their own.

Not a full response. A single line from Diane Pryor: "Send your greenhouse expert. We'll send ours. Information exchange begins Thursday."

Sarah read it twice. Then she smiled.

"She's in," Helen said.

"She's not in the coalition yet. She agreed to share greenhouse techniques."

"Same thing. She's opening the door. She just needs it to be her idea."

Sarah put the message in the coalition log. "Schedule Lena for Thursday. She'll need the truck."

## — Marcus —

Tommy rolled through the gate at six-fifteen, just as the sun dropped behind the western ridge.

Marcus met him at the truck. Joel was already there, which meant Joel had been watching the road since five. Tommy climbed out looking like a man who'd spent eight hours on bad roads and come home with something worth the drive.

"Willow Creek's in," Tommy said.

"Tell me."

They walked to the lodge. Sarah joined them with the coalition log. Tommy talked while he ate — elk stew from the mess hall, corn bread, the last of the spring onions from Lena's greenhouse.

Harlan Cope. Thirty-seven people. Goats, honey, mint tea, a generator that didn't work, and a man who'd slaughtered his herd to keep his neighbors alive. Tommy laid it out the way he laid out everything — clear, practical, no embellishment. He gave Cope a radio. Cope gave him a handshake and a Beacon location.

The room went quiet on the Beacon.

"East of Willow Creek," Tommy said. "Old ski resort near the pass. Cope lost two people to it. His patrols won't go east anymore."

Marcus pulled the mental map. Crystal Falls at the center. Basalt Station, twelve miles southwest. Glenwood Springs, thirty-two miles northwest. Willow Creek, forty miles east. And now a Beacon east of Willow Creek, in terrain nobody from Crystal Falls had scouted, blocking Cope's access to half his grazing land.

Four Beacons identified. Two killed (Carbondale and Snowmass). One fortified and dangerous near I-70, still awaiting Joel's controlled burn plan. And now a fourth, east of the Sawatch Range, in unknown territory.

"The fungal network's not just in our valley," Marcus said. "It's regional."

"Looks that way," Joel said. He'd been quiet through Tommy's report. Processing. Joel always processed before he spoke. "Every direction we look, there's another one."

"Which means there are more we haven't found."

Sarah wrote the Beacon location in the coalition log. Drew a rough map on the facing page. Crystal Falls in the center. Lines radiating outward to the three settlements. X marks for known and suspected Beacons.

"Look at it," she said.

They looked. The settlements formed a rough triangle across the western Colorado corridor. Crystal Falls at the apex, Basalt southwest, Glenwood northwest, Willow Creek east. The Beacons were distributed around them like pressure points on a body. One near each major route in or out of the valley network.

"They're blocking the passes," Marcus said.

"Or growing toward populations," Joel said. "People make noise, heat, vibration. The Beacons might be seedin' toward us, not away."

"Either way, the coalition isn't just politics anymore," Sarah said. "It's a detection grid. Four settlements watching four directions. Shared patrols and messenger routes covering the gaps. If a Beacon seeds within fifty miles of any of us, someone sees it."

She tapped the map. The triangle of settlements. The web of routes between them.

"That's a corridor," she said. "Not a coalition. A corridor. Connected territory with shared defense and shared eyes."

Marcus looked at the map. Three hundred and forty-nine people in Crystal Falls. Sixty-three in Basalt. A hundred and twenty-three in Glenwood Springs. Thirty-seven in Willow Creek.

Five hundred and seventy-two people in the corridor.

He didn't say the number out loud. But he looked at the system interface in his peripheral vision, at the requirement line that read 349/500, and thought about what it would mean if the system counted coalition population, not just Crystal Falls.

It probably didn't. The system was specific about its requirements. Settlement population meant settlement population. But the corridor created pathways. Trade routes brought people. People brought skills. Skills attracted more people. The line between "Crystal Falls resident" and "coalition member who relocated" was going to get blurry fast.

One hundred and fifty-one people. A gap that was shrinking from both ends — Crystal Falls growing inward, the corridor pulling outward.

"We need to deal with Cope's Beacon," Marcus said. "Before it grows. Before it breeds Sentries and cuts Willow Creek off completely."

"Add it to the list," Joel said. "After the I-70 Beacon."

"Before."

Joel looked at him.

"Cope's Beacon is threatening a settlement of thirty-seven people with no combat capability. The I-70 Beacon is a fortress that needs a coordinated burn. Cope's is probably smaller, younger, and killable with a small team. We clear it first, prove the coalition works, and buy time to plan the big one."

Joel considered this. "Take Keiko and Dale. Three-day run. I'll have the burn plan ready for the I-70 site by the time you're back."

"Done."

Sarah closed the coalition log. The leather cover was already worn at the spine from Helen's daily entries. Two weeks of a coalition and the book was filling up.

"Glenwood Springs sent a message," Sarah said. "Pryor wants to exchange greenhouse experts. She's opening the door."

Tommy grinned. The first real grin since he'd gotten home. "Told you she'd come around."

"She hasn't joined the coalition."

"Give it a month. Once Lena shows her how to grow tomatoes in December, she'll be sendin' us Christmas cards."

Sarah rolled her eyes. Joel snorted. Marcus leaned back in his chair and looked at the map Sarah had drawn. Four dots. Four lines. A triangle of settlements connected by bad roads and messenger runners and a shared understanding that the ground beneath them was alive and growing and watching.

The western Colorado corridor. Not a government, not a nation, not even a formal alliance yet. Just four clusters of people who'd decided to look out for each other across forty miles of mountain and valley and the thing that grew underneath.

Noah came downstairs in his pajamas and climbed into Marcus's lap without asking. He had a drawing in his hand. The mountains again, but this time with four houses scattered across the page and lines connecting them. Roads, maybe. Or telephone wires. Or the idea that things could be connected even when they were far apart.

"What's this one?" Marcus asked.

"That's us," Noah said. He pointed to the houses. "And those are the other ones. And the lines are so they can talk."

Marcus looked at Sarah. Sarah looked at the coalition log. Tommy looked at the map. Joel looked at Noah's drawing and said nothing, which was Joel's way of saying everything.

"Yeah," Marcus said. "That's exactly right."
