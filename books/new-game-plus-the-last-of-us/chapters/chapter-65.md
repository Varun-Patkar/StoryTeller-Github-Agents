# Chapter 65: Portrait

*Marcus's POV*

July 2033.
Crystal Falls.

Two days after Ellie finished the truck stop story at midnight (still pretending she hadn't), Tommy decided to make biscuits.

Marcus knew this was coming. There were signs. Tommy had been consulting the agricultural guide at breakfast, which he only did when he was about to attempt something ambitious with flour. He'd also reorganized the kitchen pantry that afternoon, which was either enthusiasm or displacement activity, and in Tommy Miller those two things were often the same.

"They're gonna be fine," Tommy said, not looking up from the mixing bowl.

"I didn't say anything," Marcus said.

"You were thinking it."

"I was thinking about the generator schedule."

Tommy looked at him over his shoulder. "The face you make about the generator schedule is different."

This was probably true.

Sarah came in from the garden with a handful of rosemary and set it on the counter. She looked at the mixing bowl, then at Tommy, then at Marcus, and said nothing, which was its own kind of verdict.

"I've been making biscuits since before either of you were born," Tommy said.

"You make them like you're building something," Sarah said. "Which is not how biscuits work."

"The structural principles are the same."

"They're really not."

She washed her hands and started on the rosemary. Tommy turned back to the bowl. The kitchen had the warm crowded feel it got in early evening when everyone was moving through it at different purposes — Sarah at the counter, Tommy at the stove, Marcus with his coffee at the table already, Noah at the far end with his sketchbook and the particular focused silence that meant he was actually tracking every conversation in the room while pretending to draw.

Ellie came in through the back door at that point with mud on her boots and a copy of the Stephen King paperback in her hand, the one Joel had told her to finish.

"Did you finish it?" Marcus asked.

"It's terrible," she said, pulling off her boots.

"You were up until midnight."

"I needed to see how it ended."

"Did it improve?"

She set the book on the counter next to the rosemary and looked at Tommy's mixing bowl. "No. But at least it was short." She climbed into her usual chair — the one sideways against the wall, clear sightline to both doors, which she still took every night without appearing to notice she was doing it. "What are we doing?"

"Biscuits," Tommy said.

She looked at the bowl. She looked at Tommy. She looked at Marcus with an expression that said she had formed a complete assessment of the situation and was choosing not to share it yet.

Joel came in from the front room a few minutes later, coffee cup already in hand like it had grown there, and dropped into the corner chair without comment. Captain materialized from somewhere near the hall stairs and positioned himself under the table at Joel's feet.

"Where's he been?" Noah asked without looking up.

"Under the stairs," Joel said. "Same as always."

"He goes under the stairs when Tommy cooks," Ellie said.

"Captain has preferences," Tommy said. "That doesn't mean—"

"He hid when you made the barley soup."

"That soup was excellent."

"He was under the stairs for an hour and a half."

"The dog," Tommy said firmly, "is not a food critic."

Joel looked into his coffee and said nothing. Noah wrote something in his sketchbook.

___

The biscuits came out dark on the bottom.

Not burned, Tommy said. Caramelized.

Sarah looked at them without speaking.

Tommy plated them alongside venison stew from the pot he'd had going since midday, which was actually good and everyone knew it, which meant the biscuits' reception was going to be weighted against an unfair comparison. Marcus filled his bowl and sat down. Sarah sat. Noah pushed his sketchbook to the corner with his elbow, which was the closest he came to clearing the table. Joel refilled his coffee. Ellie angled her chair back to the table and served herself stew in the efficient, spare way she still served herself food — enough, no more, like abundance required some kind of explanation.

Captain circulated once beneath the table, registered that nobody was dropping anything interesting, and went back to his spot at Joel's feet.

Joel picked up a biscuit. He tapped it on the edge of his bowl. It made a hollow sound. He looked at it for a moment, then put it down and took a bite of stew instead.

"These are good," Tommy said about the stew.

"The stew's good," Sarah agreed.

"The biscuits are also good."

"Tommy."

"They're caramelized."

Nobody argued. The stew was genuinely good and conversation moved on — Helen's weekly security report, which Sarah had been carrying in her folder for two days and couldn't stop thinking about, a supply run Tommy wanted to schedule to Basalt before the end of the month. Joel offered a thought about the mountain route's drainage after last week's rain. Marcus confirmed the weather window. Normal. Comfortable. The specific low-frequency hum of a family eating dinner.

Then Noah reached for a biscuit.

He picked it up. He turned it over. He set it down and took a different one, tapping the bottom of it with one finger. He held the second one up and looked at the underside.

"What're you doing?" Ellie asked.

"Testing," Noah said.

"For what?"

"Load capacity."

He said it the way eleven-year-olds say things that are obvious to them and require no further elaboration. The table went quiet for exactly half a second.

Then Ellie looked at the biscuit in his hand. She looked at Tommy. She looked at Joel.

"Load-bearing biscuits," she said.

"That's not a term," Tommy said.

"It's absolutely a term. You could use those in the barricade. Probably hold better than the east section corner where the rebar's been loose since spring." She picked one up and held it out at arm's length, considering it. "You could build a dam."

"I'm right here," Tommy said.

"I know. You made them. I'm complimenting your construction. You should tell Carson. Save on materials."

Joel laughed.

Not the short quiet exhale he gave when something was mildly funny and he didn't want to encourage it. An actual laugh, chest-deep, the kind that started before he could stop it and got two full beats out before he got his hand over his mouth. He shook his head once with the look of a man who'd lost an argument with his own face.

Sarah rolled her eyes.

Not at Ellie. At Joel, which was a distinct direction of eye-roll that meant she'd known this would happen and she'd been waiting for it and she wasn't entirely displeased but she wasn't going to say so either.

"It's funny," Joel said, getting himself under control. "She's right."

"These are not structural," Tommy said. He picked one up and looked at it for a long moment. Then he put it back down. Then he picked it up again and looked at the bottom.

He did not eat it.

"I hate all of you," he said, with the tone of a man who did not hate them at all.

Noah drew something in his sketchbook with the expression of someone capturing history as it happened.

___

He'd been working on the portrait all through dinner.

Marcus had seen him start it sometime around the biscuit verdict, when the conversation shifted and everyone settled into the longer rhythm of a meal going well. Noah worked the way he always worked — without announcing it, without asking anyone to hold still, tracking the room with the patient peripheral attention he'd had since he was small and needed to understand every space he entered before he could relax in it.

An old habit, Marcus thought. One they'd both come in with.

After the table was cleared and Tommy was doing dishes with the quiet dignity of a man processing a culinary reversal, Noah pushed the sketchbook across the table to Marcus without comment.

Marcus looked at it.

Six figures around a long rectangle that was clearly meant to be this table. Sarah at one side with paper in front of her, pen in hand, the focused look she got when she was doing numbers. Tommy with his arms crossed, which was accurate and appeared to be unconscious. Joel in the corner chair with his cup, rendered with the compressed stillness of a man conserving energy. Marcus himself appeared to be floating approximately an inch above his seat.

"The lamp," Noah said.

"The lamp made me float?"

"The angle. The light was behind you." He pointed at the sketch. "You're not floating. I just got the shadow wrong."

"I trust the process."

"I can fix it later."

Sarah reached over and took the sketchbook. She looked at her own figure for a moment. "I look like a math teacher."

"You were doing accounting."

"Federation accounting."

"That's math."

She studied it a beat longer, then passed it to Tommy, who had come back from the sink to see what was happening. Tommy looked at himself. He looked at his sketched arms, crossed over his sketched chest.

He uncrossed his own arms.

Noah looked at him.

Tommy crossed them again. "It's accurate," he said, and passed the book to Joel.

Joel took it. He looked at it for a while without speaking, the way he went still sometimes when something got through before he had time to put the usual frame around it. He tilted the book slightly, then tilted it back. He set his thumb against his own figure, the small shape with the coffee cup, and didn't say anything.

Then he passed it to Ellie without comment, and she took it.

She held it at arm's length.

The portrait had all six of them at the table. Five of the figures were rendered in Noah's careful, slightly stiff style — proportionate, posed from observation, clearly recognizable. The sixth was her.

The sixth had hair.

A lot of hair. Noah had rendered it with genuine commitment, the kind of commitment that came from a week of study and an honest eye and possibly also some editorial enthusiasm. Ellie's portrait hair extended a generous distance past what physics would ordinarily permit and framed her head in what could charitably be described as a presence.

She looked at it for a long time.

"Is this how I look?" she said.

"That's how you look from the couch," Noah said, "when the window light's behind you."

"It's enormous."

"It's your hair."

"I don't have this much hair."

"You kind of do," Sarah said from across the table, not looking up from the papers she'd reclaimed. "From the back, in afternoon light, it's significant."

Ellie looked at Marcus.

He thought carefully about his expression and kept it neutral.

"Historically impactful," he said.

"You're not helping."

"You're in a family portrait with historically impactful hair. That's a legacy."

"I don't want a legacy. I want a normal amount of hair in a drawing."

"Too late. It's been captured."

She looked at the drawing again. Something shifted in her face, quiet and quick, and she covered it by looking at Noah. "Can I keep it?"

Noah blinked. "It's not done."

"When you're done."

"I was going to put it in the front room."

"Make a color version for the front room."

He considered this with the seriousness of an artist being asked to donate original work. "The color version was going to be the real one."

"So this is a study."

"It's a sketch."

"I want the sketch."

He thought about it another moment. Then he glanced at Marcus.

Marcus said nothing and looked back at him steadily.

Noah tore the page out along the spiral binding, careful enough that it came clean. Ellie took it, folded it once along the crease of its own edge, and tucked it into her back pocket with the brief practiced motion of someone who'd already decided where it was going before she asked.

Joel watched this happen. His expression stayed quiet. He picked up his coffee cup and found it empty and set it back down.

*Ellie's POV*

The upstairs hall was quiet.

Downstairs: the kitchen radio Joel left on most evenings, low enough that you had to stand still to hear it but there enough to fill the silence. The sound of dishes being finished. Noah's feet on the stairs, then the deliberate pause before the third step from the top, then the creak anyway.

She closed her door.

She stood at the window first. The habit.

In the QZ, her room had faced the inner courtyard. She'd mapped it so many times in the dark she could have walked it blindfolded — the distance to the shed corner, the gap in the perimeter fence by the old classroom building, the guard rotation times and where they overlapped. Every room she'd lived in, she'd mapped the exits first.

Here the window faced the valley. The dam was lit, the walkway strung with string lights that had been up since the wedding photo Marcus had shown her, fourteen years ago by the look of it, and apparently nobody had bothered to take them down. Below in the yard, Captain moved through the garden in his slow end-of-day circuit, checking the perimeter the way he always did before he decided it was safe to sleep.

She turned around and looked at the room.

Three weeks. Pine bookshelf, nine books now, the ones Sarah had been leaving on her windowsill every few days without mentioning it. The Vonnegut. The two camping manuals she'd read twice. A couple of novels she'd gotten through and returned to the shelf because returning things to shelves was a habit she hadn't decided to stop yet. A potted plant on the windowsill that was somehow thriving despite her watering it irregularly and with no real feeling of investment in its survival.

The wall was empty.

The box of thumbtacks was on the windowsill. She'd found it her first day, sitting in the corner like it had always been there. She'd assumed it came with the room, left by whoever had lived here before. She'd put the box in the top drawer of the desk because she wasn't ready to use it. Three days ago she'd taken the box out of the drawer and put it back on the windowsill, because she was tired of making decisions based on not being ready.

She took the drawing out of her back pocket and unfolded it.

Six people around a table. Five rendered from careful observation, one rendered with historically impactful hair.

She'd had a bulletin board in her dorm room at the QZ. The corkboard kind that came standard in the building, mounted to the wall at roughly chest height, already pockmarked from whoever'd had the room before her. She'd had two things pinned to it for the three years she was in that particular building.

One was a hand-drawn map of the QZ interior she'd made herself, updated twice when FEDRA moved checkpoints. Practical. The map she didn't need to make but made anyway because having it on paper meant she'd stopped having to carry it in her head.

The other was a photograph she'd found tucked into the back of a book in the school library — a family at a kitchen table, black and white, caught in the middle of something. Laughing at something out of frame. Nobody famous. Just people. She'd kept it because they were happy in a way she couldn't explain, in a way that looked like they didn't know anyone was watching.

When she'd left Room 204, she hadn't taken either of them.

Not because she hadn't had time. She'd had thirty seconds while Joel and Marcus were at the door. She'd thought about it and decided no, and hadn't thought too hard about why.

The room was 204 from the outside. From the inside it had been a room, four walls and a door that locked from the outside, not the inside, and a window that faced the courtyard she'd mapped in the dark a hundred times. She'd been there almost a year. She'd never thought of it as hers the way this room was starting to feel.

She stood at the wall.

The plaster was white, painted by someone a long time ago, and there were small holes in it — three or four, the kind left by picture hooks that were never filled in, evidence of a life that had been here and interrupted. Someone had hung things here. Then they'd stopped.

She looked at the drawing in her hand.

Noah had drawn her at the table. Not at the edges of it. Not in the chair turned sideways to the door. At the table, elbow on the wood, book just out of frame to the left, turned toward the group. Like she'd always been there. Like placing her anywhere else wouldn't have made sense to him.

She picked up the box of thumbtacks.

She pressed one into the wall at eye level, slightly left of center, and hung the drawing.

Stepped back.

It looked like a thing. Not much — a pencil sketch on a white wall, figures rendered by an eleven-year-old with an honest eye and a strong point of view about hair. The kind of thing that ended up on refrigerators, eventually moved to a drawer, found years later and laughed about.

She'd never had a wall to put something on.

Not that she'd owned. Not really. The QZ walls were FEDRA's. The bulletin board was building property. The room was temporary, officially, always, even when you'd been in it for a year.

This room had a lock on the inside. Sarah had put a plant on the windowsill without asking, just left it there one morning, and Ellie had watered it without being asked either, because it was on her windowsill and it needed water and it seemed to be hers.

The shelf was hers. She'd been taking books off and putting them back for three weeks instead of leaving them, because leaving them meant she was staying, and she hadn't decided she was staying until she apparently had.

She sat on the edge of the bed and looked at the drawing.

The guitar started up downstairs. Joel's particular late-evening mode, when he wasn't really playing but his hands needed to do something. A few chords, feeling for something, not quite landing on it yet. She'd heard it every night this week at about this time, sometimes the lullaby, sometimes other things, sometimes just patterns.

She'd asked him for the lullaby two nights ago because she wanted to hear the real version. The practiced version, the one he knew without thinking. She'd told herself it was research, comparison for her own learning, which was a lie she'd believed for about fifteen minutes.

She'd wanted to hear it because nobody had ever played her something that was meant for her. And Joel had just played it. Twice. Without making it a thing. Like it was obvious that she was allowed to ask.

She looked at the drawing on the wall.

Downstairs, the guitar found the melody. The lullaby. She recognized it now after two lessons, could hear which chord he was on without seeing his hands.

Hold the blanket, close your eyes.

She'd put nine books on the shelf and hadn't returned a single one for three days. She'd watered the plant. She'd asked for the sketch before she'd decided she was going to, and now it was on the wall, thumbtack in white plaster, and it looked like it had been there for a while already.

She lay back on the bed.

Three weeks.

She knew what three weeks was. Long enough to understand the routine, short enough to leave without it costing much. She'd told herself that every morning since she got here, when she woke up and the room was still there and nobody had come through the door.

But the difference between a room you were waiting to leave and a room you were starting to live in — she could feel that difference now, and pretending she couldn't was getting harder.

The shelf was hers. The plant was hers. The lock was on the inside.

On the wall: six people at a table, one of them with historically impactful hair.

From below: a guitar, and voices, and the sound of a house not being empty.

She looked at the nine books on the shelf. She'd return them when she was done with them, she'd been telling herself. She'd put them back on the lodge library cart.

She wasn't going to put them back.

They were hers.

She closed her eyes and listened to the guitar until it finished.

*Marcus's POV*

He heard her on the stairs.

Light step, quick. The cautious one she still used, the one that checked the floor before committing. She'd been doing it since her first night. He'd noted it on day three and decided not to say anything, because some habits unwound on their own if you left them alone.

Tonight the step stopped at the top of the stairs and didn't come back down.

He was at the kitchen table with what was left of the evening — Sarah's accounting folder, Noah's pencil case, two mugs that needed to go in the sink. Tommy had gone out to check the generator, which was his way of decompressing after a dinner that hadn't gone entirely according to plan. Joel was on the front porch, the guitar audible through the screen, working through the lullaby at a pace that suggested he was playing it for himself rather than anyone in particular.

Marcus sat with his coffee and didn't hurry it.

He'd been tracking a version of this moment for seven years. Not the specifics — he'd gotten the specifics wrong in a dozen ways. The route to Boston had been harder than he'd mapped. Rivera hadn't been part of any plan. Ellie's first response to the bookshelf had been to arrange the books by spine color in descending order of darkness, which had taken him a full day to understand was her version of taking ownership of a space rather than just occupying it.

But the shape of it: Ellie Williams in a house in western Colorado with a lock on her door, a guitar teacher downstairs, and a family portrait on her wall.

That part he'd gotten right.

He thought about the Austin highway underpass and Sarah bleeding on the asphalt and the twenty years that had followed. He hadn't had a plan that night. He'd had a direction and a desperate need to be faster than what was already happening. Everything after was construction.

The accounting folder. The security rotations. The barricade that had held. The cleared corridors and the morning walks and the kitchen table at six a.m. where you could hear who was awake by their footstep on the third stair.

He finished his coffee.

From upstairs, nothing moved. She'd gone to her room and stayed there, which was different from the first week when he'd heard her check every window twice before she slept.

On the front porch: the guitar still moving through the melody, the same line repeated, unhurried.

Marcus stacked the accounting folder back into order and pushed Noah's pencil case to the side of the table. He looked at the blank wall in the front room, the one Sarah had said needed something, where Noah wanted to hang the color version of the portrait when it was done.

It would fit there. Good light in the morning.

He turned off the kitchen lamp and left the porch light on for Tommy.

Done.
