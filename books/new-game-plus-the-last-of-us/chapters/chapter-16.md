# Chapter 16: What You Carry

## — Marcus —

October 9th. Day 13 on the road.

The sky out here was a different blue. Not the hazy, washed-out blue of Central Texas, where the air had weight and the horizon smeared into white at the edges. This was the hard, clean blue of the High Plains. Empty. Sharp. Stretching so far overhead that Marcus had to stop looking at it sometimes because the scale of it made his stomach drop.

He was sitting outside a collapsed gas station that they'd camped in the night before. The pumps were rusted solid, the canopy tilted at fifteen degrees like a hat put on wrong. Inside, Joel and Tommy were dividing the last of the MREs they'd taken from the Humvee. Sarah was still asleep in the backseat of a stripped Buick that someone had left in the parking lot with the keys on the dashboard and the doors open, like they'd planned to come right back.

Five hundred and ninety miles to the nearest dam site. At their pace, three weeks. Maybe four if the terrain got rougher through New Mexico.

They'd skirted Abilene two days ago. Way around, like he'd told Tommy they would. Twenty miles of detour through rangeland and barbed-wire fence lines and dried creek beds that hadn't seen water since spring. The map had shown the city as a dense red mass. Hundreds of dots packed into the grid of downtown streets and strip malls and subdivisions. A hundred and twenty thousand people had lived there once. Most of them were still there. Just not in a way that counted.

Marcus pulled up his status.

**[Points: 721]**
**[Distance traveled: 214 miles]**
**[Distance remaining: ~590 miles]**

Seven hundred and twenty-one points. Fourteen short of the settlement starter kit. Two Runners would cover the gap. One Clicker would cover it twice over. But there hadn't been any infected on this stretch. The map was clean in every direction. Just four people and the road and a landscape that had been empty before the outbreak and was emptier now.

He closed the status screen. Watched the horizon instead. There was nothing to see. That was kind of the point.

Joel came out of the gas station holding two MRE packets and a water bottle.

"Here."

"Thanks."

Joel sat on the concrete island where the pumps used to work. He opened his MRE. Menu #8. Beef patty, wheat snack bread, cheese spread. He ate without comment. He ate the way he did everything. Efficiently, without waste, like even chewing was a task to be completed rather than an experience to be had.

Marcus opened his. Menu #14. Creamy spinach fettuccini. It tasted like warm cardboard with a suggestion of spinach. He ate it anyway.

"Tommy thinks we should push hard today," Joel said. "Eighteen, twenty miles. Make up for the detour."

"He's right."

"We'll be in the Panhandle by the weekend."

"New Mexico after that."

Joel nodded. Finished the beef patty. Folded the MRE wrapper into a precise square and put it in his pack. Then he looked at Marcus. Not the sideways, calculating look he'd been wearing the first two weeks. Something more direct.

"You've been quiet," Joel said.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're always thinkin'. That's different from quiet. Last couple days you've been actually quiet."

Marcus didn't respond right away. Joel wasn't wrong. Since the rest stop, since Gerald, since the tire iron and the shallow grave and Dorothy's dry-eyed goodbye, he'd been running a loop in his head. The same loop, over and over.

"I'm fine," Marcus said.

Joel didn't push. That was one of the things about Joel that matched the games perfectly. The man had an almost surgical ability to register that something was wrong and then choose not to address it. Not because he didn't care. Because he respected the right to carry your own weight.

"Sarah's been askin' about you," Joel said. Almost as an afterthought.

"What kind of asking?"

"The kind where she writes in that journal and then looks at you like she's tryin' to decide somethin'."

"Decide what?"

"Hell if I know." Joel stood up. Packed the water bottle. "I stopped tryin' to figure out what Sarah's thinkin' about the time she turned ten." He paused. "She's worried about you."

He went back inside without waiting for a response.

...

They walked.

The road was a two-lane state highway cutting northwest through the rolling plains. Straight enough to see a mile ahead and a mile behind. No curves. No surprises. The wind blew from the west, steady and dry, carrying dust and the smell of dead grass and something faintly mineral that Marcus couldn't place.

Tommy on point. Joel behind Sarah. Marcus at the back. The formation had settled into this pattern days ago and nobody questioned it anymore. Tommy had the best eyes and the most terrain experience. Joel stayed near Sarah by instinct. Marcus was at the rear because the map was at the rear, watching for contacts from any direction.

Sarah dropped back to walk with Marcus around noon.

She'd been up front with Joel all morning, talking about something Marcus couldn't hear. Now she fell into step beside him. She had the journal tucked under her arm and a pen clipped to the collar of her shirt. Her walking was smooth and painless. Had been for a week. The hip was healed. The limp was gone. The scar would always be there, but she moved like someone who'd forgotten about it.

"Hey," she said.

"Hey."

"You look like you're doing math in your head."

"I'm not."

"You have the same face you make when you're looking at that invisible thing. The menu or whatever it is."

Marcus glanced at her. She met his eyes without hesitation. That was something about Sarah. She didn't look away from things. Most people, when they wanted to ask you something uncomfortable, would look at their shoes or the horizon or anywhere that wasn't your face. Sarah looked straight at you. Her way of saying: I'm here. Deal with it.

"I'm not checking the system," Marcus said. "Just thinking."

"About Gerald."

It wasn't a question.

"Among other things."

"You've been weird since the rest stop. Weirder than usual."

"Thanks."

"I don't mean it badly. I mean you're carrying something around. You walk like there's extra weight on you that wasn't there before." She paused. "Tommy noticed too. He asked Dad about it."

"What'd Joel say?"

"He said you were processing. Said everyone processes at their own speed and to leave you alone."

"Joel's right."

"Joel's not always right." Sarah adjusted the journal under her arm. "He thinks leaving people alone is the same as helping them. It's not."

Marcus didn't have a response to that. So he said nothing. And Sarah, to her credit, didn't push. She walked beside him and the wind blew dust across the road and they covered two more miles in comfortable silence.

---

They made camp that night in a cluster of buildings that used to be a feed store and a small Baptist church. The church still had its steeple, white paint peeling, a wooden cross on top that leaned east at a thirty-degree angle. Inside, the pews were intact but dusty. Hymnals scattered on the floor. A piano in the corner with three broken keys and a layer of grit on every surface.

Tommy chose the church. Better sight lines. Thick walls. Only two doors, both visible from the pulpit where he set up his sleeping bag.

They ate around a fire Tommy built in a metal trash can in the parking lot. MREs again. Marcus supplemented with crackers and canned peaches from the Mall. Thirteen points. Down to 708. He should stop buying food when they had military rations, but the peaches were real food and the MREs were technically food and there was a difference.

Nobody talked much. Tommy cleaned the Remington. Joel sharpened the hunting knife with a whetstone from the gas station. Sarah ate and wrote and ate some more.

The sky got dark fast. No ambient light from cities. No glow on the horizon. Just the fire, then blackness, then stars. More stars than Marcus had ever seen in his life before the transmigration. The sky was so full of them it looked painted. A smear of white across the middle that he was pretty sure was the Milky Way, something he'd only seen in photographs.

Joel took first watch. He sat on the church steps with the SIG on his knee and stared out into the dark. Tommy went inside. Sarah stayed by the fire.

Marcus stayed too.

He didn't plan to talk. He'd spent four days not talking about the rest stop, and the plan was to keep not talking about it until the feeling faded on its own. You had feelings. You carried them. They faded. That was the deal.

But the fire was low and the stars were absurdly bright and Sarah was sitting three feet away, not writing, just watching the embers. And his brain decided, without his permission, that silence wasn't working anymore.

"I couldn't save him," he said.

Sarah didn't look up. Didn't move. Just said, "Gerald."

"Yeah."

"You tried."

"Trying doesn't matter when someone dies."

"It does to the person who tried."

Marcus picked up a stick and poked at the fire. Watched the embers rearrange themselves into new patterns that meant nothing. "I have this system. It gives me points. I kill infected, I get points. I spend points, I get supplies. Food, weapons, medical equipment, building materials. Anything I need, as long as I can afford it."

Sarah turned her head. Looking at him now. Waiting.

"I've been treating it like a cheat code. Like I've got the answers in the back of the textbook. Kill things, get points, buy solutions. Build a settlement. Keep us safe. Win."

"You've said stuff like that before. The game talk."

"I know. It's how I think about it. How I make it make sense." He dropped the stick. "But Gerald didn't die from an infected. He died from a guy with a tire iron. And I was standing right there, with seven hundred points and a shop full of medical supplies, and none of it mattered. Because a skull fracture isn't something you fix with gauze and an antiseptic wipe. It needs a hospital and a surgeon and a CT scanner. And those don't exist anymore."

"So you feel guilty."

"No." Marcus shook his head. "Not guilty. Scared."

The word sat in the air between them. He'd said it out loud and it was too late to take it back.

"Scared of what?" Sarah asked. Quiet. No judgment in it.

"Of the responsibility. Of being the person with the resources and the knowledge and the plan, and still not being enough. I can farm points and buy gear and clear nests and build walls, and somebody's still going to die because a guy with a tire iron wanted their truck. I can't buy my way out of that. I can't system my way around it."

Sarah pulled her knees up. Wrapped her arms around them. She looked younger than twelve in the firelight and older than twelve at the same time.

"You're scared of not being enough," she said. "For what, exactly?"

"For whatever's coming." Marcus gestured at the road, the dark, all of it. "I have a plan. Colorado, the dam, the settlement. I've got it mapped out. Where we'll live, what we'll build, how we'll defend it. But plans don't account for people. People are messy. People show up at your gate with a tire iron or a pregnant daughter or both. And I can't point-farm my way to a solution for that."

"Nobody's asking you to solve people, Marcus."

"Nobody has to ask. I'm the one with the invisible supply system and the map that shows where the monsters are. If I don't figure it out, who does?"

Sarah was quiet for a moment. The fire popped. A log collapsed, sending a scatter of sparks up into the dark.

"Dad does," she said. "Tommy does. I do. You're not the only person here."

"I know that."

"Do you? Because you talk about plans and points and building things like it's all on you. Like if something goes wrong, it's your fault. Gerald died because three men with guns decided to rob people on a highway. That's not your fault. That's not anybody's fault except theirs."

"I know."

"Then stop carrying it like it's yours."

Marcus looked at her. She looked back. Joel's eyes. A face that still had baby fat at the jawline but didn't act like it.

"I don't know how to do that," he said. The honesty surprised him.

Sarah's expression shifted. Not softer, exactly. More like something clicked behind her eyes. A decision she'd been making for a while now, finally landing.

"You're weird," she said.

"I'm aware."

"No. I mean it. You're weird. You know things you shouldn't know. You make supplies appear out of thin air. You found us on the worst night of our lives because you somehow knew exactly where to be and when. You've got a map in your head that nobody else can see and a plan for the next decade that you won't tell anyone about. You're the weirdest person I've ever met, and I once had a classmate who ate paste through sixth grade."

"Sarah..."

"But you're the only person I trust."

Marcus stopped talking. Stopped poking the fire. Stopped doing anything except sitting there and hearing those words, which were bigger than they sounded and meant something he wasn't fully ready to hold.

"I trust Dad," Sarah said. "I love Dad. But Dad runs from things. He doesn't mean to. He'd say he's protecting us. But when it gets hard, when the thing in front of him is too big or too scary or too complicated, his answer is to keep moving. Put distance between us and the problem. That's how he works."

Marcus didn't say anything. Because she was right. It was exactly how Joel worked. It was how Joel survived twenty years without Sarah in the original timeline. Not by confronting the grief. By outrunning it.

"You don't run," Sarah said. "You sat with Dorothy while her husband died. You buried a stranger behind a gas station, and then you sat on a curb and talked to me about what it meant. You're scared and you don't know if you're enough and you told me that just now because you'd rather say it out loud than pretend it isn't there."

She paused. The fire was almost out. Neither of them moved to add wood.

"That's why I trust you. Not because of the supplies or the map or whatever your system is. Because you stay."

Something in Marcus's chest loosened. Not dramatically. Not a cinematic moment. Just a knot he'd been carrying since the rest stop, one of many, coming undone a little.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay?"

"Okay. I'll try to stop carrying all of it by myself."

"You won't," Sarah said. "But trying counts."

She picked up her journal. Stood. Brushed dirt off her jeans.

"I'm going to bed. Don't stay up all night staring at the invisible menu."

"I won't."

"You will. But at least you're thinking about not doing it."

She walked toward the church. At the door, she paused and looked back over her shoulder.

"Goodnight, Marcus."

"Goodnight, Sarah."

She went inside. The door closed with a creak of old hinges.

Marcus sat by the fire. It was mostly embers now. Orange and grey and a thin line of smoke rising straight up because the wind had finally died.

## — Joel —

Joel had heard everything.

He was sitting on the church steps, twenty feet away, in the shadow where the firelight didn't reach. He'd been watching the dark and the flat horizon and the nothing that stretched in every direction. He hadn't intended to listen. But sounds carried in flat country, especially at night, and they hadn't been whispering.

He'd heard Marcus talk about fear. About not being enough. About Gerald dying three feet away from a man with seven hundred points worth of whatever-it-was, and none of it mattering.

He'd heard Sarah say she trusted Marcus. Not just trusted. The only person.

He'd heard what she said about her own father. That Joel ran.

Joel turned the SIG over in his hands. The metal was cold where the night air touched it, warm where his palm had been. He rubbed his thumb along the slide and felt the slight roughness where the finish had worn from use.

She wasn't wrong.

That was the thing that sat in his chest. She wasn't wrong about any of it. He ran. He'd always run. When things got too heavy, when the grief or the fear or the responsibility piled up to where it threatened to break something he couldn't afford to break, he put one foot in front of the other and he moved forward and he called it survival. And it was survival. It had kept him alive for three weeks and it would keep him alive for however long he had left.

But his daughter, his twelve-year-old daughter who'd been shot in the hip on a highway and left on the ground while he ran north because Tommy was pulling his arm and the bombs were falling, his daughter trusted a stranger more than she trusted him.

Because the stranger stayed.

Joel wanted to be angry. Wanted that hot surge of something he could use, something that would let him stand up and walk over to Marcus and set things straight. That's my daughter. I didn't leave her. I thought she was dead.

But the anger wouldn't come. Because under the pride and the protectiveness and the need to control the situation, there was something quieter. Something that sounded, if he was being honest with himself, a lot like what Marcus had said.

Not enough.

He'd thought those words more times in three weeks than he'd thought them in thirty-six years of living. Not enough to see the soldier's gun come up. Not enough to check Sarah's pulse before Tommy dragged him away. Not enough to go back.

He had his reasons. Good ones. She'd been shot at close range and gone limp and there was so much blood on the pavement and Tommy was saying "Joel, she's gone, we gotta go" and the bombs were starting and the infected were coming from every direction and staying meant dying next to her body and he'd made a choice that wasn't a choice because the only alternative was death.

But he'd left. And a stranger had stayed.

Joel sat on the church steps and thought about the man who held gauze against Gerald's broken head and counted the breaths and didn't look away. The man who buried a stranger and then sat on a concrete curb with Joel's daughter and talked about what it meant. The man who was scared and said so. Out loud. To a twelve-year-old. Because pretending he wasn't scared felt worse than admitting it.

Joel didn't admit things. Joel didn't sit with things. Joel moved. That was how he was built. Not because he didn't feel it. He felt everything. He felt so much that the only way to survive was to keep the feelings behind a wall and the wall behind motion and the motion pointed at whatever was next, because if he ever stopped moving, all of it would catch up at once and it would crush him.

Sarah had seen through it in three weeks. She'd watched her father kill a man at a gas station and set ambushes at checkpoints and sharpen knives with steady hands, and she'd looked at all of that and seen what was underneath. A man who kept moving because stopping meant feeling, and feeling meant breaking.

Tommy came out at two AM to relieve him. Joel went inside without a word.

He walked past Sarah's sleeping bag. She was asleep on a pew, facing the wall, the journal tucked under her arm like a stuffed animal. Joel stood over her for a long moment. Counted her breaths. Steady, even, slow. The breaths of a kid who felt safe enough to sleep deeply, which was its own kind of miracle three weeks into the end of the world.

"I'm not goin' anywhere, baby girl," he whispered.

Sarah didn't stir.

Joel lay down on the pew across from her. He put the SIG on the floor within reach and stared at the ceiling where moonlight came through a crack in the roof and made a thin silver line on the old wood.

He didn't sleep. But he stayed.

## — Marcus —

Morning came grey and cold.

October on the High Plains. The temperature had dropped overnight, enough that Marcus's breath made a faint cloud when he exhaled. First time since the outbreak. The seasons were turning. By the time they reached Colorado, they'd be walking into winter. He needed to think about cold weather gear. Jackets, boots, thermal layers. The Mall had everything, but nothing was cheap.

**[Points: 708]**

Twenty-seven short of the settlement starter kit. Two Runners would cover the gap. One Clicker would cover it three times over. But the map had been clean for days.

They packed. Left the church. Started walking.

The wind was from the north today. Colder than yesterday. Marcus could smell something on it. Not decay. Not dust. Something organic. Wet earth and old leaves. Autumn had been happening somewhere ahead of them and the wind was carrying the news.

Sarah walked with Joel this morning. Close to him. She said something Marcus couldn't hear and Joel said something back, and they walked together for a while. Just a father and daughter on a road in the middle of nowhere, and the end of the world was background noise.

Marcus watched them from the back of the formation. The way Joel adjusted his stride to match Sarah's shorter one. The way Sarah's hand brushed Joel's arm when she gestured at something in the distance. Small, unconscious movements between people who belonged to each other.

She trusted Marcus. That was what she'd said. But watching her walk with Joel, watching the easy proximity of it, the gravitational pull of a kid who needed her dad, Marcus understood something that Sarah maybe didn't yet. Trust and love weren't the same thing. She trusted Marcus because he stayed. She loved Joel because he was her father. And love was the one you didn't choose.

Tommy appeared beside him.

"You good?" Tommy asked.

"Getting there."

"That's honest." Tommy looked at the road ahead. Joel and Sarah. "She's a good kid."

"She is."

"She's got Joel's stubbornness and her mother's brains. Dangerous combination." Tommy paused. "She told you something last night."

"You heard?"

"Sound carries out here."

Marcus didn't respond. Tommy didn't push.

They walked another mile. A water tower appeared on the horizon. The angular shape of a grain elevator beside it. A small town.

"She's right, you know," Tommy said. "About you staying."

Marcus looked at him.

"Joel and I are the same kind of animal. Different wars, different styles, same instinct. When it goes bad, we fight or we move. We don't sit still. Sitting still is how you die." Tommy adjusted the Remington strap on his shoulder. "After Sarah, after the highway, Joel couldn't stop. He ran north because if he stopped running, he'd have to think about what he left behind. Thinking about it would've broken him."

"I know."

"You're different. You sit with things. I saw it with Gerald. You didn't get up and walk away. You stayed and held the gauze and counted the breaths and you were still sitting there when it was over." Tommy looked at him. "That's not a skill most people have. Most people look away."

"It's not a skill. I just didn't know what else to do."

"That's the same thing, most of the time." Tommy was quiet for a few steps. The town was getting closer. A main street, storefronts, a church steeple smaller than the one they'd slept in. "When we get to Colorado, when we start building, the settlement isn't going to need another fighter. Joel and I can handle the threats. What it's going to need is someone who stays. Who sits with the hard stuff instead of solving it with a hammer or a rifle."

"That's the part I'm worst at."

"That's the part everyone's worst at." Tommy smiled. The tired, real smile. "But you've got a head start. You've got a twelve-year-old who trusts you more than she trusts anyone else on earth. Start there."

Marcus let that sit. The town was close now. Half a mile. He opened the map.

Six red dots. All inside the main cluster. Level 1 Runners.

Easy points. Enough to cross the threshold.

"There's a nest in the town ahead," Marcus said. "Six infected. I need the points."

Tommy unslung the Remington. "Joel."

Joel stopped. Turned. He looked at Marcus and the look was not the calculating one, not the suspicious one. It was something else. Something that might have been, in Joel's limited vocabulary for these things, acknowledgment. Of what, exactly, Marcus wasn't sure.

Joel didn't say anything. He checked the SIG, chambered a round, and started toward the town.

Marcus followed. The wind pushed at his back and the grain elevator got taller against the grey sky and the road kept going the way roads do, straight and flat and indifferent to whoever walked it.

Twenty-seven points. Six Runners. Then the settlement kit. Then Colorado.

Then the hard part.
