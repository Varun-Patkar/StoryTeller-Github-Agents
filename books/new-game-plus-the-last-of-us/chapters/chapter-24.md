# Chapter 24: The Table

## — Helen —

Day fifty-two. Helen Park was awake before the sun.

Old habit. Eight years at Fort Carson had burned the 0500 wakeup into her bones so deep that the end of civilization hadn't fixed it. She'd been out of the Reserve for three years before the outbreak, working supply chain logistics for a distribution center in Colorado Springs, and she still couldn't sleep past five-fifteen.

The compound was quiet. She could hear the dam, a low steady hum that vibrated in the floor of the maintenance building where the new arrivals slept. Frank was snoring two rooms over. Mateo was silent, which either meant the boy slept like the dead or he'd been awake all night staring at the ceiling. Helen had shared enough barracks with enough people to know the difference, but she didn't check. The kid deserved whatever privacy a fifteen-year-old could carve out of a shared building.

She dressed, laced her boots, and went outside with her pocket notebook.

The notebook was a Field Notes brand, three and a half by five and a half inches, graph paper, olive cover. She'd had it in her jacket pocket when the world ended. It had survived fifty-two days of walking, sleeping in cars, eating whatever wasn't rotted, and three separate encounters with infected. The pencil she used was a golf pencil she'd taken from a mini-golf course outside Leadville. Short, no eraser, slightly chewed at the end by someone who wasn't Helen.

She walked the perimeter.

The compound was better than she'd expected. Not military-grade, not even close, but better than the ad-hoc camps and failed communes she'd seen since September. Five buildings arranged in an L along the river. One access road. Natural bottleneck at the valley entrance. A dam providing real power, not generator power, which meant it wouldn't run out of fuel in a week.

Somebody here knew what they were doing. Multiple somebodies, actually. The wiring was recent and competent. The tree stand at the valley entrance was rough carpentry but well-positioned, covering the road for at least a quarter mile. The solar panels on the garage were mounted correctly, south-facing, proper angle for this latitude.

But the gaps were obvious.

Helen wrote in the notebook:

_North ridge: brush clearance started but incomplete. 50-60 yards of cover remaining between tree line and maintenance bldg. Any competent force uses this approach._

_West draw: rocks piled across game trail. Not a barrier. A speed bump. Need actual obstacle — felled trees, wire, something._

_South approach: boulder OP position adequate for single observer. No fallback position if OP is compromised. Tree stand covers road but not the river bank. Blind spot south-southeast._

_No night watch rotation documented. Donna mentioned she does "most nights." One person, eight hours, no relief, no log. Unsustainable._

_No armory. Weapons stored in powerhouse. Locked? Unclear. No inventory list visible._

_Water source is river, filtered. Single point of failure — contamination upstream affects entire supply._

She filled three pages before the sun cleared the ridge.

It wasn't criticism. It was assessment. Helen had written dozens of these at Fort Carson, walking supply depots and motor pools with a clipboard, cataloging what worked and what would get people killed if nobody fixed it. The compound wasn't bad. It was just run by people who were learning as they went, which was fine for the first fifty days but wouldn't hold at thirty people or fifty or a hundred.

And people would keep coming. The lights made sure of that.

She was writing a note about the lack of a designated medical space when she heard footsteps behind her. Donna, coming off the night watch, rifle over her shoulder, looking tired.

"You're up early," Donna said.

"Couldn't sleep. How was the night?"

"Quiet. Deer came through the draw around two. Scared me half to death." Donna looked at the notebook. "Taking notes?"

"Habit."

"Good notes or bad notes?"

Helen considered the question. "Useful notes."

Donna nodded slowly. "Meeting's at nine. Council's first one. Marcus said anyone can bring agenda items."

"Anyone?"

"That's what he said."

Helen looked at her notebook. Three pages of useful notes.

"I might have a few," she said.

## — Marcus —

The council met in the residence kitchen.

Marcus had imagined something more formal. A table, chairs, maybe a whiteboard. What they got was five people standing around a kitchen counter with coffee that Frank had made in a percolator he'd found in the powerhouse storage closet. The coffee was terrible. Frank had used twice the grounds needed and half the water, producing something closer to motor oil than a morning beverage.

Nobody complained. Coffee was coffee.

"First meeting," Marcus said. "I'll keep this simple. Each of us reports on our domain. Issues, needs, plans. Then open floor. Anyone in the community can bring something to us. Helen asked to present."

Tommy raised his eyebrows. "She's been here one day."

"And she's got eight years of military logistics experience. I'll hear what she has to say."

Joel was leaning against the wall by the window, arms crossed, coffee untouched. He hadn't said anything since walking in. His expression was the neutral one, the one that could mean anything from "I'm listening" to "I'm deciding who to hit."

Sarah had her journal open on the counter, pencil ready. She'd appointed herself secretary without anyone asking, which was efficient and also meant she controlled what got written down.

"Tommy, you first," Marcus said.

Tommy set down his cup. "Power's stable. Dam output steady at 168 kilowatts. Phase three winding's still degraded but holding. Solar backup's online, gives us about a kilowatt in decent sun. Ray wants to add two more panels. I told him we'd discuss it." He paused. "Main concern is the outbuilding wiring. I've got three buildings partially wired. Carson offered to help with the conduit runs, which would speed things up. I need Marcus to buy more wire."

Marcus nodded. "How much?"

"Two hundred feet of ten-gauge. Maybe fifty feet of six-gauge for the main feed to the garage."

Marcus checked the Mall in his head. Rough estimate, 30 points. Doable.

"Done. Sarah?"

Sarah looked at her notes. "Food. We're still Mall-dependent for meals. Three raised beds are prepped but nothing's producing yet. Winter wheat goes in this week if the ground temperature holds. Lena's our best resource for agriculture. I want to formalize her role as lead grower."

"She's not on the council," Joel said from the wall.

"She doesn't need to be on the council to run the garden. I'm saying I want her to have authority over food production decisions. What to plant, when to plant, how to allocate harvest. She reports to me, I report to the council."

Joel's jaw worked. He didn't argue.

"We're burning through food fast," Sarah continued. "Fourteen people, three meals a day. Marcus, what's the cost?"

"About six points a day for MREs. More if people want anything besides MREs."

"Six points a day that could go toward infrastructure," Sarah said. She let that sit. "We need the garden producing by spring. That's the timeline."

Marcus looked at Donna. "Watch and perimeter?"

Donna straightened. "I've been running night watches solo. That's not sustainable and I know it. I'd like to set up a rotation. Three shifts, eight hours each. I'll take midnight to eight. I need two more volunteers for the other shifts."

"Carson said he'd take a shift," Tommy offered.

"Good. One more."

"I'll talk to Helen," Marcus said.

"Already did," Donna said. "She volunteered before I could finish the sentence."

"That handles the watch. Joel, security?"

Joel pushed off the wall. He moved to the counter, set his palms flat on it, and looked at each of them in turn. It was the look he used on people he was about to screen. Direct, steady, measuring.

"Three new arrivals yesterday. I screened 'em. They're fine." He paused. "But we got people comin' from the north now. First group from that direction. Means word is spreadin' further than we thought. The lights are visible from the highway fifteen miles south. If they're visible from the pass to the north, we've got a two-direction problem."

"Problem?" Sarah said.

"Refugees come in groups. Groups get bigger. Bigger groups bring bigger problems. I want a checkpoint on the access road. Permanent. Not a boulder and a tree stand. An actual gate with an actual person who can stop traffic and ask questions before anyone gets within rifle range of the compound."

"That's construction," Tommy said. "That's my domain."

"It's security," Joel said. "I decide what we need. You figure out how to build it."

Tommy opened his mouth. Marcus cut in.

"He's right. Security requirements come from Joel. Implementation comes from Tommy. That's how the domains work."

Tommy looked annoyed but nodded.

"Anything else?" Marcus asked.

"Yeah." Joel looked at the door. "Helen Park asked to speak."

____

Helen came in with her notebook.

She'd been waiting on the porch with Frank and Mateo. Frank was measuring the kitchen doorframe with a cloth tape measure and muttering about lumber dimensions. Mateo was sitting on the steps, watching a hawk circle the ridge. Neither of them looked up when Helen went inside.

She stood at the end of the counter. Five faces, four of which she'd known for less than twenty-four hours. The fifth, Donna, she'd known for about twelve.

"I want to say first that I appreciate being allowed to speak," Helen said. "I know I just got here. I know there's a probation period and I respect that. But I spent eight years assessing installations for the Army, and old habits die hard."

She opened the notebook.

"I walked the perimeter this morning. Made some notes. The compound is solid for what it is. Good site selection. Good power infrastructure. But there are gaps that will get people hurt if they're not addressed before the population doubles."

She ran through her assessment. North ridge brush clearance. West draw obstacle. Blind spot on the south approach. Night watch sustainability. Weapons storage. Water vulnerability.

Marcus watched Joel while Helen talked. Joel's face went through three phases. First, the flat neutral. Then a tightening around the eyes that meant he was hearing things he already knew and didn't appreciate someone else pointing out. Then something else. Something Marcus hadn't seen before on Joel's face.

Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own concerns spoken back to him by someone who understood them.

Helen finished. She closed the notebook.

"I'm not trying to step on anyone," she said. "But this is what I'm good at. If there's a way I can contribute beyond watch shifts, I'd like to."

Silence.

Joel spoke first.

"The north ridge. I started the brush clearance two days ago. Carson's been helping. We'll have it done by end of week."

"Good," Helen said. "The west draw needs more than rocks."

"I know."

"And the south blind spot. If I were approaching this compound with hostile intent, I'd come up the river bank at night. Your tree stand doesn't cover it. Donna's boulder position doesn't cover it. You need a second observation point or a tripwire system or both."

Joel stared at her. Helen stared back.

Tommy looked at Marcus. Sarah's pencil had stopped moving.

"You want to build a tripwire system on my perimeter," Joel said.

"I want to close a gap in your perimeter. How we close it is your call."

Joel's jaw worked. The kitchen was quiet except for the hum of the dam and, somewhere outside, the sound of Frank measuring something.

"I'll look at it," Joel said.

"Can I come along?"

Another pause. Longer this time.

"Yeah," Joel said. "You can come along."

Marcus let out a breath he hadn't realized he was holding. Then he caught himself. Sarah caught him too, and her mouth did that thing where she wasn't smiling but her eyes were.

"There's one more thing," Helen said.

Of course there was.

"Resources. I don't know where they come from and I'm not asking you to tell me." She looked directly at Marcus. "But the community should know what we have. An inventory. What's in storage, what's allocated, what's available. I can build one. I've managed supply chains for eight years. If you let me count what's here and track what comes in and goes out, people stop guessing and start trusting."

That was the real ask. Not the perimeter. The transparency.

Marcus felt the weight of fourteen people who ate his food, used his tools, and didn't know where any of it came from. He couldn't tell them about the Mall. He couldn't explain why supplies materialized behind the garage. But Helen wasn't asking for an explanation. She was asking to count what was already visible.

"That's fair," Marcus said. "Start an inventory of everything in the compound. Storage, tools, food, medicine, ammunition. Track what we have and what we use. Report to the council weekly."

Helen nodded once. Crisp, military.

"The sourcing stays between me and the council," Marcus added. "How things arrive is my concern. What's here and how we use it is everyone's concern."

"Understood."

Carson appeared in the kitchen doorway. He'd been listening. Quietly, the way Carson did everything.

"I've got a question," he said.

Marcus gestured him in.

Carson stepped inside. He was a big man, quiet, with the build of someone who'd spent years carrying heavy things for a living. He spoke carefully, like each word cost him something.

"Who decides what gets built and in what order? Tommy says the wiring's priority. Joel says the gate's priority. Helen's talking about tripwires. I'm one guy with two hands. I need to know whose list I'm working from."

It was a fair question. The fairest question anyone had asked since they formed the council.

Sarah answered before Marcus could.

"The council sets priorities together. Not one person's list. We'll do it now." She flipped to a clean page in her journal. "What are the top three construction needs?"

"Gate on the access road," Joel said immediately.

"Finish the outbuilding wiring," Tommy said.

"Brush clearance on the north ridge," Joel added.

"That's three from two people," Sarah said. "Donna?"

"Covered watch positions. If we're running three shifts, the people on watch need shelter from weather. A simple lean-to at the boulder would do."

Sarah wrote it down. "Marcus?"

"Storage building. Dedicated. Right now everything's crammed into corners of other buildings. We need a centralized supply depot."

Sarah looked at the list. Five items. One construction laborer. One shop teacher who could build things. One contractor who was also running security.

"We prioritize by safety first, then function," Sarah said. "Gate and brush clearance are safety. Wiring and watch shelters are function. Storage is organization."

"Gate first," Joel said.

"I agree," Helen said from the end of the counter.

Tommy sighed. "My wiring can wait a few days. But I need Carson for at least two afternoons on the conduit runs."

"Split the week," Sarah said. "Carson works the gate Monday through Wednesday. Conduit Thursday and Friday. Frank can help with the watch shelters. He's been looking for something to build since he got here."

"I'll handle the brush clearance," Joel said.

"I'll help," Helen said.

Joel looked at her. "Fine."

Sarah wrote it all down. The schedule. The priorities. The assignments. She tore the page out of her journal and pinned it to the kitchen wall with a tack from the corkboard.

"First weekly plan," she said. "We do this every Monday. Council meets, sets priorities, posts the schedule. Everyone knows what's expected."

Nobody objected.

Marcus looked at the page pinned to the wall. Sarah's neat handwriting, organized by day, by task, by person. A work schedule. A chore chart with authority, like he'd described it to Sarah two nights ago. Except Sarah had turned it into something real in fifteen minutes while five adults argued about tripwires.

"Meeting adjourned," Marcus said. "Unless anyone else has business."

"I'm building a table," Frank said from the doorway. He'd been there for a while, apparently. "For the meetings. You can't run a council standing around a kitchen counter. It's undignified."

"A table," Tommy said.

"A proper one. Pine. I saw some good lumber in the old rancher's shed up the hill. Dry, straight, minimal checking. Eight-foot top, seats six comfortably, eight if you squeeze. Trestle base so it won't wobble."

"We've got bigger priorities than furniture," Joel said.

"You've got bigger priorities," Frank said, pleasantly. "I don't. I'm a shop teacher. Building things for people to use is what I do. And right now the most useful thing I can build is a table where you five sit down and look each other in the eye instead of leaning on a counter like you're waiting for a bus."

Joel opened his mouth. Closed it. Looked at Marcus.

Marcus shrugged. "Build the table, Frank."

Frank smiled, patted the doorframe like it was a dog, and went back outside.

## — Sarah —

She found Marcus on the dam walkway at lunch.

He was sitting on the concrete rail, legs dangling over the water, eating an MRE that looked like it had been designed by someone who hated food. Beef stroganoff, supposedly. It was the color of wet cardboard.

"How do you think that went?" Sarah asked, sitting next to him.

"Could've been worse."

"Helen's sharp."

"Helen's exactly what we needed. Someone who knows logistics and isn't afraid to say what she sees."

"Joel didn't hate her."

"Joel respected her. Which is different from not hating her, but better."

Sarah pulled her journal out and opened it. She'd been keeping a separate page at the back. Community roster, skills, notes. She added Helen's entry.

_Helen Park. Army Reserve, 8 yrs. Supply/logistics. Fort Carson. Perimeter assessment on day 1 — identified 5 gaps Joel already knew about and 2 he didn't. Volunteered for watch rotation and brush clearance. Direct, professional, doesn't waste words. Joel let her come along to inspect the south approach. That's trust on a faster timeline than anyone except Donna._

"Carson's question was the important one," Sarah said.

"I know."

"If we grow to twenty people, we'll have five construction projects at any given time and two people who can build. That math doesn't work."

"It works if I can buy the right tools. Power saw, nail gun, things that multiply labor."

"Points?"

Marcus checked. "Four thirty-one. I spent twelve on bourbon last night, which I'm going to regret if the math gets tight."

"The bourbon bought Joel's cooperation. That's worth twelve points."

"When did you get this pragmatic?"

"Day one." Sarah closed the journal. "Marcus. Helen's inventory idea is good, but it's also going to surface the question nobody's asking."

"Where the supplies come from."

"Everyone's noticed. Ray noticed on day two. Donna noticed on day one. Helen's going to document every item in the compound and at some point the math won't add up. Fourteen people, nine days, and we have enough tools, food, wire, and medicine for a settlement three times our size. Nobody carried it in. Nobody drove it in. It just appeared."

Marcus ate a spoonful of beef stroganoff. It tasted like regret.

"I can't tell them," he said.

"I know. But you need a story. Not a lie. A story that's close enough to the truth that people can accept it and stop wondering."

"What would you tell them?"

Sarah thought about it. The river rushed below them. Somewhere in the compound, Frank was sawing lumber. The rhythmic sound of a handsaw on pine, back and forth, steady.

"I'd tell them you had a cache," she said. "Pre-positioned supplies. A survivalist stash. You planned for this, and the cache is large but finite, and that's why you manage the resources carefully. It explains why things appear without explanation. It explains why you control the supply. And it's true enough. You do plan. You do manage carefully. And the supplies are real."

"That's a lie."

"It's a frame. The supplies exist. The planning exists. The only part that's fictional is the cache. And people want to believe it. It's a better story than 'I don't know where the food comes from but it keeps showing up.'"

Marcus looked at her. Thirteen years old. Running a community's food production, chairing council meetings, and now advising him on information management.

"When did you grow up?" he asked.

"Somewhere between the gas station and the school full of Stalkers. I wasn't keeping track."

...

That afternoon, Marcus walked the south perimeter with Joel and Helen.

Joel pointed out the blind spot from ground level. Helen confirmed it from her notes. They stood at the river bank where the Crystal River curved east below the dam, creating a flat gravel bar that was invisible from both the tree stand and the boulder.

"Trip wire here," Helen said, pointing. "Low. Ankle height. Attached to cans or something that makes noise. Doesn't need to stop anyone, just needs to alert the watch."

"I can do cans," Joel said. "Plenty in the kitchen."

"And a second OP. Not permanent. Just a position someone can move to if the trip wire goes off. That rock outcrop at the bend." Helen pointed to a granite shelf ten feet above the river bank. "Good elevation, natural cover, clear line of sight to the gravel bar."

Joel looked at the outcrop. He looked at Helen.

"You've done this before," he said.

"Perimeter assessment was half my job at Carson."

Joel nodded. Not warmly. But not coldly either. The nod of a man who recognized competence because he'd spent his life surrounded by the absence of it.

"We'll set it up tomorrow," Joel said.

They walked back to the compound in silence. Not uncomfortable silence. Working silence. The kind that meant the conversation was over because everyone knew what needed doing.

## — Marcus —

Frank finished the table at sundown.

It wasn't fancy. Pine boards, hand-planed, trestle base, six feet long because the eight-foot lumber had a split that Frank refused to work around. The surface was smooth, lightly sanded, with a natural grain that caught the kitchen light. Frank had even cut shallow grooves around the edge to keep pencils from rolling off.

"Pencil gutters," he said, when Sarah asked about the grooves. "Every workbench I ever built had them. Saves you crawling under the table six times a day."

They put it in the kitchen, against the wall under the window. Frank brought in two benches he'd knocked together from the remaining lumber. Not chairs. Benches, because benches seated more people and took less material.

That evening, dinner happened at the table. Not just the council. Everyone. Fourteen people, squeezed onto benches and folding chairs and, in Mateo's case, a five-gallon bucket turned upside down. The table was too small for fourteen. People's elbows bumped. Someone knocked over a water cup. Gene's chair scraped against Carson's bench every time he shifted.

It was loud and crowded and slightly chaotic, and it was the first time the whole community had eaten at the same piece of furniture.

Helen sat between Donna and Carson. She ate quietly, listening to Frank explain to Tommy the load-bearing properties of different wood joints. Mateo and Ava were at the far end of the table, not talking to each other, but sitting close enough that their elbows almost touched. Mia was on Lena's lap, eating crackers, watching the room with the wide, careful eyes of a child who was still deciding if this place was safe.

Marcus sat at the head of the table because Frank had put him there. "You called the meeting. You sit at the head. That's how it works." Marcus wasn't sure that was how it worked, but Frank had built the table, so Frank got to decide where people sat.

Joel sat at the other end. He hadn't said anything about the seating arrangement. He ate his food, drank his coffee, and watched the room the way he always did. Cataloging. Assessing. Counting exits and threats and people.

But halfway through dinner, Marcus saw something he hadn't seen before. Joel leaned back on the bench, set his fork down, and just looked at the table. Not at the people. At the table itself. The pine surface, the pencil gutters, the trestle base. A contractor looking at a piece of carpentry and judging it.

Whatever his verdict was, he kept it to himself. But the line between his eyebrows softened, and he picked up his fork and kept eating.

After dinner, Sarah pinned the weekly schedule to the wall above the table. The list of names, tasks, and days looked official against Frank's pine. Like a notice board in a real building.

Marcus checked the settlement screen before bed.

**[Settlement: Crystal River Dam]**
**[Population: 14]**
**[Power: Dam (168 kW) + Solar (1 kW backup)]**
**[Water: River (filtered)]**
**[Food: Mall-supplied (transition to agriculture in progress)]**
**[Defenses: Perimeter watch (3-shift rotation), natural bottleneck, observation post, south perimeter tripwire (planned)]**
**[Governance: 5-seat council (established Day 51)]**
**[Morale: Stable]**

**[Points: 431]**

He'd need to make a clearing run soon. South this time, maybe. The map showed a cluster of eight contacts near a trailhead four miles downstream. Standard Runners, all orange. No dark orange. No Enhanced.

But first, the wire for Tommy. And Gene's medication was ticking down. And the gate for Joel. And seeds for Sarah. And the trip wire supplies for Helen and Joel's south perimeter project.

Four hundred and thirty-one points. Fourteen people. The math was always the same. More mouths, more needs, more pressure on a number that only went up when Marcus went out and killed things.

He lay in bed and listened to the compound settle. Frank's saw had stopped. The watch shift changed. Donna's boots on gravel, then silence, then Helen's boots going the other direction. The dam hummed.

Fourteen people. A council. A schedule pinned to a kitchen wall. A table built by a shop teacher from a high school that didn't exist anymore.

"It's working," he thought. Not the system. Not the points. The other thing. The people thing.

It was the hardest thing he'd ever tried to build, and he couldn't buy it from the Mall.
