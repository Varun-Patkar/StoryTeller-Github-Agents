# Chapter 32: The Denver Wave

## — Marcus —

April 19, 2015. Crystal Falls, Colorado.

The Humvee was starting to look like something.

Carson and Frank had spent four days fitting the rebuilt diesel engine into the chassis, arguing about torque specs the entire time. Tommy had rewired the electrical system. The armor panels sat in a row against the maintenance building wall, waiting for Frank to weld the mounting brackets.

Marcus stood in the back lot watching Frank grind a bracket smooth, sparks bouncing off his leather apron. The noise was enormous. Two years ago, Marcus had lived in a world where noise meant nothing. Now it made him nervous. Sound carried in the mountains. Every hammer strike was a dinner bell for anything listening.

"Two more days for the brackets," Frank said, lifting his welding mask. "Then fitting. Then we test it."

"How's the weight?"

"Heavy. She'll do forty on flat ground, maybe thirty uphill. Don't expect any race wins."

"I don't need fast. I need bulletproof."

"Then you'll love her."

Marcus's map pinged.

He blinked. The Settlement Management interface had a proximity alert function he'd unlocked at population fifty. Anything moving within twenty-five miles of the dam showed up as colored contacts. Runners were red. Stalkers were orange. Humans were blue.

There were a lot of blue contacts on the southern approach.

He opened the full map. A cluster of blue dots on Highway 133, eight miles south, moving north. He counted. Then counted again.

Forty-two people.

Not a patrol. Not a family. A column. Spread across half a mile of highway, bunched in groups of four and five, with stragglers trailing behind. Moving slow. Maybe two miles an hour.

Marcus closed the map and walked to the gate.

Joel was there, leaning against the observation post with a pair of binoculars Helen had scavenged from a sporting goods store in Carbondale. He was already looking south.

"You see 'em?" Joel asked.

"Map picked them up. Forty-plus."

Joel lowered the binoculars. His face was the Joel face. The one that said he was calculating threat and response and he didn't like either answer.

"Forty people don't sneak up on you," Joel said. "They're not raiders."

"No."

"Refugees?"

"Has to be. That road comes from the south. Glenwood Springs. Or further."

"Denver."

Marcus nodded. Denver was eighty miles east. The FEDRA quarantine zone there had been one of the bigger ones in the mountain west. Rumors had been filtering through for months. Overcrowding. Food shortages. A military commander who'd started shooting people for curfew violations. The kind of place that collapsed not from infected, but from the weight of its own misery.

"Forty people, Joel."

"I can count."

"We have a hundred and six."

"I can count that too."

Joel put the binoculars back to his eyes. A long silence. A redtailed hawk circled the valley entrance. The creek was loud with snowmelt.

"I ain't sayin' no," Joel said. "I'm sayin' we do this right."

"What does right look like?"

"I screen every single one of them. Not Helen's clipboard version. Mine. Anyone with weapons surrenders them at the gate. No exceptions."

"Agreed."

"And we hold them outside the perimeter until I'm done. All of them. I don't care if it takes two days."

"It won't take two days."

"You don't know that. Forty people, Marcus. Some of 'em might be fine. Some of 'em might've done things in Denver that we don't want inside our walls."

He was right. Marcus knew he was right. That was the thing about Joel. When it came to security, he was almost always right, because he assumed the worst about people and the worst was usually accurate.

"Set it up," Marcus said. "I'll get Sarah."

---

They arrived at three in the afternoon.

Marcus stood at the gate with Joel, Helen, Tommy, and four militia members. He'd told the rest of the community to stay back. Not because he expected trouble, but because forty strangers walking up a mountain road toward a lit settlement was the kind of thing that could go wrong in ways you didn't predict.

The column came around the bend in the valley road and stopped.

They looked exactly like what they were. People who had walked a long way with not enough food and no plan beyond "get somewhere else." Dirty clothes. Sunburned faces. Children riding on parents' backs. A man near the front had a bandage wrapped around his left hand, the gauze brown with old blood. A woman in the middle of the group was limping badly.

A tall man stepped forward from the front of the column. Fifties. Dark skin, grey beard, heavy coat that had been expensive once. He held his hands out, palms up. The universal gesture.

"My name is Raymond Osei," he said. His voice was hoarse. The voice of someone who'd been talking all day for many days, keeping people calm, keeping people moving. "We're from the Denver QZ. It fell nine days ago."

"Fell how?" Joel asked.

"Colonel Briggs declared martial law in February. Rations cut to one meal a day. Curfew at four in the afternoon. He started executing people for hoarding." Raymond paused. "Three weeks ago, there was a riot at the food distribution center. The soldiers opened fire. Killed seventeen civilians. After that, people started leaving. We're the ones who went west."

"How many armed?"

"Six. Hunting rifles and two pistols. We're not soldiers. We're teachers and plumbers and a woman who ran a bakery."

Joel looked at Marcus. Marcus looked at Joel.

"Weapons at the gate," Joel said. "All of them. Non-negotiable."

Raymond nodded. He turned and spoke to the column. A murmur ran through them. Six people came forward and placed weapons on the ground. Three hunting rifles, two pistols, and a shotgun that looked older than Marcus.

"We'll screen you in groups of four," Joel said. "It'll take a while. In the meantime, you sit down, drink water, don't go anywhere."

"We've been walking for nine days," Raymond said. "Sitting down sounds fine."

## — Sarah —

Sarah found out about the Denver caravan when Donna came running into the school at three-fifteen, interrupting Barbara's geography lesson.

"Forty people," Donna said. She was breathing hard. "At the gate."

Sarah closed her notebook. Opened it again. Wrote: 40 new arrivals. Denver QZ. Then she started making lists, because lists were how Sarah Miller kept the world from falling apart.

Housing. Forty people needed roofs. Crystal Falls had twelve cabins, three bunkhouses, the residence, the maintenance building, and the dining hall. All occupied. All full. She could squeeze maybe eight people into the dining hall overnight, four into the school after hours, and the rest would need tents or the open air.

She didn't have forty tents.

Food. The community consumed roughly two hundred pounds of food per day. Lena's farms produced about sixty percent of that. The rest came from Marcus's "cache," hunting, and scavenged canned goods. Adding forty people meant an extra eighty pounds per day. Lena's spring planting wasn't in the ground yet. The greenhouse couldn't scale that fast.

Water. Tommy's filtration system handled a hundred and twenty people at current capacity. Adding forty would push it past the limit. He'd need to expand, which meant more filters, more pipe, more time.

Medical. Dr. Reyes had supplies for a hundred people with normal injury rates. Forty road-weary refugees with blisters, infections, malnutrition, and untreated wounds would empty the clinic's reserves in a week.

Sarah did the math three times. Each time, the answer was the same.

They couldn't absorb forty people. Not with what they had.

They needed Marcus to spend. A lot.

She found him at the gate at four o'clock. Joel was running the screening interviews in the observation post, one group at a time, with Helen recording everything on her clipboard. The refugees sat on the gravel road in clusters, drinking water from jugs that Tommy had brought down from the dam.

"How many points do you have?" Sarah asked Marcus. No greeting. No preamble.

"Four forty-seven."

"That's not enough."

"I know."

"I need shelters for forty people. Food for two weeks minimum. Water filtration expansion. Medical supplies. That's... what, two thousand points?"

"At least."

"Where are you going to get two thousand points?"

Marcus looked at the mountains. The afternoon light was turning the snowcaps orange. Somewhere up there, in the abandoned towns and collapsed buildings between here and Aspen, infected were waiting. Points on legs.

"I'll run north tomorrow. Extended range. Hit the nests I've been mapping past the clean perimeter."

"Can you get two thousand in one run?"

"Probably not. But I can get enough to cover the first week. Then I run again."

Sarah wrote it down. Marcus, clearing run, Day 1. She underlined it.

"The community isn't going to be happy," she said.

"The community will manage."

"The community is going to see forty strangers eating their food and sleeping in their buildings and they're going to have opinions, Marcus. Loud ones."

He looked at her. She looked back. Fourteen years old and she could read a crowd better than anyone in Crystal Falls, including Marcus. She'd learned it the same way she'd learned everything else in the apocalypse. By paying attention and keeping score.

"I'll call an emergency council meeting tonight," Marcus said. "Before the screening is done. The council needs to vote before this becomes a rumor problem."

"It's already a rumor problem. Donna told the school. Barbara told the kitchen crew. By dinner, everyone will know."

"Then we address it at dinner."

Sarah nodded. She looked at the refugees on the road. A girl about her age was sitting with her back against a rock, knees pulled up, staring at nothing. Next to her, a boy maybe ten was asleep with his head on a rolled-up jacket.

"We're taking them in," Sarah said. It wasn't a question.

"We're taking them in."

"Then I need to rearrange everything. Tonight."

She left. Marcus watched her go. The notebook was already open, the pen already moving.

## — Joel —

Joel screened forty-two people in four hours.

He'd developed a system. Three questions that mattered and a dozen that didn't. The ones that didn't matter were there to see how people acted when they were talking. Did they make eye contact? Did they fidget? Did their stories stay consistent when he asked the same thing twice in different words?

The three that mattered:

Have you killed anyone? If yes, why?

What did you do before the outbreak?

Why did you leave the group you were with?

Most of the Denver refugees answered the same way. No, they hadn't killed anyone. They'd been ordinary people. They left because the QZ became a prison.

A few answers were different.

Raymond Osei, the man who'd spoken at the gate. Former high school principal. Steady, careful, used to managing large groups. He'd organized the caravan's departure and kept forty-two people alive for nine days on the road. Joel respected competence. He marked Raymond's file: leadership potential, no flags.

A woman named Patricia Delgado. Nurse, mid-thirties, pregnant. Seven months along. She'd left Denver because the medical supplies in the QZ had dried up and she didn't trust the remaining doctors. Joel's jaw tightened at that. A pregnant woman walking eighty miles through infected territory. He marked her file: medical priority, Dr. Reyes immediately.

A man named Warren Hess. Late twenties, strong build, quiet voice. Former Denver QZ security. Joel pressed him harder. Warren admitted he'd been part of Colonel Briggs's force before defecting three months ago. He said he'd never fired on civilians but couldn't prove it.

"Why should I believe you?" Joel asked.

"You shouldn't," Warren said. "But I walked eighty miles to get away from what that place became. If I wanted to keep doing what they were doing, I'd have stayed."

Joel marked his file: watch closely, restrict weapons access, reassess in 30 days.

A couple, David and Margaret Chen. David was a structural engineer. Margaret had managed the Denver QZ's water treatment system. Joel's eyebrows went up at that. A structural engineer and a water treatment specialist. He marked their file: critical skills, fast-track integration.

Two families with children under ten. An elderly man in a wheelchair being pushed by his adult daughter. A teenager with a thousand-yard stare who answered Joel's questions in monosyllables.

Nobody set off alarms. Nobody contradicted themselves badly enough to matter. Forty-two exhausted people who'd bet their lives on a rumor about lights in the mountains.

Joel finished the last interview at eight. He found Marcus at the dining hall.

"Clear," he said. "Forty-two total. One former QZ security, I want him watched. One nurse, seven months pregnant, needs the clinic tonight. Rest are civilians. No weapons beyond what they surrendered."

"Any concerns?"

Joel sat down. Meatloaf again. Lena's onions. Frank's questionable cooking. The dining hall was loud. A hundred people eating dinner and whispering about the forty-two people sitting on the road outside.

"Concerns," Joel repeated. "Yeah. We just went from a hundred and six to a hundred and forty-eight. That's not a concern. That's a math problem we don't have the answer to yet."

"We'll find the answer."

"You keep sayin' that."

"I keep being right."

Joel ate his meatloaf. It was adequate. The onions were doing the heavy lifting.

## — Marcus —

The emergency council meeting started at nine.

All seven seats filled. Marcus, Joel, Sarah, Tommy, Helen, Dr. Reyes, Lena. The pine table that Frank had built looked small with the weight of the discussion on it.

Marcus laid it out. Forty-two refugees from Denver. Joel had screened all of them. No security disqualifications. Full list of names, ages, former occupations, and medical conditions on Helen's clipboard.

"Where do they sleep tonight?" Tommy asked. The practical question first. Tommy always started with the practical question.

"Dining hall floor for most," Sarah said. She had a sheet of paper with a layout drawn in pencil. "I can fit twenty-eight in the dining hall with blankets. Eight in the school after hours. The rest in the two empty cabins on the north side that Carson hasn't finished."

"Those cabins don't have windows yet," Carson said from the back of the room. He wasn't on the council, but he'd shown up. Half the community had shown up. The dining hall was standing room only.

"They have roofs and walls," Sarah said. "That's better than sleeping on a highway."

"The food situation," Lena said. Her voice was flat. Not angry. Practical. "I can feed a hundred and six people through May with what we have planted and stored. I cannot feed a hundred and forty-eight. Not without cutting portions or burning through reserves."

"I'll cover the gap," Marcus said. "I'm running a clearing operation tomorrow. Extended range, two to three days. I'll have enough in the cache to bridge us to your spring harvest."

"You keep saying 'the cache,'" Carson said from the back. Loud enough for the room to hear. "Where exactly is this cache, Marcus? Because it keeps filling up and nobody's ever seen the supply line."

The room went quiet.

Marcus had expected this. He'd expected it for months. The fiction of the cache had held because people wanted it to hold. Because the alternative was asking questions that didn't have comfortable answers, and comfort was the currency that kept a community stable.

But comfort had a shelf life. And forty-two strangers were testing it.

"The supply situation is managed," Marcus said. "It has been since day one. The specifics are a security matter."

"Security matter," Carson repeated. He crossed his arms. "That's what you say when you don't want to answer."

"That's what I say when the answer doesn't change the outcome. The food will be there. The medicine will be there. The building supplies will be there. Same as last month. Same as the month before."

"Same as always." Carson's voice wasn't hostile. It was frustrated. The frustration of a man who built things for a living and didn't understand how the raw materials kept appearing without a visible source. "I'm not trying to start something, Marcus. But you're asking us to stretch everything we have for forty strangers, and you're funding it with a magic box that nobody's allowed to look inside."

Raymond Osei spoke from the doorway.

He'd come in quietly. Nobody had invited him, but nobody had stopped him either. He stood with his hands clasped in front of him, a tall man who looked like he'd been taller before nine days of walking had compressed him.

"May I say something?"

Marcus looked at Joel. Joel shrugged. Good enough.

"Go ahead," Marcus said.

"We left Denver because a man with resources decided the rest of us weren't worth feeding," Raymond said. His voice was level. The principal's voice. The one that made rooms listen. "Colonel Briggs had stockpiles. Ammunition, food, fuel. Enough for everyone. He chose not to share. He chose to control. When people got hungry enough to take what they needed, he shot them."

He looked at Carson.

"I understand the concern about resources. I lived it. I watched a city die because one man decided that the people inside it were a burden instead of a community." He paused. "If you tell us there's no room, we'll leave. We've walked eighty miles. We can walk further. But if you're debating whether we're worth the cost, I'd ask you to consider what kind of place you want this to be."

Silence.

Lena spoke first. "I'm not saying no. I'm saying the soil doesn't care about speeches. If we don't get the spring planting in the ground on schedule, we'll be rationing by August whether we have forty new people or not."

"I'll double the labor for planting," Sarah said. She was already writing. "The Denver group has farmers. At least three families listed agricultural experience. Margaret Chen ran water treatment. David Chen is a structural engineer. These aren't mouths to feed. They're hands to work."

"We need the water filtration expanded first," Tommy said. "I can do it in a week if I get the parts."

"You'll have the parts," Marcus said.

"From the cache," Carson said.

"From the cache."

Carson stared at Marcus for a long moment. Then he looked at the floor. Then he looked at Raymond Osei, standing in the doorway with the expression of a man who'd been arguing for his people's lives for nine days and was prepared to do it for nine more.

"I vote yes," Carson said. "But I want it on record that I asked about the supply line and nobody answered."

"Noted," Sarah said. She wrote it down.

The vote was unanimous.

## — Sarah —

Midnight.

Sarah sat on the dam walkway with her notebook. Below her, the dining hall glowed with light. Twenty-eight people were sleeping on the floor in there. Blankets and pillows that Marcus would replace tomorrow with proper bedding from the cache. From wherever the cache actually was.

The two unfinished cabins held another six each. Carson had gone back out after the meeting with a lantern and a caulking gun and sealed the window frames with plastic sheeting. He hadn't said a word to anyone. He'd just done it.

Patricia Delgado was in the clinic. Dr. Reyes had taken one look at her and gone quiet in the way that meant she was worried but wasn't going to say so in front of the patient. Seven months pregnant, malnourished, dehydrated. The baby's heartbeat was strong. Patricia's wasn't.

The rest of the Denver group had scattered into whatever spaces Sarah could find. Porches. The school hallway. A family of four was sleeping in the back of Tommy's pickup truck under a tarp.

A hundred and forty-eight people.

Sarah wrote the number in her notebook. Underlined it. Then she wrote the number that mattered more: the food reserves in days. Fourteen, at current consumption plus forty-two new mouths.

Two weeks. If Marcus's clearing run didn't produce, they'd be rationing before May.

She heard footsteps on the walkway. Marcus sat down next to her. He smelled like coffee and the cold night air.

"How's Patricia?" he asked.

"Dr. Reyes is keeping her overnight. She'll know more in the morning."

"The baby?"

"Fine. The mom isn't. But Nadia's good at this."

They sat for a while. The creek was loud. An owl called from the tree line and another answered from further up the valley. The stars were clear and sharp above the mountains, the way stars only got when there was no light pollution for a hundred miles. Which was everywhere now, except here.

"Carson's not wrong," Sarah said.

"I know."

"The cache story has maybe three months left in it. Helen's tracking every item that comes in. She's going to notice that nothing comes from outside and everything comes from the gravel lot behind the maintenance building."

"I know."

"So what do you tell them?"

Marcus didn't answer right away. He looked at the lights below. The dining hall. The clinic. The cabins. The greenhouse, faintly green in the dark. A town of a hundred and forty-eight people, alive and lit and real, built on a foundation that nobody except Sarah, Joel, and Tommy understood.

"The truth," he said. "Eventually. Not yet."

"Why not yet?"

"Because right now, they trust the results. If I tell them the truth, they'll trust the explanation. Or they won't. And if they don't, the results stop mattering."

Sarah considered this. She'd heard variations of this argument before. It was the same logic Joel used about the armory. The same logic Dr. Reyes used about not telling patients their odds. People could handle facts or they could handle hope. Rarely both at the same time.

"Raymond is useful," Sarah said. "He kept forty-two people alive on the road for nine days. That's not nothing."

"He reminds me of Tommy."

"He reminds me of you. Minus the magic supply closet."

Marcus almost laughed. Almost.

"I'm leaving at dawn," he said. "Three days north. Aspen corridor. There's a cluster of buildings around the old ski resort that my map shows heavy activity. If I can clear it, I'll have enough to cover the food gap and the filtration expansion and the building supplies for four new cabins."

"How many infected?"

"Map shows forty to sixty contacts."

"Sixty infected. Solo."

"The enhanced ones are worth more. And if there's a Clicker nest, the bonuses stack."

Sarah wrote it down. Marcus, 3-day run, Aspen corridor. She didn't write the part about sixty infected, because writing it down made it real and real meant she'd think about it while he was gone.

"Come back," she said.

"I always come back."

"I know. I'm saying it anyway."

She stood up. The notebook went into her jacket. The pen clipped to the spine.

Below them, Crystal Falls slept. A hundred and forty-eight people, old and new, under roofs and tarps and open sky. The lights from the dam reflected off the creek. Somewhere in the dining hall, a child was crying softly, the kind of crying that came from being in a strange place and not understanding why.

In the morning, Marcus would head north and Sarah would start rearranging everything. Again. The school schedule. The meal rotation. The work assignments. The patrol routes. The housing list. The food reserves. All of it, recalculated for forty-two new names.

She was good at it. She'd been doing it since she was thirteen.

The list was six pages long now.

"Goodnight, Marcus."

"Night."

She went inside. Marcus stayed on the walkway, looking at the map in his head, counting contacts, running point estimates, planning the route north.
