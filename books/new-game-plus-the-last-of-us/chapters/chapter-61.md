# Chapter 61: The Part That Stays

## — Ellie —

**July 2033. Crystal Falls, Colorado.**

She woke up at six-fifteen without an alarm.

For a few seconds she lay still and ran the same check she always ran. Sounds. Threats. Exits. Then she reached sideways and found the drawer handle and pulled it open, just to confirm. Knife still there.

She closed it and sat up.

The headboard shelf had Noah's drawing on it. She'd put it there herself, which meant she'd meant to do it, which meant she was apparently making decisions now instead of just surviving them. She wasn't sure how she felt about that.

She got dressed, splashed water on her face at the hallway basin, and went downstairs.

Noah was already at the table. He was wearing a shirt that had a faded logo on it she didn't recognize, and he had a small tin mug in front of him and a look on his face like he'd been waiting for something.

He pointed at her. "Welcome-to-Colorado starter pack," he said. "Second installment."

He slid a chipped mug across the table.

She picked it up. There were two wrapped hard candies inside, a pencil that had been sharpened with a knife and was therefore slightly crooked, and a folded note that said, in Noah's block printing: SURVIVAL SUPPLIES.

Ellie looked at the contents, then at him. "This is a chipped mug."

"It's a vessel," Noah said. "Vessels are classic."

"There are two pieces of candy."

"Candy is medicine."

"Is the crooked pencil medicine too?"

"That's precision engineering." He took a large drink of tea. "You're welcome."

She sat down across from him. Joel was at the stove. Sarah wasn't in the kitchen yet, which probably meant she'd been up before either of them and was already somewhere doing something that needed doing.

Ellie unwrapped one of the candies. It was cherry. She ate it.

"What's today?" she asked.

Noah counted on his fingers. "Marker walk at nine. School at ten. After lunch is greenhouse rotation, except you don't have to do that until your second week because new-person rule."

"How many new-person rules are there."

"Many. I made most of them up."

Joel set a plate down in front of each of them without saying anything. Eggs and cornbread and something from last night's stew that had been re-warmed and was better than it had any right to be.

"Eat first," Joel said. "Walk after."

Ellie ate. The candies were in her pocket and the crooked pencil was behind her ear by the time they finished, which she hadn't planned but somehow happened.

---

The marker walk took an hour. Ellie was better at the route this time, not because anything had changed but because she'd run it once already and her brain had filed it.

Blue. Yellow. Red. Blue. Yellow.

The leaning stake from yesterday had been reset. Donna had gotten Noah's note.

She made a note of that. Someone here actually did maintenance when you flagged it. In the QZ, you reported a broken lock and it went on a list and the list went somewhere and six weeks later someone showed up to look at it and two weeks after that someone showed up to fix it. Unless it was a lock on a cell door, in which case it got fixed the same day.

Noah was telling her about the greenhouse setup. Which beds had tomatoes, which had the herbs Lena guarded with unreasonable intensity, which one had a broken irrigation line that everybody knew about but nobody had fixed yet because the part for it needed to come from the Basalt supply run.

"You could fix it," Noah said. "If you knew irrigation stuff."

"I don't know irrigation stuff."

"Frank could teach you."

"Maybe."

She meant it, which surprised her.

School was the same shed, same chalkboard, but a different topic today. Water treatment chemistry. Marta explained filtration systems, why they failed, what you looked for when the output changed color or smell, how to test for contamination with the strips they made from the chemistry supply kit.

Actual useful information for a world where the water supply had been maintained by exactly one person for years and that person could theoretically get sick or leave.

Bryce was in the back row. He kept his eyes on the chalkboard the whole time.

At the break, he walked past her on the way to the water barrel and held out a canteen.

"Pump line's muddy today," he said. Not looking at her. "Use this one."

"Thanks," Ellie said.

He nodded and kept walking.

That was more than she'd expected. She filed it and moved on.

---

The alarm ripped through the settlement at eleven-forty.

A metal clatter from the south tree line, then two sharp whistle blasts from the watch post. It was a sound she didn't have a label for yet, but her body understood it immediately and completely, and she was on her feet before she knew she'd stood up.

Noah had gone pale.

Ellie grabbed his sleeve and walked him hard toward the school doorway. "Inside."

Marta had the younger kids moving already, calm voice, two directions, no panic. She'd done this before too.

Joel came around the corner of the main lodge with a rifle in one hand and a field bag in the other. His eyes found Ellie in three seconds.

He didn't ask if she was okay. He held out the bag.

"Post two," he said. "Stay close. Stay low."

Orders were easier than feelings.

She nodded and fell in behind him.

They moved along the fence line at a controlled jog. Not running. Not slow. The pace that covered ground without broadcasting how bad you thought it was. Helen took a position on the east flank. Keiko came in from the west. Nobody was shouting.

At the south line, Donna was kneeling beside a tripped wire, reading the ground.

Joel crouched beside her. Ellie dropped the field bag and put her back to a fence post, watching the tree line. Her breathing was wrong, too high in her chest, the kind that happened when your body committed to a threat before your brain had information about it.

Donna pointed at the dirt. "Single animal. Fresh. No boot prints."

A mule deer exploded out of the scrub twenty yards up the slope. One rear leg tangled in a broken section of wire, eyes white, crashing through brush and gone before anybody moved.

Joel exhaled once. Long and slow.

"False alarm," he called.

Keiko relayed it on the radio. The settlement's noise came back in pieces: a hammer resuming somewhere, voices, the thump of something being dropped.

Ellie didn't move.

She was still watching the tree line. Her hands had developed a fine shake she hadn't noticed until just now and couldn't stop.

Joel looked at her. He didn't say anything about the shaking. He picked up the pliers from the field bag and held them out, handle first.

"Come on," he said. "We reset the line."

She followed him.

He showed her the angle on the first stake, how to thread the new wire through the eyelet, how to set the tension. Not too tight, you wanted signal, not snap. She tried it.

"Better," he said, and moved to the next post.

They worked the line in short steps. Hook. Thread. Tension check. Move. By the fourth post, Ellie had the rhythm. By the sixth, her hands had stopped shaking.

Joel didn't point that out either.

At the last post he stood and brushed dirt off his palms.

"You did good with Noah," he said.

Ellie looked at the fence wire. "I just moved him."

"Yeah," Joel said. "That's the job."

He picked up the field bag and headed back. Ellie walked beside him, which wasn't something she'd planned. It was just the direction she was going.

---

After lunch, Frank put her to work sanding shelf boards in the workshop.

Joel was already there, fitting wall brackets, measuring twice before every mark. He muttered at a warped plank for about thirty seconds before deciding it wasn't worth the argument and setting it aside.

Ellie worked the sandpaper in long strokes like Frank had shown her and watched the wood dust drift in the light coming through the open side of the workshop.

"You always build stuff after an alarm?" she asked.

Joel didn't look up from his measuring. "Usually. Hands need something to do."

Frank grinned without stopping what he was doing. "He says it like it's a practical strategy and not like he's stress-built half the furniture in this valley."

"Frank," Joel said.

"I'm just saying the lodges are very well-furnished."

Ellie smiled without meaning to and looked back at her board.

When it was smooth enough, Joel showed her how to seat it on the side supports. He held one corner. She held the other. Frank passed the next board. Repeat.

The work had a rhythm to it. Same as the fence line but slower, no urgency, just the sequence of the thing. She'd always been better at having something physical to do when her brain wanted to spiral. The QZ had never given her useful things to do with her hands. Mostly she'd read and waited and tried not to think too hard.

By late afternoon, the shelf ran the full length of one wall. Clean. Solid. The kind of thing that looked like it had always been there.

She set her comics on one end and squared them up.

Joel stepped back and looked at it. He nodded once.

"Good enough to outlive all of us," he said.

"You saying I'm immortal or messy."

"Both, probably."

She laughed. It came out easier than yesterday.

---

Dinner was stew and cornbread and a twenty-minute argument about whether peach cobbler counted as a food group or a medication. Noah held the position that it was medicine with full conviction and cited several made-up studies. Tommy said three servings would be required for a proper diagnostic. Sarah said no, and delivered it with the calm finality of someone who ran a federation of eleven communities and was not going to lose to a dessert argument.

Ellie mostly listened.

That was still new. In the QZ you talked to fill the space because silence got filled with other things. Here the silence wasn't empty. It was just the room breathing.

After cleanup, she took her mug and went out to the back steps. The river sound was louder here, the valley cupping it up from below. She sat with her elbows on her knees and watched the lights along the lower path blink on as the evening settled in.

She heard the door a minute later and didn't turn around.

Sarah sat one step above her. Not close. Not far.

Neither of them said anything for a while.

---

## — Sarah —

She'd watched Ellie through most of the afternoon without being obvious about it.

The tripwire alarm had hit the girl hard. Not in a way anyone else would've noticed, the response had been fast and right, she'd gotten Noah inside and held her post and reset the line without complaint. But Sarah had seen the hands when Ellie thought nobody was watching. Seen the way she'd been still for too long after the all-clear.

That was the kind of thing that came from years of having alarms mean something that couldn't be undone.

You didn't talk someone through that. You gave them work and let them come back themselves, which was what Joel had done, and it had worked, because Joel understood that particular kind of person because he'd been that particular kind of person for most of his adult life.

Sarah let the quiet run for a while before she spoke.

"Alarm hit you hard today," she said.

Ellie was looking out at the valley lights. "Yeah."

"Makes sense."

"I hate that it does."

Sarah looked at the line of lights along the lower path. Donna's team, running the evening perimeter check. The same check, every evening, same route.

"You can hate it and still work through it," she said. "Both can be true."

Ellie picked at a small crack in the ceramic of her mug.

"Every time something here is..." She stopped. Started again. "Good. When something here is good, my brain starts running the math on how long until it gets taken away."

Sarah didn't answer right away. She waited.

"Like, okay," Ellie continued, voice lower. "Nice room. Good food. People who aren't screaming. Great. So what's the bill? Where's the part where I find out what it costs?"

Sarah shifted on the step.

"There is a bill," she said. "Chores, classes, safety rules, accountability. That's real."

Ellie made a face. "You sound like a training manual."

"I run a lot of training manuals."

That got a short laugh out of her.

Sarah's voice went quieter. "But the part you keep waiting for, where we decide you're only worth keeping if you perform right and the second you stop performing we put you back, that part isn't coming."

Ellie's jaw tightened. "People say that until things get hard."

"Sometimes they do," Sarah said. "That's real too."

She set her mug on the step and looked at the middle distance.

"I'm not asking you to trust the whole settlement at once. I'm asking for one honest inch at a time." She paused. "You did three today already. You came downstairs this morning without looking like you were planning an exit. You took the canteen from Bryce and said thank you. You reset the tripwire instead of sitting with the shakes."

Ellie stared at the valley.

"It still feels fake sometimes," she said.

"It probably will for a while."

Sarah nudged Ellie's boot with her own.

"It's okay to be scared of being happy," she said.

Ellie was quiet for a moment. "That's a messed-up sentence."

"It's also accurate."

---

## — Ellie —

The river ran somewhere below the valley dark, invisible but there.

Ellie let the sound fill the space where she didn't have words yet.

"I don't know how to do this family thing," she said. It came out smaller than she intended.

"Neither did we," Sarah said. "Noah bit Marcus when he found him. Literally. Teeth. Dad barely talked in full sentences for the first year after he came here unless someone was actively on fire." She picked up her mug again. "We learned by doing."

"Great ad campaign."

"We're working on it."

Ellie rubbed her thumb over the handle of the mug.

"What if I screw it up," she said.

Sarah shrugged. Not carelessly. The shrug that meant I've thought about this and here's what I actually believe.

"Then we deal with what happened and keep going. That's the whole model."

Ellie looked at her. "You really don't do big speeches, do you."

"I have one about resilience and dawn if you want."

"I really don't."

"Your loss. It's very inspiring."

They sat another minute in the easy quiet of two people who didn't need to fill every second. Ellie finished her tea. Sarah finished hers.

Then Sarah stood and stretched.

"Pick one routine for tomorrow," she said. "Something repeatable."

"Like what?"

"Breakfast at seven. Marker walk with Noah. School. Workshop. Pick one and keep it. That's how this works."

Ellie nodded slowly. "Breakfast," she said. "And marker walk."

"Good picks."

Sarah took both mugs. Paused at the door.

"Seven o'clock, kiddo," she said.

After she went in, Ellie stayed on the steps until the air went cold enough to feel it.

She watched the patrol lights move along the lower path. She counted them without meaning to, then stopped when she noticed.

Upstairs, she put her knife in the drawer again.

Then she pulled out Noah's pencil, the crooked one, and wrote three words on the back of his drawing.

SEVEN. MARKERS. STAY.

She pinned it to the headboard shelf.

The door stayed unlocked.

Not wide open.

Unlocked.
