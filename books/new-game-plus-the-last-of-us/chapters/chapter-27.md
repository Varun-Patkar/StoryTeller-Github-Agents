# Chapter 27: Pages

## — Sarah —

*From the journal of Sarah Miller. Composition notebook, college-ruled, found in the Marble Mercantile general store on Day 43. Cover: black and white marble pattern. First page torn out because she started writing her name too big.*

____

**Day 52. Evening.**

The council met today. For real this time, with an agenda and everything.

Tommy reported on the turbine. Joel reported on the gate. Donna reported on the watch rotation. Marcus sat at Frank's new table and listened to all of it, and I could tell he was doing the thing he does where he looks calm but his brain is running numbers behind his eyes.

I took notes. Not because anyone asked me to. Because someone should, and nobody else was going to.

Helen presented a perimeter assessment. She found five gaps in our defenses, including a river bank approach from the south that Joel hadn't accounted for. Joel's face did the thing where it goes completely flat, which means he's either annoyed or impressed, and with Joel those are usually the same emotion.

Carson asked about construction priorities. He was right to ask. We have one Carson and about six things that need building. I drew up a schedule that splits his time between the gate and Tommy's wiring, and nobody argued, which either means it was a good schedule or everyone was too tired to fight about it.

Frank built the table we met at. Pine, trestle base, pencil gutters along the edges. He didn't say anything about it. Just put it in the kitchen and went back to his workshop. That's Frank. He builds things nobody asked for and they turn out to be exactly what was needed.

Fourteen people ate dinner tonight at that table. We bumped elbows and passed plates and Gene told a story about catching a catfish in 1994. Nobody listened to the story and everybody listened to the story, if that makes sense.

It felt like something. I don't know what to call it yet.

____

**Day 53. Night.**

I slept through the gunshots.

That's the first thing. I took one of Gene's sleep pills because my hip was bad, and I slept through a gunfight fifty yards from my bedroom window. Seven armed men came up the river in the dark and I was unconscious. If they'd gotten past Marcus and Dad, I would've woken up to strangers with rifles standing over my bed.

So that's a feeling I get to carry now.

Dad told me about it at dawn. The Joel Miller version, which means he listed the facts in order and left out everything that mattered. Seven men, south approach, trip wires cut, Marcus and Joel handled it, one dead, six sent west, Marcus took a bullet in the shoulder.

"He's fine," Dad said. Like getting shot is the same as stubbing a toe.

I pushed. Dad pushed back. Then he stopped pushing back and told me the truth, which is the thing that's been sitting in my chest all day like a stone I swallowed.

Marcus took five of them down. By himself. In fifteen seconds. In the dark, on a gravel bar, outnumbered, and he did it so fast that Dad — who has fought more people in uglier places than I want to think about — called it "not normal."

Dad said it wasn't normal for a trained fighter. Wasn't normal for anybody. He's fought soldiers and smugglers and hunters and none of them moved like Marcus moved on that gravel bar.

"Practice," I said. "Fifty-three days of clearing runs."

Dad just looked at me. The look that means he loves me too much to tell me I'm wrong but also isn't going to lie.

I know it's not practice.

I've known since the farmhouse in Texas. Since the first week, when he walked straight to the infected without a compass and came back without a scratch and couldn't explain how he knew where they were. I've been keeping a list. Not on paper, not until now, but in my head, cataloging every impossible thing Marcus Webb has done since the night he appeared out of nowhere and saved my life.

Here's the list.

He knew where we were. Not our house, specifically, but the highway, the overpass, the exact stretch of road where the soldier shot me. He came running out of the dark toward a girl he'd never met, in a city he'd never lived in, on the worst night of the world, and he knew where to find me.

He pulled supplies out of thin air. Bandages, antibiotics, food, water. That first night, when I was bleeding and Dad and Uncle Tommy were gone and the world was burning, he produced a first aid kit that wasn't in his pockets thirty seconds earlier. I was half-conscious and I still noticed.

He knew things before they happened. Not everything. Not perfectly. But the infected at the farmhouse, the ones approaching from twelve miles out — he told Dad they were coming before there was any way to know. He walked four miles southeast to find them without landmarks, without tracks, without anything a normal person could have used.

He healed too fast. The gunshot wound from last night. Helen said it was a through-and-through, deltoid, and she expected weeks of limited mobility. I watched him carry a case of water with that arm today. Twenty-four bottles. He used both hands like neither one had a hole in it.

He has a system. A store, a shop, a catalog — I don't know what to call it. He buys things with points he earns from killing infected. I know this because he told me, back in the church in Texas, the night he opened up about being scared and not good enough. Points. A mall. Prices and purchases. It sounds insane when I write it down, but it also explains every impossible supply that's appeared since September 27th.

He acts like he's playing a game he's already beaten.

That's the thing I keep coming back to. Not the strength, not the supplies, not the impossible knowledge. The certainty. Marcus makes decisions the way someone makes decisions when they already know the answer. He chose Colorado because he knew it would work. He chose the dam because he knew it would work. He chose to save me because he knew where I'd be and when, and he ran toward a dying girl on a dark highway like he was following a script.

He's not from this world.

I don't mean that literally. I mean he acts like someone who's seen this movie before. Like he's read ahead in a book the rest of us are living page by page, and he's trying to steer us toward a better ending without admitting he knows how the story goes.

And I don't care.

I wrote that and I'm going to leave it there because it's true and I don't want to talk myself out of it.

I don't care where he came from. I don't care how the system works. I don't care if he's lying about the cache, or about his past, or about the hundred small things that don't add up when you line them up next to each other.

He saved my life. He carried me when I couldn't walk. He sat with me in a dark kitchen and told me he was scared because he trusted me enough to be honest about something that mattered. He built a place where fourteen people eat dinner at a pine table and argue about oatmeal. He got shot last night protecting those fourteen people and his biggest concern this morning was whether the bullet scared Carson.

That's who he is. Not the impossible parts. The parts in between.

Dad knows something is wrong with Marcus. I can see it in the way he watches him, the way he cleans his rifle a little too carefully after they come back from a run together, the way he said "not normal" this morning like he was testing whether the words would make me flinch. Dad is waiting for the other shoe to drop. He's waiting for the thing about Marcus that doesn't add up to become the thing about Marcus that's dangerous.

I don't think it will. But I also know that I'm thirteen and I've been alive for eight weeks in the apocalypse and I'm not qualified to make that judgment.

Except I am. Because I'm the only one who's been with him every day since September 27th. I'm the one who saw him break down on the ranch when he thought I was asleep, crying so quietly that the sounds barely made it through the wall. I'm the one who watched him give food to refugees he couldn't save and carry me through a city that was on fire and sit on a porch with my father and take a threat from a man twice his age without flinching.

The Marcus who fights infected for points is not the whole person. The Marcus who materialized a first aid kit out of nowhere is not the whole person. The whole person is the one who burned the coffee this morning because Frank told him to, and sat at the table and drank it without complaining, and let people think he was the kind of guy who burns coffee.

That's the person I trust.

And honestly? If the universe or God or whatever put a stranger in Austin, Texas on September 27th, 2013, with the knowledge to find one specific girl on one specific highway, and gave him the tools to keep her alive, then the universe can keep its secrets.

I got to live. That's enough.

____

**Day 54. Late afternoon.**

Long day. I'm writing this at Frank's table with a cup of bad coffee that I made myself, which means I can't blame anyone else for it.

The oatmeal thing happened this morning. I already handled it — rotation chart, portion explanation, the whole show. Carson calmed down. Gene made a comment about fear. Frank offered to take the blame for coffee crimes. Normal stuff. Normal apocalypse stuff.

Then I talked to Ava.

She told me about the commune in Aspen. About a man who started as a protector and ended as a dictator. She said it took three months for people to disagree with him and four months for him to stop letting them.

I keep thinking about that. Three months. We're at eight weeks. We're halfway to the point where the Aspen guy started going wrong.

But we have a council. We have charts on the wall. We have Monday meetings and domain authority and Joel with veto power on security. The structure doesn't depend on one person's mood, and even if Marcus disappeared tomorrow — I'm not going to think about that, but even if he did — the food rotation would still work. The patrols would still run. Tommy would still fix the turbine when it rattled. Lena would still plant garlic.

The commune guy in Aspen didn't have charts on the wall.

I know that's a small thing. I know that a meal rotation and a patrol schedule aren't the same as a constitution. But they're visible. They're fair. Anyone can read them. Anyone can question them. And when someone does question them, the answer is on the wall, not in one person's head.

That's the difference. I think. I hope.

Marcus and I talked on the dam walkway tonight. I told him the deal: he gets the points, I get the people. He accepted. He didn't argue or negotiate or try to take any of the people-stuff back. He just said "yeah" and drank his coffee and looked at the river.

It should have felt like a bigger moment. A formal agreement between the two people running a settlement of fourteen survivors in the Colorado mountains. A defining moment. A scene from a movie.

It didn't. It felt like two people who'd been doing their jobs finally saying it out loud.

I think that's better.

Also: I'm going to be fourteen in November. I keep forgetting that. Helen said I organize like someone with twenty years of experience. Gene said he's not afraid of Marcus because he's seventy-one and has earned the right to not be afraid of things. Ava is seventeen and she's afraid of every strong man she meets because one ruined her life.

I'm thirteen. I can't have children. My father kills people to protect me and my hip hurts when it rains and I manage a food rotation for fourteen people because nobody else was going to do it.

I don't know what I am. A kid pretending to be an adult? An adult who happens to be thirteen? The girl who got shot on a highway and lived because a stranger appeared out of the dark with bandages he shouldn't have had?

All of those, I guess. None of them fit and all of them are true.

____

**Day 55. Morning.**

Lena says the garlic is sprouting.

Not the normal kind of sprouting where you see a green tip and get cautiously optimistic. Real sprouting. Rows of tiny green shoots breaking through the soil in the raised beds behind the residence. She's been out there since dawn, kneeling in the dirt, talking to them. I don't know if talking to garlic helps it grow, but Lena seems convinced, and the garlic seems to be listening.

Mia drew a picture of the dam with crayons I asked Marcus to buy from the "cache." She put a big yellow sun over it and green trees on both sides and a stick figure on the walkway that she says is me. The stick figure has brown hair and a pencil in its hand. I look like a scarecrow holding a magic wand, but I told her it was beautiful, and I put it on the kitchen wall next to the patrol schedule.

Five charts and one crayon drawing. The administrative center of a fourteen-person settlement in the Colorado Rockies, eight weeks into the end of the world.

It's not much. But it's ours.
