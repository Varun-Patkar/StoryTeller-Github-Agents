# Chapter 59: Lights

## — Ellie —

**Day 49. Western Colorado. 4:22 PM.**

The truck smelled like old vinyl and pine needles and the beef jerky Joel had been eating for three states.

Marcus had found it in a barn outside Dayton. A Chevy Silverado with four good tires and half a tank of gas, which he'd topped off from a red canister that appeared from wherever his supplies came from. Ellie had stopped questioning the supply thing around Day 40. The man produced things from thin air. Okay. She'd lived in a military quarantine zone where they pretended mushroom people weren't eating the world. Everybody had their thing.

She sat in the back seat with her boots on the center console because Joel had told her twice to put them down and she'd figured out by the second time that he didn't actually care. He just needed something to say.

Ohio had been flat and empty. Indiana the same. The truck ate highway at fifty miles an hour, swerving around potholes and abandoned cars and one overturned semi that had spilled its cargo of sneakers across three lanes. Ellie had wanted to stop for the sneakers. Joel said no. Marcus said they were the wrong size. She didn't ask how he knew that.

Kansas was worse. Flat and empty but also hot and full of grasshoppers that hit the windshield like rain. The sky was too big. She'd spent fourteen years inside walls and the Kansas sky felt like standing on the edge of something without a railing.

Then Colorado.

The mountains came up slow and then all at once, the way things did when you were driving toward them. Brown plains turned to foothills turned to actual mountains with trees on them, green and real, the kind of trees she'd only seen in textbooks and Savage Starlight backgrounds. The air changed. Cooler. Thinner. Her ears popped.

Marcus had been quiet for most of the drive, doing the thing where his eyes unfocused every twenty minutes. Reading the invisible map. But when they turned off the highway onto a two-lane road heading south into a river valley, something shifted in him. His shoulders dropped. His jaw unclenched. He looked like a person coming home, which is what he was.

"Ten minutes," Marcus said.

Joel sat up straighter in the passenger seat. He'd been dozing, hat over his eyes, the way he did when he trusted someone else to drive. He pulled the hat off and looked at the road.

Ellie looked too.

The road followed a river through a narrow valley. Mountains on both sides, steep and pine-covered. A sign on the right shoulder said MARBLE — 3 MI, the letters faded to ghosts. Power lines along the road, and she almost missed it because power lines were just background noise, scenery furniture, things that existed in the old world and didn't mean anything now.

Except these had wires on them. Intact wires, running from pole to pole, disappearing around a bend.

"Are those..." she started.

"Yeah," Marcus said.

They came around the bend.

---

She'd imagined it. Of course she had. Marcus and Joel had talked about Crystal Falls for weeks. Six hundred people. A dam. Electricity. Farms. A school. She'd built a picture in her head from the fragments, the way you built a picture of a person from a voice on a radio. She'd imagined something like the QZ but cleaner. Walls and guards and buildings, military neat, organized by function.

She was wrong.

The valley opened up and the settlement filled it. Not a compound. Not a camp. A town. An actual town, sitting in the late afternoon sun like it had always been there, like the apocalypse had looked at this particular stretch of valley and decided to go around.

The dam was at the far end, a concrete wall across the river with a powerhouse beside it, and from the powerhouse, wires ran in every direction like the threads of a web. Buildings clustered along the river and up the lower slopes. Some were old, pre-outbreak structures patched and maintained. Others were newer, raw lumber and corrugated metal, built by people who knew what they were doing. A greenhouse glinted on the south side, glass panels catching the sun. Garden beds in rows, green and organized. A chicken run with actual chickens.

People. Walking between buildings. Carrying things. Talking to each other. A woman hanging laundry on a line. Two men working on the roof of a long low building. Three kids running across a clearing, chasing a dog.

Kids. Running. Without guards. Without walls around the clearing. Just running, because they could, because running was what kids did when they had space and nothing was trying to eat them.

Ellie stopped walking.

She hadn't realized she'd gotten out of the truck. Marcus had parked at a lookout point on the ridge above the valley and she'd opened the door and walked to the edge without thinking, drawn forward by the view the way a moth goes toward light, and now she was standing on a granite shelf with her boots in the dirt and she couldn't move.

The QZ had walls. Guard towers. Curfews. Floodlights that burned all night so the soldiers could see who was breaking the rules. The light in the QZ was about control.

This light was different. Warm light spilling from windows. A string of bulbs along a covered walkway. The greenhouse glow. The light here wasn't watching anyone. It was just on.

"Ellie?" Joel was behind her. Close but not too close. The distance he always kept.

"Yeah," she said. Her voice came out wrong. Tight. She swallowed. "Yeah, I'm good."

She wasn't good. She was fourteen years old and she'd never seen a place where people lived instead of survived, and the difference between those two things was hitting her like a truck she hadn't seen coming.

"Take your time," Joel said.

She took a breath. The air smelled like pine and woodsmoke and something cooking. Something real, not reconstituted QZ rations, not canned mystery protein. Someone down there was making actual food.

"Is that bread?" she asked.

Joel breathed out through his nose. Almost a laugh. "Probably. Lena bakes on Tuesdays."

"It's Tuesday?"

"All day."

She looked at the town again. At the kids and the dog and the laundry and the garden beds full of things growing in actual soil. At the power lines that meant light and heat and refrigeration and a hundred other things she'd taken for granted in the Before and hadn't known existed outside a QZ wall in the After.

"You built this," she said to Marcus, who was standing at the truck with his arms crossed, watching her with an expression she couldn't read.

"A lot of people built it," he said.

"But you started it."

"Yeah." He uncrossed his arms. "Twenty years ago, with a generator and four people."

Ellie looked at the valley one more time. Then she turned to Joel.

"Okay," she said. "Let's go."

---

## — Marcus —

The south gate was new since he'd left. Keiko's design, based on the barricade that held during the FEDRA battle. Steel-reinforced timber, eight feet high, with a watch platform on each side. The council had approved it in his absence, Tommy's engineering and Keiko's specifications. It looked solid. Professional. The kind of thing you built when you planned to still be here in fifty years.

The gate was open. Late afternoon, patrols out, people coming and going. Normal. The watchers on the platforms had spotted the truck two miles out and Marcus could hear the radio chatter on the settlement frequency. His enhanced hearing picked up the words clearly: returning truck, south approach, confirmed friendly.

He parked the Silverado beside the gate. Killed the engine.

Sarah was there.

She was standing just inside the gate, leaning against the timber frame with her arms crossed, wearing the same flannel shirt she'd had on the day he'd left. Different flannel. Same style. Joel's old pattern. Her hair was longer, pulled back, and she was thinner than he remembered, the way she got when she forgot to eat because she was managing eleven communities and a hundred daily decisions.

She looked at Marcus. Then at Joel. Then at the girl standing behind Joel with a backpack and a switchblade she was holding like a security blanket.

Sarah pushed off the gate frame and walked to Marcus first. She put her hands on his face and looked at him. Not checking for wounds. Just looking.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi."

She kissed him. Quick. Practical. Not a performance. Then she stepped back and looked at Joel.

"Dad."

Joel wrapped his arms around her and held on. He didn't say anything. Just held her. Sarah's hand came up to the back of his head the way it always did, steadying him, the daughter holding up the father the same way she'd held up everything else for twenty years.

Joel let go. Wiped his eyes with the back of his hand. Cleared his throat.

"Brought someone," he said.

Sarah turned to Ellie.

Marcus watched the moment and stayed quiet. This was Sarah's. She'd been preparing for it since he'd told her about Ellie seven years ago. She'd imagined this girl, planned for her, set aside a room and a place at the table and a spot in the family for someone she'd never met. Sarah Miller, who ran a federation of eighteen communities and managed five hundred people and hadn't taken a sick day in thirteen years, looking at a fourteen-year-old girl with dirty cargo pants and a backpack.

Ellie stood her ground. She had the switchblade in her right hand, closed, thumb on the catch. Her eyes were doing the thing they'd done at every new place since Boston. Scanning. Cataloguing. Exits, threats, the distance to cover. QZ instincts. Room 204 instincts.

Sarah didn't close the distance. Didn't reach out. Didn't kneel down or soften her voice or do any of the things that would have made Ellie bolt.

"You hungry?" Sarah asked. "We have actual eggs."

Ellie's thumb stopped moving on the switchblade catch.

"Like... from chickens?"

"From chickens. Scrambled, fried, or however you want them."

"You have a stove?"

"We have three."

Ellie looked at Sarah the way she'd looked at the valley from the ridge. Like she was seeing something she'd read about but never believed was real.

"Fried," Ellie said. "If that's okay."

"That's fine. Come on."

Sarah turned and walked into the settlement. Not looking back. Not checking. Just walking, the way you walked when you trusted someone to follow because the direction was worth following.

Ellie followed.

Marcus watched her go. The switchblade went into her back pocket. Her right hand was free. Open. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her palm as she walked, quick, the kind of gesture you made when you didn't want anyone to see.

Joel saw it too. He looked at Marcus.

"She cryin'?" Joel asked. Low.

"Yeah."

Joel nodded. He picked up Ellie's backpack, which she'd dropped by the gate without noticing, and followed them in.

---

## — Ellie —

The settlement smelled like sawdust and cooking and something she eventually identified as horses.

Sarah walked her down a dirt path between buildings. People noticed them. Of course they did. A new face in a community of six hundred was an event. But nobody stared. Nobody stopped her or asked questions. A man on a porch raised a hand and said "Welcome home" to Sarah, then nodded at Ellie like she was expected.

Maybe she was. Marcus had a radio.

The path opened into a central area. A firepit ringed with flat stones. A covered pavilion with long tables. Bulletin boards with schedules and announcements pinned to them in neat rows. A sign on a post that read COMMUNITY HALL with an arrow pointing left.

Kids were playing some kind of tag game in the clearing beside the pavilion. Five or six of them, ages maybe seven to twelve. The dog from the ridge view was with them, a big mutt with a grey muzzle, barking at nobody in particular.

One of the kids broke from the group and ran toward them. A boy, eleven or twelve, dark-haired, lean. He skidded to a stop in front of Sarah.

"Is that her?" the boy asked. He was looking at Ellie with the unfiltered curiosity of a kid who hadn't learned to pretend he wasn't staring.

"Noah," Sarah said. "Manners."

Noah looked at Ellie. Stuck out his hand.

"I'm Noah. I'm their kid." He pointed at Sarah and then at Marcus, who was coming up the path behind them with Joel. "Marcus found me in a crawl space when I was five. I bit him."

"You bit him?"

"He was pulling me out. I didn't know he was helping."

Ellie looked at the boy's outstretched hand. She shook it.

"Ellie."

"I know. Dad's been planning to get you for like seven years. He has a whole file."

"Noah," Sarah said again.

"What? It's true."

Ellie looked at Sarah. "He has a file on me?"

"Marcus likes plans," Sarah said. There was something in her voice. Not embarrassment. More like the tone of a person who'd lived with a particular kind of crazy for so long it had become furniture. "We can talk about it later. Let's get you fed first."

The kitchen was in the main lodge, a big log building near the dam. Real kitchen. Gas stove, running water from a tap, shelves with actual food in jars and bags. Not canned. Fresh. Flour, dried beans, preserved fruit. A basket of eggs on the counter.

Sarah cracked three eggs into a pan. The sound of them sizzling was, for some reason, the thing that broke Ellie.

Not the valley view. Not the kids playing. Not Noah's outstretched hand or the laundry on the line or the chickens or the string lights or any of it.

The eggs.

Because eggs meant a stove that meant gas that meant someone had set up a system that meant someone had planned and built and maintained and kept going for twenty years so that a girl who showed up dirty and scared with a switchblade in her pocket could walk into a kitchen and have eggs fried for her like it was nothing. Like feeding a stranger was just something you did on a Tuesday.

She sat at the kitchen table and pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes.

"Allergies," she said. "The pollen here is different."

"Must be," Sarah said. She didn't look over from the stove. She added salt to the eggs and flipped them. Gave Ellie a full minute of nobody looking at her.

Joel came through the kitchen door. He saw Ellie at the table. Saw her hands on her eyes. He went to the counter, poured a glass of water from the tap, and set it in front of her without comment. Then he sat across from her and started unlacing his boots. Like sitting in this kitchen was something he'd done a thousand times, which it was.

Captain the dog pushed through the screen door and went straight for Joel, tail wagging, nose in his lap. Joel scratched behind the dog's ears.

"Hey, bud," Joel said. "Miss me?"

Captain's tail hit the table leg. Ellie dropped her hands. Her eyes were red. She didn't explain.

"Is that your dog?" she asked.

"Family dog," Joel said. "He's old. Lazy. Sheds on everythin'."

Captain turned and sniffed Ellie's knee. She held her hand out and he licked it.

"Hey, Captain."

Sarah put the plate in front of Ellie. Three fried eggs, slightly crispy at the edges. A piece of bread, actual bread, toasted. A smear of something yellowish.

"Is that butter?" Ellie asked.

"We have goats."

Ellie picked up the fork. Ate the eggs. They were good. They were so good she ate them without talking, which was something she almost never did, and when the plate was empty she looked at it and then at Sarah and said, "Thank you."

"There's more if you want."

"Maybe later."

Sarah sat down across from her, next to Joel. Marcus came in through the back door with Noah, who immediately began telling Marcus about a snake he'd found near the greenhouse that turned out to be a stick, but a very convincing stick.

Ellie watched them. A family around a kitchen table. The dog on the floor. The sound of eggs in a pan and a kid talking about a snake-shaped stick. Normal. Stupidly, impossibly normal.

She put her fork down. "Where's my room?"

"Upstairs," Sarah said. "Second door on the left. It has a lock on the inside."

Ellie looked at her.

"Marcus mentioned you wanted that," Sarah said. "A door that locks from the inside."

Ellie remembered. The farmhouse outside Boston. Her terms for Colorado. A room with a door that locks and a bookshelf. She'd said it half as a test, half as a joke. Something to ask for that she didn't think she'd actually get.

"And the bookshelf?"

"Pine. Frank built it. He builds all the furniture here. It's empty, but we have a library."

Ellie stood up. Her legs were steady. Her eyes were dry now, mostly.

"Can I go see it?"

"It's your room," Sarah said. "You don't have to ask."

---

## — Joel —

He watched her go up the stairs. Boots on the wood steps. Door opening. The click of a lock.

The kitchen was quiet except for Captain's breathing and Noah eating a piece of toast.

Joel looked at Sarah across the table. His daughter. Thirty-two years old. Running eighteen communities and a research program and a family and making fried eggs for a stranger's kid on a Tuesday.

"She'll be okay," Joel said.

"I know."

"She's tough. Mouthy. Smart. Reminds me of someone."

Sarah raised an eyebrow. "If you say me, I'll throw this pan at you."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

Sarah looked at the ceiling. At the closed door up there, the locked door, the girl behind it who'd spent three weeks on the road with two men she barely knew because they'd walked into her room and told her she deserved better than what the world had planned for her.

"Thank you for bringing her home," Sarah said. Not to Joel specifically. To both of them. To the whole stupid, impossible, twenty-year plan that had started with a bleeding girl on a highway overpass and ended with fried eggs in a kitchen.

Joel reached across the table and squeezed her hand.

"Always," he said.

Marcus sat down at the table. Noah leaned against his side. Captain sighed on the floor. Outside, the string lights along the walkway came on as the sun dropped behind the mountains, and Crystal Falls did what it did every evening at dusk. It glowed.

Upstairs, behind a locked door, Ellie sat on a bed with a pine frame and looked at an empty bookshelf and a window facing east and thought about nothing at all for the first time in her life.
