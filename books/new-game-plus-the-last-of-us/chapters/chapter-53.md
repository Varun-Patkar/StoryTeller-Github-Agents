# Chapter 53: The Long Road East

## — Marcus —

**Day 1. Colorado to Kansas.**

The federation ended at the Topeka relay.

Not all at once. The patrol markers came less frequently after Pueblo. Every thirty miles instead of every ten. The cleared farmhouses still had federation flags in the windows, but the flags were faded, wind-torn. By the time they hit the Kansas border, the markers were hand-painted boards nailed to fence posts. After Wichita, there was nothing.

Marcus drove. Joel rode with the window cracked and his rifle across his lap and his eyes on the road. They didn't talk much the first day. The truck ate miles, Tommy's engine humming steady at sixty, and the high plains unrolled on both sides like a brown blanket pulled flat.

The highways were mostly clear through federation territory. Tommy's road crews had pushed wreckage to the shoulders years ago, creating a drivable corridor from Crystal Falls to the Kansas border. Beyond that, Marcus steered around rusted sedans and jackknifed semis and the occasional military vehicle left where it had died. Some of the cars still had things in them. A carseat. A suitcase. A stuffed bear pressed against a rear window, bleached white by twenty years of sun.

Joel noticed Marcus looking at the bear.

"Don't," Joel said.

Marcus kept driving.

They stopped twice. Once for fuel from a jerry can strapped to the truck bed. Once because Marcus's map showed four Runners three miles south of the highway, drifting in a loose circle around a collapsed barn. Not a threat. Not worth the detour. But Marcus marked them on the route map anyway.

"How many you figure are still out there?" Joel asked. "Total."

"Millions. Maybe tens of millions. The fungus doesn't kill its hosts. It keeps them running until the body physically can't anymore, and by then the mycelium has rooted into whatever surface they're standing on."

"Cheerful."

"You asked."

The sun dropped behind them. The plains went orange, then grey, then dark. Marcus pulled off the highway onto a county road and parked behind a rusted grain elevator. Good sightlines. One approach. The kind of spot Joel would have picked.

They ate Lena's mustard sandwiches sitting on the tailgate. The sky was enormous out here. No mountains to frame it. Just flat in every direction, and above it a spread of stars that Marcus still wasn't used to after twenty years without light pollution.

"Sarah used to name the constellations," Joel said.

Marcus looked at him.

"When she was little. Before the outbreak. I bought her one of those glow-in-the-dark star maps from a bookstore on South Congress. She stuck it to her ceiling and memorized every constellation in a week. Then she'd drag me outside on clear nights and point at the sky and tell me which ones I was lookin' at." He took a bite of sandwich. Chewed. "I never remembered 'em. She'd test me every time and I'd get maybe two right. She stopped bein' disappointed eventually. Just started tellin' me the names again like it was the first time."

"She still does that. The teaching thing."

"I know she does. I'm sayin' she got it from somewhere, and it wasn't me."

A coyote called somewhere east. Another answered. The old sounds of the plains, unchanged by fungus or collapse.

"Her mother was like that," Joel said. "Patient with other people's gaps. Didn't make you feel stupid for not knowin' somethin'. Just filled you in and moved on. Sarah got that from her."

Joel almost never talked about Sarah's mother. At Crystal Falls, surrounded by six hundred people and twenty years of built routine, he kept her sealed. Out here, with nothing but a grain elevator and the dark, the seal came loose.

"What was her name?" Marcus asked.

"Claire. Died when Sarah was two. Complication from a surgery that shoulda been routine. I had a newborn and a funeral in the same month." Joel set the sandwich wrapper down. Folded it. The careful way he handled small objects when his hands needed something to do. "I didn't know what I was doin'. Built houses for a livin'. Couldn't figure out how to warm a bottle."

"You figured it out."

"Tommy helped. My neighbor helped. The daycare lady on East Seventh, Ms. Rodriguez, she helped more than anyone. Took Sarah four days a week for three years and never charged me full price." He looked at the stars. "I keep thinkin' about those people. Ms. Rodriguez. The guys on my crew. Sarah's friend Naya from down the street. Where they ended up. Whether they made it."

"Some of them probably did."

"Some of them probably didn't."

The coyotes stopped. Wind across the grain elevator made a low, hollow sound.

"I talk about her more out here," Joel said. "Sarah. At home, I don't need to. She's right there. I can walk to the greenhouse and watch her argue with Lena about tomato spacing and I know she's okay. Out here..." He trailed off. "Out here I remember what it felt like to not know."

Marcus didn't fill the silence. Joel didn't need him to.

They slept in shifts. Marcus took first watch. Joel fell asleep in the truck cab with his hat over his eyes, the same way he'd slept on a hundred overnight patrols. Three minutes from alert to unconscious. A skill he'd never lost.

Marcus sat on the tailgate and watched the stars and thought about a girl in Boston who didn't know they were coming.

## — Joel —

**Day 3. Kansas City.**

He smelled it before he saw it.

Smoke. Old smoke, the kind that soaked into concrete and steel and never came out. And under it, something sweet and wrong. The fungal smell. Faint, but there.

"Skirting south," Marcus said. He was already turning the wheel.

The highway had brought them to the western sprawl of Kansas City. The skyline was still visible from ten miles out. Taller buildings standing like broken teeth against a grey sky. Shorter ones collapsed into rubble fields. A water tower on the outskirts had fallen across an intersection, its tank split open and rusted through.

Marcus's map showed contacts. Dozens of orange dots scattered through the city proper. Runners, Stalkers, at least two Clickers. And something else, further in, that the system flagged with a yellow marker Joel hadn't seen before.

"What's that one?" Joel asked, pointing at the map interface Marcus had projected onto the dashboard.

"Unknown. Could be a new variant. Could be a Beacon. I'm not going in to find out."

"Didn't ask you to."

They took a state highway south of the city. Two lanes, cracked asphalt, weeds growing through the seams. The suburbs were worse than the skyline. Houses with doors ripped off. Cars in driveways with the windows smashed. A school with a chain-link fence wrapped in razor wire and a faded spray-painted sign: FEMA INTAKE — CAPACITY REACHED.

Joel watched it all go by. He'd seen ruins before. Every clearing run passed through some version of this. But Kansas City was different because of its size. Crystal Falls sat in a valley. Its problems were contained, manageable. This was a city of two million people that had simply stopped.

A body hung from a highway overpass. Skeletal, clothes rotted to strips, rope still holding after twenty years. Joel looked at it and looked away.

"That's a hunter sign," he said. "Warning for the territory."

"I know."

"How far does their zone extend?"

Marcus checked the map. "Hard to say. No system data on human territorial boundaries. But the infected density drops east of the city, which means someone's been clearing the outskirts for their own use."

"Hunters clearin' infected to farm travelers."

"Yeah."

Joel checked his rifle. Habit. The magazine was full, the same way it had been full an hour ago. He checked it anyway.

They passed another sign. This one was newer. Red paint on plywood, nailed to a telephone pole: TURN BACK. NO AID. NO MERCY.

"Friendly," Joel said.

Marcus kept driving. His hands were loose on the wheel. Relaxed. Joel studied him from the passenger seat. Twenty years of knowing this man, and sometimes he still didn't recognize him. The kid who'd shown up on Outbreak Night with bandages and shaking hands and a refusal to let Sarah die was gone. In his place was something else. Something that could tear an armored vehicle apart with its bare hands and still cry at a funeral the next morning.

"Noah's from here," Joel said.

Marcus's jaw shifted. Barely visible.

"Yeah."

"You ever think about that? What his life was before the crawl space?"

"Sometimes. I don't know anything about it. His mother's name, what she did, where they lived. He doesn't remember and I never pushed."

"Smart. Some things don't need diggin' up."

They cleared the city's southern edge by noon. The highway opened up again. Flat land, farm fields gone wild with ragweed and switchgrass. A hawk circled something dead in a ditch.

Joel settled back into the seat. The highway stretched east. Nothing on it but them.

## — Marcus —

**Day 4. The Mississippi.**

The bridge was half gone.

They'd expected that. The I-70 crossing at St. Louis had been bombed in the first month, along with every major Mississippi bridge from Memphis to St. Paul. Standard military containment protocol. Cut the country in half to slow the spread.

Tommy's scouts had identified a railroad bridge thirty miles south of St. Louis that was still standing. Single track, steel truss, built in the 1920s and sturdy enough to hold a truck if Marcus drove slowly and didn't think too hard about the gaps in the deck plating.

He didn't think too hard about the gaps.

The truck crept across at five miles an hour. The river was brown and wide below. Barges had piled up against the bridge piers, rusted into a permanent dam of steel and silt. A flock of pelicans sat on one of them, unbothered.

"I hate this," Joel said. His knuckles were white on the door handle.

"Almost across."

"You said that two minutes ago."

"Still true."

The truck's tires thumped over the last section of deck plating and hit solid ground on the Illinois side. Joel exhaled through his nose. He didn't let go of the door handle for another quarter mile.

Marcus parked on a bluff overlooking the river. Evening light. The water caught the sun and turned copper.

"Radio check," Marcus said.

He pulled out the radio Sarah had programmed. Dialed the frequency. The Mississippi relay, the last station in the federation's network, was a solar-powered repeater Tommy had installed on a grain silo near Topeka in 2031. Its signal reached this far on good days.

Static. Then a click.

"Crystal Falls, this is Eastbound. Day 4 check-in. We crossed the Mississippi at the railroad bridge. Both fine. Proceeding on foot from here. Next check-in attempt tomorrow at 2100, but we'll be at the edge of relay range."

A pause. Then Sarah's voice, thin and broken by distance.

"Copy, Eastbound. Federation status normal. Keiko ran the militia session this morning. Tommy replaced a bearing on turbine two. Noah finished his carpentry project." A beat. "Be safe."

"We will. Love you."

"Love you too. Both of you. Crystal Falls out."

The radio hissed. Marcus tried the frequency twice more. Nothing but static. The relay's range ended here.

He turned it off. Put it in his pack. Kept it anyway, because Sarah had told him the emergency frequencies and because hope was light enough to carry.

"That's it," Joel said. "We're dark."

"We're dark."

Joel looked at the river. The sun was touching the water now, flattening into a red line on the horizon.

"Used to be you could drive from Austin to New York in a day and a half," Joel said. "Fill up at a gas station, eat bad food, sleep at a motel, keep goin'. Whole country was connected. You didn't think about it. Just got in your truck and drove."

"I remember."

"Nobody remembers. That's the thing. The kids in Crystal Falls, Noah, all of 'em. They've never been in a motel. Never seen a gas station that works. Never driven on a highway with other cars." He shook his head. "This river used to be a state line. Now it's the edge of the world."

Marcus stowed the radio equipment. They'd leave the truck here, hidden under a highway overpass with the battery disconnected and the tires covered. If everything went right, they'd be back in six weeks.

If everything went right.

## — Joel —

**Day 9. Somewhere in Indiana.**

Walking was different.

In the truck, the apocalypse passed by at sixty miles an hour. A smear of brown and green through glass. You could pretend it was just a bad road trip. The truck had doors and an engine and the illusion of control.

On foot, the world got close. Every sound mattered. Every building was a question. Every tree line could hide a Stalker or a man with a rifle.

Joel's body remembered this. Twenty years at Crystal Falls hadn't erased the instincts from those first weeks on the road in 2013. Check the corners. Watch the rooflines. Listen for the click.

They made twenty-three miles the first day on foot. Twenty-five the second. Joel's knees complained on the uphills and shut up on the flats. Marcus could have covered the distance in hours, but he matched Joel's pace without comment. The same way he'd matched it twenty years ago, walking west with a wounded twelve-year-old in a sports wagon.

The eastern corridor was bad.

Not dangerous, necessarily. Not yet. Just empty. Towns that had been abandoned in the first year and never reoccupied. Houses with front doors standing open, swollen on their hinges. Cars with flat tires and bird nests in the engine bays. A grocery store with the windows intact and the shelves stripped bare. A church with the steeple collapsed through the roof.

And everywhere, the green. Twenty years of unchecked growth had turned the eastern states into something between forest and jungle. Kudzu covered entire buildings. Trees grew through parking lots, their roots cracking the asphalt into chunks. Deer grazed on what had been someone's lawn, and they didn't run when Joel and Marcus walked past. They just watched with dark, unworried eyes.

"They've never seen people," Joel said.

"Not many."

"Huh."

At night they camped in defensible structures. A fire station with brick walls and a single entrance. A highway rest stop with good sightlines. Once, a hunting cabin set back from the road with a woodstove that still worked. Joel built a fire in it and they ate canned food heated on the stovetop while rain hit the tin roof.

Joel cleaned his rifle. Marcus studied the route map by firelight.

"Tell me about her," Joel said.

Marcus looked up. "Ellie?"

"No, the other girl we're walkin' across the country to rescue."

Marcus set the map down. The fire popped. Rain on the tin roof. A good sound.

"She's fourteen. Grew up in the Boston QZ. Military boarding school, FEDRA-run. No parents. Her mother, Anna, died the day Ellie was born. Marlene promised Anna she'd look after Ellie, but she did it from a distance. Visits, letters, nothing consistent."

"So she's been in the system."

"She's been in the system."

"What's she like?"

Marcus chose his words. This was the hard part. Everything he knew about Ellie came from a game he'd played in another life. A game that took place in a timeline that didn't exist anymore because Marcus had changed it. The Ellie he knew was a character on a screen. The Ellie in Boston was a real person, shaped by a world Marcus had already altered in a thousand ways he couldn't predict.

But some things were probably still true.

"She's tough. Grew up fighting for everything. She's mouthy. Uses humor to keep people at arm's length. Bad jokes. Puns. The worse the situation, the worse the pun."

"Great."

"She reads comic books. There's a series she loves, Savage Starlight. She'll have a copy in her backpack. She probably has a switchblade. She's left-handed."

Joel raised an eyebrow. "Left-handed."

"Left-handed."

"You know she's left-handed but you don't know what she looks like."

"I know what she looks like. Red hair, sometimes brown depending on the light. Small for her age. Green eyes."

Joel was quiet for a while. He set the rifle aside. The fire was low, casting more shadow than light.

"What's she afraid of?" Joel asked.

"Being alone. Not physically. She can handle being alone in a room. She's afraid of being alone in the world. Of everyone she gets close to leaving or dying. That's the thing that drives everything else."

"The humor."

"Yeah. The humor, the toughness, the attitude. It's all armor. Underneath it she's a kid who wants someone to stay."

Joel looked at the fire. His face in the dim light was the face of a man doing math he'd done before. Adding up the variables of a child who needed something and whether he had it to give.

"I know that math," he said. "I did it with Sarah, after Claire died. I looked at this baby and thought, I don't know how to do this. I'm a contractor. I build things. I don't know how to be the only person a little girl has."

"You figured it out."

"I figured it out because I didn't have a choice. You don't figure it out in advance. You just show up every day and do the next thing."

Rain on the roof. The fire settling into coals.

"She's gonna fight us," Joel said. "You know that."

"I know."

"She's gonna think it's a trick or a trap or we want somethin' from her. Because everyone in her life has wanted somethin' from her. The Fireflies want her immunity. FEDRA wants order. Marlene wants to save the world. Nobody's just shown up and said, 'I'm here because you matter.' Nobody's done that for this girl."

"No."

"Then that's what we do." Joel pulled his jacket tighter. The rain was picking up. "We don't explain the plan. We don't tell her about Crystal Falls or the federation or the medical research or any of that. Not at first. We just tell her she's in danger, and we're here, and we're not leavin'. Everything else comes later."

Marcus nodded. It was good. It was exactly right. Joel Miller, who couldn't give a speech to save his life, who expressed love through carpentry and gunfire and sitting in dark rooms with scared children, had just laid out the best possible approach to a girl he'd never met.

"You've been thinking about this for three years," Marcus said.

"Every day." Joel lay back on the cabin floor and put his hat over his eyes. "Wake me for second watch."

## — Marcus —

**Day 19. Western Pennsylvania.**

The hunter territory started at a bridge.

They'd been making good time. Twenty-two miles a day on average, sometimes more when the highways were clear and Joel's knees cooperated. Marcus's map had been mostly empty. Scattered Runner contacts in the tree lines, easily avoided. A Clicker in a rest stop bathroom that they walked past without engaging. Deer. Birds. The quiet mechanics of a world that had moved on without people.

The bridge was over a creek in what had been a small town. A banner hung across it, professionally painted: PITTSBURGH TERRITORY. TOLL REQUIRED. ALL TRAVELERS REGISTER.

"Pittsburgh," Joel said. He said it the way someone might say "rattlesnake."

"The intelligence says Pittsburgh fell to hunters in 2015. A group calling themselves the Regulators took the QZ after a FEDRA collapse. They've been running it as a walled territory ever since. Travelers go in, get stripped of supplies, and get conscripted or killed."

"How big is their perimeter?"

Marcus checked the map. Red contacts, human, clustered in a five-mile radius around the city center. Patrols on the major roads. Checkpoints on the bridges.

"We go around. Ten miles south, there's a gap in their patrol pattern. We can cross through at night."

"Let's go."

They moved off the highway and into the tree line. Single file. Joel first, reading terrain the way he'd taught a hundred militia members. Marcus behind, monitoring the map for movement.

It took six hours to navigate the southern gap. They crossed through industrial backlots and rail yards, stepping over tracks that hadn't seen a train in two decades. Once, they froze behind a warehouse while a two-man patrol walked past fifty yards away. Joel watched them through his scope. Two men, rifles, winter coats despite the June heat.

"Scared," Joel whispered. "Look at how they're walkin'. Tight to each other. Short steps. They're not huntin'. They're survivin'."

Marcus watched the patrol disappear around a corner. The system awarded no points for observing humans. No quest objectives pinged. Just two men in a dead city, trying to get through the night.

They cleared Pittsburgh's southern edge by midnight and made camp in an abandoned auto shop two miles beyond the last patrol marker. No fire. Cold food. Joel didn't complain.

"Twenty years," Joel said in the dark. "That's what happens to a city with no one buildin' anything. Just people takin' from each other until there's nothin' left to take."

Marcus didn't answer. He was thinking about Crystal Falls. About the first council meeting and the first harvest festival and the first baby born in a community with running water and electric lights. About how close they'd been, in those early weeks, to becoming exactly what Pittsburgh had become.

The difference was a seized turbine and a twelve-year-old girl who said "Home" on a ridge in Colorado.

## — Marcus —

**Day 22. Eastern Pennsylvania.**

The trees were taller here. Old growth mixed with new. The roads worse, broken by frost heaves and root systems. They'd left the interstates two days ago, moving through back roads and forest trails that Marcus's scouts had never reached.

Joel was tired. He didn't say it. He walked the same pace, checked the same corners, kept the same steady breath. But Marcus could hear the difference in his heartbeat. The small hitches when his left knee took a step down. The extra second he paused before climbing over fallen trees.

Fifty-three years old. Twenty years of hard living. Joel Miller was the toughest person Marcus had ever known who didn't have a system giving him superpowers, and even Joel had limits.

"We stop early today," Marcus said.

"I'm fine."

"I know. We stop early anyway."

They found a farmhouse on a hill. Stone foundation, solid walls, a well with a hand pump that still drew water. Marcus cleared it. Empty. No infected, no squatters, no signs of recent activity. Just dust and furniture and a calendar on the kitchen wall frozen on October 2013.

Joel sat on the porch and stretched his knees while Marcus pumped water and filled their canteens.

"How far?" Joel asked.

"Two hundred miles. Maybe eight days at this pace."

"Eight days." Joel looked east. The hills rolling away into haze. "She's been waitin' her whole life for someone to come. Eight more days won't break her."

"No."

"But I'd rather it was six."

Marcus brought him the canteen. Joel drank. The water was cold and clean, filtered through limestone for a hundred years before the world ended and kept going after.

"Six it is," Marcus said. "I'll carry the packs."

"The hell you will."

"Joel."

"I said I'm fine. We walk together or we don't walk." He stood up. Tested his knees. They held. "I'm not showin' up to save this girl while you carry my bags like I'm some old man who can't manage."

"You're fifty-three."

"And I can still outshoot every person in Crystal Falls except you, and you cheat."

Marcus almost smiled. "Enhanced perception isn't cheating."

"It's cheatin'."

They left the farmhouse. Headed east. Two men on a road, one of them more than human and the other stubbornly, completely human. The same way they'd walked out of Austin twenty years ago, except back then Joel had been the strong one and Marcus had been the kid who didn't belong.

The road bent through a stand of oaks. The leaves were full and green, filtering the afternoon light into moving patterns on the cracked asphalt. A robin sang from somewhere high up. Normal sounds. Normal world. As if nothing had ever gone wrong.

Eight days to Boston. Seven, if Joel's knees cooperated and the roads stayed clear.

A girl was waiting. She didn't know it yet.
