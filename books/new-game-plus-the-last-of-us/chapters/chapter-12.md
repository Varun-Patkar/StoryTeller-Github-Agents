# Chapter 12: What It Cost

## — Marcus —

The medical textbook cost twenty-five points.

He bought it at four in the morning, sitting against the wall of a ranch house kitchen while the others slept in the living room and Tommy kept watch on the porch. He hadn't planned to buy it tonight. He'd been browsing the Mall's Knowledge section, looking at farming guides and engineering manuals for the dam project, when he'd scrolled past a listing that stopped him.

**[Emergency & Trauma Medicine Reference (comprehensive, 1,400 pages): 25 points]**

**[Purchase confirmed. Points remaining: 610]**

He'd been telling himself for ten days that Sarah was fine. She was walking. She was eating. The wound was closed, no infection, the antibiotics had done their job. She'd been shot in the hip and she was walking without a limp. That was a miracle by any reasonable standard.

But Marcus had played enough games to know that "healed" and "undamaged" were different stats. HP could refill while a debuff lingered underneath. And he wasn't a doctor. He'd packed the wound with hemostatic gauze and given her antibiotics because the system told him to, and it had worked, and he'd never once stopped to ask what the bullet had actually done on its way through.

The book materialized on the kitchen counter. Heavy. Blue hardcover with gold lettering, the kind of textbook that cost three hundred dollars in a college bookstore and weighed enough to prop open a door. He opened it to the index. Found "ballistic trauma, pelvic." Page 847.

He read for an hour.

The chapter was clinical. Diagrams of the pelvic cavity with entry and exit angles marked in red lines. Cross-sections of the iliac region showing bone, muscle, vascular structures, organs. The language was dry and precise, describing catastrophic injuries with the same flat tone as a weather report.

A bullet entering the right anterior hip at a downward angle, the text explained, would pass through the iliac fossa and into the pelvic cavity. Depending on velocity and trajectory, it could damage the iliac artery (fatal without immediate intervention), the bladder, the colon, or the reproductive organs.

Sarah had been shot from the front, slightly to the right. The soldier was standing. She was being carried. The angle was downward.

Marcus turned to section 12.4: Reproductive Organ Trauma in Pediatric Patients.

He read three paragraphs and stopped.

Then he read them again, slower, because the first time his brain had refused to process the words and he'd gotten to the end of the section without any of it actually registering.

The third time, it stuck.

Ballistic injury to the uterus and ovarian structures in pre-pubescent females, the textbook stated, frequently results in irreversible damage to developing reproductive tissue. Surgical intervention within the first six hours can sometimes preserve partial function. Without surgical repair, scar tissue formation typically compromises the uterine lining and fallopian tubes. Long-term prognosis for fertility: poor to none.

Marcus closed the book.

He sat in the dark kitchen of a dead family's ranch house and stared at the wall and thought about a twelve-year-old girl sleeping on a couch fifteen feet away who didn't know yet that the bullet that almost killed her had taken something else too.

He'd saved her life. He'd stopped the bleeding and given her antibiotics and watched her heal and felt proud of it. Five hundred points and a Guardian trait. He'd felt like he'd beaten the level.

But the bullet had been inside her for seconds. Maybe less. And in those seconds, it had passed through tissue that a first aid kit couldn't fix and a system shop apparently couldn't replace.

He checked the Mall. Scrolled through medical items with a kind of desperate thoroughness. Bandages, antibiotics, painkillers, surgical tools, blood bags, splints, suture kits. Nothing about reconstructive surgery. Nothing about organ repair. Nothing about reversing damage that had already scarred over and healed wrong.

The system could sell him a textbook that described the problem. It couldn't sell him a solution.

A spider was building a web in the corner of the kitchen window. Marcus watched it work for a while. Methodical. Patient. Attaching silk to the frame one strand at a time. The spider didn't know the world had ended. The spider had work to do.

He waited for Joel to wake up.

---

That was the hardest part. The hour between reading the textbook and hearing Joel stir in the living room. He put the book in his pack. He made coffee on the propane stove. He poured two cups and set one on the counter and sat on a kitchen chair and looked at the spider and the pre-dawn grey through the window and tried to figure out what to say.

Joel came in at five-thirty. His hair was flat on one side from the couch cushion and he moved the way he always moved in the morning, slow and deliberate, checking corners and windows before he checked anything else. Habit. Contractor's awareness repurposed for a world where the thing coming through the window might have teeth.

He saw the coffee. Picked it up. Took a sip. Made a face.

"That's bad," Joel said.

"I know."

"We got anythin' else?"

"No."

Joel took another sip anyway and leaned against the counter and looked out the window. Tommy was visible on the porch, sitting in a wooden chair with the Remington across his knees, watching the road.

"Joel," Marcus said. "I need to talk to you about something."

Joel looked at him. The coffee cup stopped halfway to his mouth. Joel had a radar for bad conversations. Ten days of apocalypse had sharpened it, but Marcus suspected it had always been there. You didn't raise a kid alone for twelve years without developing an instinct for the moment a conversation was about to go somewhere you didn't want it to.

"What."

"I bought a medical textbook. From the supplies." Marcus gestured vaguely. They'd never named the system out loud. Joel knew Marcus had access to something impossible. The details were filed under "deal with later." "A real one. Trauma surgery reference. Fourteen hundred pages. I was reading about Sarah's wound."

Joel set the coffee down. "She's healin' fine."

"She is. The wound is closing. No infection. She's walking, she's running. All of that is true." Marcus took a breath. "But the bullet went through her pelvic area, Joel. And I'm not a doctor. But the textbook is written for doctors, and it's clear about what happens when a bullet passes through the reproductive organs of a twelve-year-old girl without surgical intervention in the first six hours."

Joel didn't move.

"The damage to her uterus and the surrounding tissue," Marcus continued, keeping his voice level, keeping it clinical because clinical was the only way he could get through this. "Scar tissue forms. The structures don't develop normally. The textbook calls the long-term fertility prognosis 'poor to none.'"

"Say what you mean."

"She probably can't have children."

Joel's coffee sat on the counter two inches from his hand. He didn't pick it up. He didn't move. He stood perfectly still, looking at Marcus, and Marcus watched Joel's expression shift into something that was worse than anger. Anger would have been easier. Anger had direction.

This was the look of a man who'd been given a gift and then told the price.

"You sure," Joel said. Not a question. A final request for the answer to be different.

"I'm not a doctor. I could be wrong. But the textbook isn't ambiguous. Pelvic gunshot wound, downward trajectory, pre-pubescent female. The diagrams match her wound location. I've read the section three times."

Joel turned away. He faced the window. The dawn was coming in slow and grey, the sun not up yet but the sky starting to separate from the hills. A thin band of orange sat on the horizon like a line drawn with a marker. Tommy's silhouette on the porch hadn't moved.

"She's twelve," Joel said.

"I know."

"She's twelve years old and she don't even..." He stopped. His voice had dropped. Not the dangerous quiet, the kind that came before Joel did something violent. The other kind. The kind that came from somewhere deeper, somewhere Marcus had never been and hoped he'd never have to visit.

"She don't even know what that means yet. Not really."

Marcus didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that wasn't hollow or presumptuous or wrong. So he sat at the kitchen table in a dead family's house and let Joel stand at the window and said nothing, because sometimes silence was the only honest response.

Joel stood there for a long time. Two minutes. Three. The coffee cooled on the counter. The sky got lighter. A mockingbird started up somewhere in the yard, running through its whole catalog, six or seven borrowed songs in a row. The mockingbird didn't know about the apocalypse either.

"The night she was born," Joel said, and his voice was almost normal, almost steady, "I was workin' a job in Round Rock. Commercial bathroom renovation. My phone was on silent because the foreman was a hardass about phones on-site. Her mama called four times before Tommy drove out and told me in person."

Marcus waited.

"I drove ninety on I-35. Got there in twenty minutes. She was already out. Seven pounds, four ounces. Screamin' so loud the nurse said she'd be trouble." Joel paused. "I held her and I thought about all the things she was gonna do. All the things she was gonna be. College. Career. Family of her own someday. Grandkids. The whole thing. And now you're tellin' me one of those things got took from her before she even knew she wanted it."

"I'm sorry, Joel."

"You saved her life."

"I know. But I couldn't fix..."

"I know what you couldn't fix." Joel picked up his coffee. Took a drink. His hand was steady. "Don't apologize for the part you did right."

He drained the rest in two swallows. Then he rinsed the cup and set it in the sink. An automatic gesture, absurd in context. Muscle memory from a life that didn't exist anymore.

"When do we tell her?" Marcus asked.

"That's my call. She's my daughter."

"Of course."

"Not today. Let her have today."

"Okay."

Joel put his hands on the edge of the sink and stood there for another moment, looking out the window at the brightening sky and the porch and his brother sitting in the chair with a rifle across his knees. Then he straightened up and walked out of the kitchen. Marcus heard the front door open. Heard Tommy say something. Heard Joel's low answer, too quiet to make out.

Marcus poured his own coffee into the sink. It was cold. He'd never taken a sip.

## — Sarah —

Something was wrong with her dad.

She'd noticed it by mid-morning. They'd left the ranch house at seven, same as always, same formation. Joel in front, Tommy behind him, Sarah in the middle, Marcus at the back. The road cut through flat grassland with mesquite trees poking up along a fence line that stretched to the horizon in both directions. The fence was pointless now. Nobody owned the land. Nobody owned anything.

Her dad was walking faster than usual. Not running, not rushing. Just a half-step ahead of where he'd been yesterday, like he wanted to be far enough in front that nobody could see his face.

She'd seen him do this before. Not in the apocalypse. Before. The week her mom left, when Sarah was seven. Joel had come home from work every day at the regular time and made dinner and checked her homework and done everything exactly the same, except he was always moving a half-step quicker than normal. Fast enough that the feelings couldn't catch up.

She'd been seven. She'd noticed.

She was twelve now and she noticed more.

She also noticed that Marcus wasn't making jokes. Marcus always made jokes. Stupid references to things nobody understood, comments about the weather or the food or whatever dumb observation popped into his head. Today he was quiet. Walking at the back of the line with his pack and his map and his mouth shut.

The two of them were doing the same thing. The half-step. The silence. The not-looking.

She didn't ask. Not yet. She'd learned from twelve years of watching Joel Miller that asking too early got you "I'm fine, kiddo" and asking at the right time got you the truth. Her dad had a schedule for honesty. You couldn't rush it.

She ate her second-to-last Jolly Rancher. Watermelon. Saved the last one for later.

The sky was overcast. No rain, just flat grey clouds that made the whole landscape look like a photograph someone had turned the saturation down on. Somewhere behind them, a dog was barking. Not close. A mile, maybe two. Barking at nothing, or at something, or just because it was a dog and the world was confusing and barking was the only thing it knew how to do about it.

They walked.

---

## — Joel —

He told her that evening.

Not because he was ready. He wasn't. He'd never be ready for this kind of conversation. He hadn't been ready for the talk about her mama leaving, or the one about why Grandpa was in the hospital, or any of the conversations that being a father forced on you before you'd figured out what to say.

He told her because she cornered him.

They'd made camp in a culvert under a two-lane highway bridge. Dry, sheltered from wind, big enough for four people to spread out without sitting on each other. Tommy was boiling water over the propane stove near the far end. Marcus was sitting on the concrete slope with his back against the bridge support, doing the eye thing, reading his invisible newspaper.

Sarah sat down next to Joel on his sleeping bag. She had her journal in her lap, the one she'd found in a gas station three days ago. Floral cover. Half the pages already used by whoever had owned it before, filled with grocery lists and dentist appointments from a life that was over. She wrote in the back half.

She wasn't writing now.

"Dad."

"Yeah."

"What happened this morning?"

"Nothin' happened."

"You and Marcus were in the kitchen before anyone was up. You've been walking ahead of us all day. You haven't looked at me since breakfast." She paused. "Something happened."

Joel looked at his boots. Old Timberlands. He'd been wearing them since Austin. The right sole was starting to separate from the upper. He needed new boots. Marcus could probably produce new boots from thin air. He could probably produce a whole shoe store from thin air. But he couldn't produce the words Joel needed right now, and neither could anyone else.

"Sarah."

"Yeah."

He tried three different openings in his head. Rejected all of them. Too soft. Too clinical. Too dramatic. None of them sounded like him.

"Marcus bought a book," he said. "A medical book. He was readin' about your wound. The hip."

Sarah's hand went to her right side. The place where the bullet had gone in and come out the other side and left a scar she'd carry for the rest of her life. She touched it through her shirt. She did that sometimes. An unconscious check. Hey, still there. Still healed. Still me.

"What did it say?"

"It said you're healin' good. Infection's gone, the muscle's repairin'. You're gonna be fine for... for walkin' and runnin' and all that."

"But."

"But the bullet..." Joel stopped. Started again. "When it went through, it damaged some things inside. Things that have to do with... when you're older."

Sarah was looking at him. Not with fear. With that flat, steady attention she gave to things that mattered. The same look she'd had at the ranch when the distant thump of bombs rolled across the sky and she'd watched the smoke without blinking.

"What things?"

"The parts that..." Joel pressed his palms flat on his knees. "The book says you probably won't be able to have babies, Sarah. When you're grown up. The bullet went through the parts that... that do that. And without a surgeon in the first few hours, the damage can't be undone."

Sarah didn't say anything.

Joel forced himself to look at her. Her face was very still. Not blank. Still. Like she was running the words through her head a second time to make sure she'd heard them right.

She looked at the journal in her lap. Looked at her hands. Looked at the culvert wall, where someone had spray-painted "CLASS OF 2014" in red, a graduation that would never happen for kids who were probably dead or running or hiding in some quarantine zone.

"So the scar," she said finally. "It's not just on the outside."

"No, baby. It's not just on the outside."

"Can a doctor fix it?"

"I don't know. The book says surgery right after it happened could've helped. But..." He didn't finish. They both knew why there hadn't been a surgeon. They'd been running through Austin in the dark, dodging infected, hiding from soldiers. There'd been Marcus with a first aid kit and a miracle that stopped the bleeding, and that had been enough to save her life, and it hadn't been enough for everything else.

"But we didn't have a doctor," Sarah said.

"No. We didn't."

She was quiet again. Her hand was still on her hip, pressed flat against the scar. She held it there like she was measuring something. Taking inventory.

"I don't even want babies," she said.

Joel almost laughed. The sound got as far as his throat and became something else.

"I know you don't, sweetheart."

"Ashley Parker's little brother threw up on my backpack in third grade. It smelled for a week. Mrs. Kendrick made me leave it outside the classroom."

"I remember. You made me buy you a new one."

"The purple one."

"The purple one."

"So it's fine."

It wasn't fine. Joel knew it and Sarah probably knew it too, somewhere underneath, in the part of her brain that understood futures and consequences and the things you don't want until someone tells you that you can't have them. But she was twelve. And at twelve, "I don't even want that" was a perfectly reasonable response to being told you couldn't have something, and maybe that was okay. Maybe the real weight of it would come later, in years, in pieces, in moments she couldn't see from here.

For now, she was handling it the way she handled everything. Head on. With a story about Ashley Parker's baby brother and a purple backpack.

"Is Marcus okay?" she asked.

That caught him off guard. "What?"

"He's been weird today too. Quiet. Did he..." She paused. "Does he feel bad about it?"

"He saved your life, Sarah."

"I know that's not what I asked. Does he feel bad about the part he couldn't save?"

Joel looked at his daughter. Twelve years old. Shot, scarred, walking through the end of the world. And the first thing she wanted to know after learning she couldn't have children was whether the person who saved her life was hurting because of it.

"Yeah," Joel said. "I think he does."

"That's dumb."

"Yeah."

"I'm gonna go talk to him."

"You don't have to do that right now."

"I know I don't have to." She tucked the journal under her arm and stood up. "I'm gonna go tell him he's being dumb."

Joel watched her walk across the culvert toward Marcus. No limp. Hadn't been one for days. She moved the way twelve-year-olds moved. Fast, careless, all knees and elbows, not thinking about her body or what it could or couldn't do.

He stayed on the sleeping bag. His cup was empty. The sky through the bridge opening was turning colors, the tail end of a sunset he hadn't watched.

Tommy appeared next to him. Sat down. Didn't say anything for a minute. Tommy was good at that. Knowing when to talk and when to just be there. The Army had taught him that, or maybe he'd always known.

"She okay?" Tommy asked quietly.

"She will be."

"You?"

Joel picked up a piece of gravel from the concrete and turned it between his fingers. Small, grey, nothing special. He dropped it.

"Mom used to say," Tommy started, then stopped. "Never mind."

"Say it."

"She used to say the worst part of bein' a parent isn't the bad things that happen. It's knowin' you can't stop all of 'em."

"Yeah." Joel exhaled. "Mom was right about most things."

"She was Mom."

They sat in the culvert. The sunset faded. The sky turned dark. Neither of them moved.

## — Sarah —

Marcus was doing the eye thing when she sat down next to him.

He stopped when he noticed her. Blinked. The invisible thing disappeared or paused or whatever it did when she showed up.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey."

She sat with her back against the concrete support, same as him. The culvert floor was cold through her jeans. She could hear Tommy and her dad talking behind her, low and steady, the rumble of two men who'd known each other their whole lives.

"Dad told me," she said.

Marcus looked at her. His face did the thing she'd noticed on Day One, at the gas station, when she'd woken up and seen him for the first time. Not panic, not pity. The face of someone running through a list of possible responses and throwing all of them out because none were right.

"I'm sorry, Sarah."

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't do the face. The sorry face. Dad already did the sorry face and Tommy's doing a version of it right now and I really don't need three of them."

Marcus closed his mouth. Opened it. Closed it again. Then he laughed. A small one. Just air through his nose and a twitch at the corner of his mouth that came and went like a reflex.

"Fair enough," he said.

"I mean it."

"I know you do. No sorry face. Got it."

She pulled her knees up to her chest. Her scar twinged. It did that sometimes. Not real pain, just a reminder. Like her hip was checking in. "Hey. Still here. Still the thing that happened to you."

"It's weird," she said. "Knowing something's wrong inside you that you can't see. My hip looks normal now. The scar's there but it's healed over. Everything feels fine. I can walk and run and it doesn't hurt. But there's this whole broken thing in there that nobody knew about until Marcus Webb bought a textbook at four in the morning."

"I should have checked sooner. I should have bought the book on Day One and..."

"And what? Been sad about it for ten extra days? What would that have changed?"

He didn't answer. She appreciated that. He could have said something reassuring or deflective. Instead he just sat with the question and let it be a question.

"How long have you known?" she asked.

"Since this morning. Four AM."

"So you carried it around all day."

"Yeah."

"That's why you weren't making jokes."

"Yeah."

"Marcus."

"Yeah."

"You saved my life." She said it simply. No drama. Just the fact, laid out. "You showed up out of nowhere in the middle of the worst night of my life and stopped me from dying on a sidewalk. You bandaged a gunshot wound in the dark while things were trying to eat us. You kept me alive. You kept all of us alive. You're walking eight hundred miles to build me a house next to a dam."

"Sarah..."

"I'm not done." She looked at him. "If you spend the rest of that walk feeling guilty about the one thing you couldn't fix, I'm going to be really annoyed."

"Annoyed."

"Very annoyed. And I am extremely annoying when I'm annoyed. Ask my dad."

"I believe you."

"Good."

They sat against the concrete support and listened to the evening. Crickets in the grass beyond the bridge. The faint clink of Tommy rinsing out the coffee pot. Joel's boots on concrete, pacing slow. The creek that ran through the bottom of the culvert, shallow and steady, going somewhere it didn't need to think about.

"Marcus."

"Yeah."

"You know how in games..." She trailed off. Restarted. She'd never outright said the word "games" to him before. Never confirmed that she'd figured out his whole deal, or at least the outline of it. But they were past pretending. "In games, can you go back? Like, undo something you missed?"

He was quiet for a second. "In some games. Not all of them. And usually not the things that matter."

"So this is the kind where you can't."

"Yeah. This is that kind."

She nodded. She'd known the answer before she asked. She was asking the question underneath the question, which wasn't about games at all, and he'd answered that one too.

She unwrapped her last Jolly Rancher. Green apple. She looked at it for a second. Pale green, translucent, catching the last light from the bridge opening. It tasted like fake apple and sugar and gas stations and summer and being a kid.

"When we get to Colorado," she said. "When you build the house and Tommy fixes the turbines and Dad builds the walls. I want a room with a window that faces east."

"East?"

"So I can see the sunrise. The ranch house had a window like that. Remember? The light came in early and it was warm on the floor. I liked waking up to that."

"I'll see what I can do."

"And a bookshelf. A real one. Floor to ceiling."

"Okay."

"And a dog. If we find one."

"Adding it to the list."

She smiled. Small and private, the kind that came and went fast. Not a happy smile or a sad one. Just the smile of a person who'd been told something heavy and decided, deliberately, to keep walking anyway.

The Jolly Rancher was gone. The last one. She folded the wrapper into a small square and tucked it in her jacket pocket.

Zero Jolly Ranchers remaining. Seven hundred miles to go.

## — Marcus —

Later.

Tommy's coffee, reheated, which made it worse. The fire they didn't build because fires attracted things. The dark under the bridge with stars visible through the opening and the creek running below them, low and constant.

Joel was sitting with Sarah. She was writing in her journal, leaning against his shoulder. He wasn't reading what she wrote. He just sat there, solid and still. A wall you lean on. Not doing anything, not needing to. Just present.

Tommy was cleaning the Remington. He'd cleaned it yesterday too. Some people had coping mechanisms that made sense. Tommy's was gun maintenance.

Marcus opened the Mall. Didn't buy anything. Just looked at the numbers.

**[Points: 610]**
**[Settlement Starter Kit Target: 735]**
**[Deficit: 125]**

One hundred and twenty-five points. Six or seven infected. One good clearing run. The math hadn't changed since yesterday. The road to Colorado was the same length. The dam was still sitting in a valley somewhere, waiting.

But when Marcus looked at the settlement cost list and thought about walls and turbines and rooms, the picture was different now. It had a twelve-year-old girl in it, standing in a room that faced east, with a bookshelf and maybe a dog, watching a sunrise through a window that existed because someone decided she deserved one.

He couldn't undo the bullet. The system didn't sell time machines or surgical do-overs or the six hours of emergency access that might have made a difference. What happened inside her had happened, and no amount of points could buy it back.

But he could build her a room.

He could build her a life where the thing the bullet took wasn't the thing that defined her. Where there were books and dogs and sunrises and walls her father built and lights that Tommy's dam kept burning and a community that grew up around her and gave her something the old world never got the chance to.

That wasn't nothing.

He closed the Mall. Lay down on the cold concrete. Listened to the creek and the crickets and Tommy reassembling the bolt on the Remington with practiced, automatic hands.

610 points. 125 short. Tomorrow he'd start closing the gap.

He fell asleep thinking about windows.
