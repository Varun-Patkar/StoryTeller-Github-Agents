# Chapter 29: Roots

## — Marcus —

Day sixty-five. First snow.

It came down in small, dry flakes that didn't stick to anything except the windshield of the truck and the shoulders of people who stood still too long. Marcus watched it fall from the dam walkway with a cup of coffee that had gone cold twenty minutes ago because he'd gotten distracted counting orange dots on his map.

The five-mile radius around the compound was almost empty now. Eight days of clearing runs since the mine, twenty-three infected killed, and the valley was clean enough that Joel had started sending two-person patrols instead of full armed teams. The ten-mile radius was getting thinner too. Marcus had pushed north to Redstone and south past the old highway junction, clearing nests that had been building since September. Enhanced Runners in a gas station. A pair of Stalkers in a church basement. Nothing as bad as the Bloater, but nothing easy either.

The points added up. Three hundred and twelve earned since Day 57. Minus a hundred and eighty on lumber, nails, and roofing tin for the two new bunkhouses Frank was building behind the maintenance shed. Minus sixty on winter clothing for the new arrivals. Minus forty on seeds for Lena's expanded garden plots, the cold-weather varieties that might survive a Colorado December.

Marcus at 393 points. Not rich. Not broke.

The new arrivals. That was the thing he kept coming back to.

Three families in the first week after the Bloater. Then two more. Then a group of eleven from a failed camp near Glenwood Springs, half-starved and carrying everything they owned in garbage bags. Joel screened every one of them. Helen logged every name, every skill, every piece of gear they brought. Sarah assigned bunks and meal rotations and work schedules and did it so smoothly that the new arrivals didn't realize they were being organized until they were already part of the system.

Community of fourteen on Day 57. Community of twenty-six on Day 65.

The dam's lights were doing what Marcus had always known they would. In a world without electricity, a light on a mountainside was a lighthouse. People saw it from the highway. They saw it from the ridgelines. They followed it up the Crystal River valley and found a gate with a crossbar and a man with a rifle who asked them hard questions before letting them through.

More came every week.

...

Day seventy-two.

Marcus cleared a Runner nest in a resort hotel east of Carbondale. Fourteen infected in the lobby and restaurant, including three Enhanced Runners that moved fast enough to test his reflexes. The fight took twenty minutes and left him with a shallow cut on his forearm that healed by dinnertime.

He bought a second generator (120 points) and an arc welding station from Tier 3 (200 points) on the walk home. Frank had been bending metal with a forge and a hammer. Now he'd have proper tools.

Marcus at 260 points. Tommy said the second generator would power the new bunkhouses without straining the dam. Ray rewired the distribution panel to handle the load.

Community at thirty-one.

...

Day seventy-eight.

A group of six arrived from the south. Two couples traveling together, and a man and woman who'd joined them on the road. The woman, Jessica Okafor, was visibly pregnant. Maybe seven months along. Her husband Derek walked beside her with one hand on her back and the other on a baseball bat that had seen use.

Joel screened them at the gate for forty minutes. Helen recorded their names. Sarah found Jessica a ground-floor room in the residence, the one with the east-facing window that used to be hers.

"You gave up your room," Marcus said.

"She's pregnant. She needs it more." Sarah picked up her sleeping bag from the hallway. "I'm in the bunkhouse with Helen and Ava. It's fine."

"Is it?"

"It's a room, Marcus. I'll live."

She walked away carrying the sleeping bag over one shoulder like a soldier on a march. Marcus watched her go. Thirteen years old. Managing the logistics for thirty-six people. Giving away her bedroom to a stranger because the stranger needed it more.

Joel, standing at the gate, watched the same thing.

"She's somethin' else," Joel said.

Marcus didn't answer. He didn't need to.

Community at thirty-seven.

...

Day eighty-five.

Marcus stood at the council table and looked at the numbers Helen had put together. Thirty-nine people. Eight housing structures, counting the original residence, the maintenance building, the two new bunkhouses, and four winterized cabins that Carson and Frank had built from lumber Marcus bought and logs Tommy cut from deadfall up the drainage. The dam was running at full capacity, 168 kW, powering everything. Water filtration for forty. Twelve raised garden beds, mostly dormant now but prepped for spring. Two observation posts manned around the clock. A gate that could stop a truck.

Tommy reported that Ray had finished wiring Bunkhouse B. Donna reported clean patrols for the third straight week. Helen reported food stores for six weeks at current consumption, longer if Marcus supplemented from the "cache."

"We need more housing before the next group arrives," Carson said. He'd become the de facto construction foreman, which surprised everyone including Carson. "Frank and I can build two more cabins if we have lumber by Friday."

"How much?" Marcus asked.

"Sixteen hundred board feet. Plus nails, hinges, and glass for four windows."

Marcus ran the numbers. About ninety points. He had 471 after this morning's clearing run east of Marble, a short one, six Runners in a hunting cabin.

"I'll have it tomorrow."

Carson nodded. He didn't ask where it would come from. Nobody asked anymore. The cache story had become one of those community fictions that everyone accepted because the alternative was a conversation nobody wanted to have.

Sarah caught Marcus's eye across the table. She raised one eyebrow. The look that said: "That story is going to break eventually."

Marcus gave a small shrug. The look that said: "I know."

Joel, seated at the far end, said nothing. Joel always said nothing at council meetings unless security came up. He watched, he listened, and when it was his turn, he spoke in short, precise sentences that ended discussions. The rest of the time, he was a weight at the end of the table. A reminder that the person who decided who got in and who didn't was paying attention.

"Next order," Sarah said. "Medical."

Helen glanced at her clipboard. "We need a doctor."

## — Joel —

Day eighty-six. One p.m.

Joel was on the gate when they came up the road.

Four people. Two men, a woman, and a teenage girl. The men were carrying packs. The woman had a medical bag, the real kind, dark leather with brass catches, the kind Joel hadn't seen since before. The girl carried nothing. She walked close to the woman and watched the trees.

Joel chambered a round and rested the rifle on the crossbar.

"That's far enough," he called.

They stopped. The taller man, bearded, forties, raised both hands. "We're not armed. We saw the lights from the highway."

"Where from?"

"Grand Junction. What's left of it."

Joel looked them over. The bearded man was skinny. Weeks without enough food. The second man was younger, maybe twenty-five, with a limp that favored his right leg. The girl was fourteen or fifteen, quiet and watchful, staying close to the younger man. The woman was the one who held Joel's attention.

She was mid-forties, dark hair pulled back, with the kind of steady posture that came from years of standing on her feet in a professional setting. Not military. Not construction. Something else. She held the medical bag at her side with the easy grip of someone who'd carried it ten thousand times, and she was looking at the compound behind Joel with an expression he recognized.

Assessment. She was evaluating the settlement the same way Joel evaluated threats. Not with fear. With professional calculation.

"You with the bag," Joel said. "Step forward."

She did. No hesitation. She stopped six feet from the gate and met his eyes.

"I'm Dr. Nadia Reyes. Emergency medicine. St. Mary's Hospital in Grand Junction."

Joel studied her face. Tired. Thin. But her eyes were clear and her voice was steady. Not the performed kind of steady. The real kind, earned through years of telling people hard news under fluorescent lights.

"You got proof?"

She opened the medical bag. Joel's hand moved to the rifle. She noticed, slowed her movements, and pulled out a laminated hospital ID badge. She held it up.

DR. NADIA A. REYES, MD. EMERGENCY MEDICINE. ST. MARY'S HOSPITAL & REGIONAL MEDICAL CENTER.

The photo matched. Younger, fuller face, but the same woman.

"How many people live here?" she asked.

Joel ignored the question. "Who are they?" He nodded at the other three.

"Paul Whitfield, maintenance staff from the hospital. Kevin Marsh was a patient in the ER when things went bad. Broke his ankle falling off a ladder two days before the outbreak. The girl is his daughter, Sophie."

"Any of you bitten?"

"No."

"You a real doctor? Not a nurse, not a tech, not somebody who watched a YouTube video?"

Something moved behind her eyes. Not offense. Dark humor, maybe. "I completed my residency at Denver Health in 2001. Board-certified emergency medicine. Eighteen years of setting bones, delivering babies, and cracking chests. Before the world ended, my biggest professional complaint was the coffee in the break room." She paused. "Is this an interview or an interrogation?"

"Both," Joel said.

"Fair enough."

Joel opened the gate.

He walked them through the standard process. Names. Skills. Medical checks. Helen met them at the maintenance building with a clipboard and a thermos of hot water. Paul Whitfield had engineering skills. The kid, Sophie, didn't speak. She held her father's arm and watched everything.

The doctor walked through the compound and Joel could see her cataloging it all. The bunkhouses. The garden beds. The dam. The observation posts. She wasn't impressed. She was counting. How many people, how much infrastructure, how long had this been here.

"How many residents?" she asked again.

"Thirty-nine," Helen said, writing. "Forty-three with your group."

"Medical facilities?"

Helen looked at Joel. Joel looked at nothing.

"We have supplies," Joel said. "Talk to Marcus about the rest."

"Who's Marcus?"

"You'll meet him."

## — Marcus —

Marcus was in Bunkhouse A replacing a window latch when Helen found him.

"There's a doctor at the gate," she said. "A real one."

"What kind?"

"ER physician. Eighteen years. She has an ID badge and the attitude to match."

He followed Helen to the maintenance building. Dr. Reyes was sitting at the kitchen table, Frank's pine table, drinking water from a tin cup and eating a bowl of the potato soup Donna had made that morning. She looked up when Marcus walked in.

"You're Marcus."

"I am."

"Joel says you have medical supplies."

"I do."

She set down the cup. "What kind?"

Marcus sat across from her. Up close, she looked like someone who'd been running on discipline and competence for nine straight weeks. The kind of exhaustion that didn't show in the body because the mind refused to let it.

"I have a portable surgical kit," he said. "Full set. Scalpels, hemostats, retractors, suture kits. I have a portable digital X-ray unit, battery-powered. A defibrillator. And an autoclave for sterilization."

Dr. Reyes stared at him.

"An X-ray machine."

"Portable. Digital flat panel."

"In a settlement of forty people. In the mountains. Two months after the end of the world."

"That's correct."

She looked at Helen. Helen looked at her clipboard.

"Where did you get a portable X-ray unit?" Dr. Reyes asked.

"We have a supply cache," Marcus said. The words came out smooth. He'd said them enough times. "Pre-positioned before the outbreak. Military surplus, medical equipment, construction supplies."

"Military surplus doesn't include portable X-ray units. Those are sixty-thousand-dollar machines. The Army uses them in field hospitals with dedicated radiology techs."

"This one's simpler."

"There's no such thing as a simple X-ray machine."

Marcus held her gaze. She was smarter about this specific subject than anyone else in the compound, which was exactly the problem. Helen had accepted the cache story because logistics officers dealt with supply chains, and supply chains sometimes produced strange inventories. Joel had accepted it because he'd decided the answers didn't matter as long as the supplies kept coming. Frank, Carson, Donna, the rest of them had accepted it because they didn't know enough about medical equipment to recognize what was impossible.

Dr. Reyes knew.

"I can show you the equipment," Marcus said. "You can decide if you want to use it."

She studied him for a long moment. The look a doctor gave a patient who was lying about their symptoms and she was choosing whether to push or let it go.

"Show me."

---

The clinic was a room in the maintenance building that had been tool storage until two weeks ago, when Marcus and Carson had cleared it out and Frank built shelves along the walls. It had power, running water from the filtration system, and a door that closed. Helen had arranged the existing medical supplies on the shelves in neat rows. Bandages, antiseptic, the antibiotics and blood pressure medication Marcus bought regularly from Tier 1. Helen's field surgery reference books.

The Tier 3 equipment was in the back, still in its cases.

Marcus opened them one by one.

Dr. Reyes didn't speak for a full minute. She opened the surgical kit and ran her fingers across the instruments, lifting each one, checking edges, testing the hemostats' tension. She examined the X-ray unit, turning the emitter in her hands, pressing the power button, watching the flat panel detector light up with a blue standby glow. She opened the autoclave and peered inside at the stainless steel chamber.

"These are new," she said.

"Yes."

"Not surplus. Not salvaged. New. The surgical instruments still have manufacturer's oil on them. The X-ray battery is fully charged. This autoclave has never been used." She looked at him. "Nobody pre-positioned brand-new medical equipment in a Colorado mine before the outbreak. That's not how caches work."

"Dr. Reyes..."

"I'm not going to ask you where you got them." She closed the autoclave lid. "I've spent the last nine weeks watching people die from things I could have treated with basic equipment. Infections. Fractures. A woman in Rifle who had appendicitis. She died because I didn't have a scalpel." She picked up a sealed suture kit and turned it over in her hands. "If you have tools that save lives, I don't care if they fell out of the sky."

"They work."

"Then I need a table. Flat surface, waist height, six feet long. Stainless if you can manage it, wood if not. I need the shelving reorganized by procedure type. I need running water from a tap, not a bucket. And I need an assistant. Not a doctor. Someone with steady hands who can follow instructions."

"Helen has Army medical training."

"What kind?"

"Combat lifesaver course. Eight years at Fort Carson."

"That's first aid with a better name." She thought about it. "But it's something. Can she handle blood?"

"She handled my gunshot wound a month ago."

Dr. Reyes nodded. She was already moving, rearranging the shelves, pulling supplies forward, creating zones. "I want to see every person in this settlement. Forty-three people means forty-three health assessments. Allergies, chronic conditions, medications, pregnancies, dental problems, old injuries. Everything."

"We have a pregnant woman," Marcus said. "Jessica Okafor. About eight months along."

Dr. Reyes stopped. "Eight months?"

"Roughly."

"Who's been monitoring her?"

"Helen. With the reference books."

"Reference books." She said it the way someone might say "thoughts and prayers." As though the concept existed but didn't qualify as medicine. "I need to see her today."

"I'll send her over."

Marcus left the clinic. Behind him, Dr. Reyes was organizing instruments by function, her hands moving with the automatic speed of someone who'd set up treatment spaces in worse conditions. He could hear her muttering to herself, a quiet running list of procedural notes.

Outside, the air was cold. Snow was falling, the small dry kind that turned the compound into something almost peaceful. The bunkhouses glowed warm. Smoke from the wood stove in the residence drifted sideways. Mia Haywood was building a snowman near the garden beds, using pebbles for eyes and a stick for a nose. Frank's dog, a mutt that had wandered into the compound on Day 70 and decided to stay, was watching the snowman from a safe distance.

Marcus at 381 points. Down from spending on window glass, lumber, and medical restocks. He'd go on a clearing run tomorrow. Push east toward Carbondale, where the map showed another cluster in a strip mall. The balance would recover. It always did.

Community at forty-three.

## — Sarah —

Day ninety-two.

Sarah's morning started at 5:45 with Gene knocking on the bunkhouse door to tell her that Cabin 3 had a leaking roof.

It didn't stop.

By seven, she'd dispatched Carson to patch the roof, reassigned Frank's crew to finish the new latrine building, and resolved a complaint from a woman named Priya Nair about the meal rotation scheduling her for breakfast duty three mornings in a row. Priya was right. The schedule had been copied wrong when Helen updated it for the new arrivals. Sarah fixed it, apologized, and made a note to double-check the rotation against the master list before posting.

By eight, she'd reviewed Tommy's power usage report (they were close to capacity with forty-eight people drawing electricity), approved Lena's request to convert the south garden bed to a cold frame for winter greens, and talked to Mateo about patrol assignment. He was fifteen now and wanted to join the perimeter team. Sarah thought he wasn't ready. Donna thought he was. Joel thought patrol was no place for kids.

"You're on patrol," Sarah told Joel.

"I'm not a kid."

"I'm thirteen and I run the council schedule."

Joel didn't have an answer for that. He rarely did when she played that card. He just looked at her with the expression that was equal parts pride and worry and uncertainty about which feeling to act on.

The compound had changed. Not just in size. Forty-eight people was a different animal from fourteen. Fourteen people was a family. Forty-eight was a small town, with all the friction and noise that implied. People had opinions. They had conflicts and habits that annoyed their neighbors and preferences that didn't match the posted schedule and ideas about how things should work that contradicted how things were working.

Sarah handled all of it.

Not because she was the best qualified. Helen had more organizational training. Donna had more authority on perimeter matters. Tommy knew more about infrastructure than everyone else combined. But Sarah had been here since Day 1. Since before the dam was running, before the first outsider arrived, before the gate existed. She'd watched this place grow from four people in an empty building to forty-eight people in a functioning settlement. That history gave her a credibility that couldn't be earned any other way.

When Sarah said "this is how we do things," people listened. Not because she was the boss. Because she'd been there when the things were decided.

She was at the clinic at ten, helping Dr. Reyes with health assessments. The doctor had been working through the settlement methodically, four or five patients a day, checking vitals and asking questions and writing everything in a notebook with handwriting so bad that only she could read it.

"Next," Dr. Reyes called.

Tommy walked in, looking uncomfortable. He sat on the exam table Frank had built to the doctor's specifications and submitted to having his blood pressure taken.

"One-thirty over eighty-five," Dr. Reyes said. "High side of normal. Are you sleeping?"

"When the turbine lets me."

"You're grinding your teeth. I can see the wear on your molars. You need a mouth guard."

"A mouth guard."

"I know it's not high on the list, Tommy. Consider it noted."

Sarah stood in the doorway, writing assessment results on her copy of the roster. Dr. Reyes was efficient in a way that didn't feel cold. She moved through patients like a machine, but she remembered names and followed up on things she'd asked about the day before.

After Tommy left, Sarah asked, "How's Jessica?"

Dr. Reyes washed her hands in the basin. "Thirty-four weeks. Baby's in the right position. Heartbeat is strong. She's anemic, but the iron supplements from your cache are helping." The pause on "cache" was subtle. Deliberate. "I want her resting. Light duties only."

"I'll adjust the rotation."

"And someone with her in the last two weeks. Not hovering. Just nearby."

"Lena volunteered."

"Good. Lena's calm. Calm is useful."

Sarah wrote it down. "Jess, light duty" on the schedule. "Lena, companion duty" on the assignment board. The charts on the kitchen wall had outgrown the kitchen weeks ago. They were in the maintenance building hallway now, covering three bulletin boards. The organizational nervous system of a settlement that was starting to need a filing cabinet.

"Dr. Reyes," Sarah said. "How many babies have you delivered?"

"Before? Dozens. ER deliveries, the ones that come in too fast for OB to take." She dried her hands on a clean towel. "Since the outbreak? Two. One in a Walmart parking lot. One in a basement in Fruita."

"Both okay?"

"Both alive."

Sarah nodded. She didn't need more than that.

She walked back to the kitchen table and pulled out the master roster. Forty-eight names. Forty-eight skill sets. Forty-eight mouths to feed and bodies to house and opinions to manage.

She remembered when the roster was four names on a scrap of paper. Marcus, Joel, Tommy, Sarah. Now it filled two pages. People from Grand Junction and Glenwood Springs and Aspen and places further south who'd seen the lights on the mountain and followed them. Some had skills the community needed: a carpenter, a plumber, two former teachers now running a morning class for the seven kids under twelve. Some had nothing but the willingness to work. Sarah found places for all of them.

The community wasn't fourteen people eating dinner at one table anymore. It was forty-eight people eating in shifts because the kitchen wasn't big enough. It was three bulletin boards instead of one. It was complaints about roofs and schedules and patrol assignments. Messy, imperfect, and growing faster than anyone could organize it perfectly.

She wouldn't trade it.

## — Marcus —

Day ninety-seven.

Marcus was on the dam walkway at sunset when he heard the crying.

Not distress. Not fear. A thin, high sound that carried through the still December air. A sound that didn't belong to any of the forty-nine people currently living behind the gate.

He set down his coffee and walked to the residence.

The clinic door was closed. Helen stood outside with her arms crossed and a look on her face that Marcus hadn't seen before. Not worry, not tension. Her eyes were bright and her mouth was pressed into a line that kept almost becoming a smile.

"Boy," Helen said. "Seven pounds, four ounces. Mother and baby are fine."

From behind the door, the crying continued. Thin and insistent.

Marcus leaned against the wall. His legs did something he didn't expect, a softness in the knees that had nothing to do with fatigue. He'd fought a Bloater alone in a mine shaft and his knees hadn't done this. He'd fought seven armed raiders on a gravel bar and his knees had been fine. But this sound.

"Derek's in there?" he asked.

"Holding the baby. Hasn't stopped crying since the cord was cut. Derek, I mean. Well. Both of them."

Joel appeared at the end of the hallway. He walked over, looked at Helen, looked at the closed door, and listened. The baby's wail rose and fell in short, hitching waves.

"Huh," Joel said.

They stood in the hallway, the three of them, listening to a newborn behind a closed door. The dam hummed. The lights buzzed. Snow was coming down outside, the heavy kind now, real December snow, covering the garden beds and the bunkhouse roofs and Mia's snowman, which had been rebuilt four times and was currently wearing one of Gene's old hats.

Dr. Reyes opened the door. She had blood on her gloves and sweat on her forehead and an expression Marcus had never seen on her face. Not clinical. Not evaluative.

Satisfied.

"Jessica and the baby are healthy," she said. "Delivery was clean. No complications. The autoclave earned its keep tonight." She pulled off the gloves. "Helen, sterilize the instruments. Everything on the tray."

"On it."

Dr. Reyes looked at Marcus. "Come in."

Marcus stepped into the clinic. Jessica was in the bed, propped up on pillows Helen had collected from three different bunkhouses. Derek sat beside her, holding a bundle of cloth that contained the smallest person Marcus had ever seen. The baby's face was red and wrinkled, its eyes were closed, its fists were curled against its chest. The crying had stopped, replaced by soft, hiccupping breaths that sounded like someone learning how lungs worked.

"His name is Owen," Jessica said. Her voice was tired and raw and happy in a way that happiness usually wasn't. Not bright. Deep. The kind that lived in the bones.

"Owen Okafor," Derek said. He looked at Marcus. His eyes were wet. "Born December fourth, 2013. In a clinic. With a doctor. With the lights on."

Marcus looked at the baby. Seven pounds, four ounces of person who hadn't existed an hour ago. A person who would never know a world before the outbreak. Whose first breath had been drawn in a room with an X-ray machine and surgical instruments and electric lights, attended by an ER doctor, in a settlement powered by a hydroelectric dam in the Colorado Rockies.

"Congratulations," Marcus said. The word came out thick.

He left the clinic.

---

The dam walkway was cold. The snow had stopped and the stars were out, sharp and hard the way they only got in mountains without cities. Except there was light now. A warm amber glow from the compound that colored the snow orange and made the tree line at the valley's edge into dark silhouettes.

Marcus leaned on the railing and looked at what they'd built.

Fifty people. Eight buildings. Twelve garden beds waiting for spring. A gate. A clinic with a real doctor. A school that met mornings in Bunkhouse B, where two former teachers taught seven kids math and reading. A dam producing 168 kilowatts. Two observation posts. A council that met on Mondays. A meal rotation on the wall. A patrol schedule.

A baby.

Ninety-seven days ago, he'd been standing in a dark apartment in Austin, Texas, reading a system notification he didn't understand while the world screamed outside. Four people with a truck and a plan and a system that dispensed supplies for killing infected.

Now there were fifty. One of them was an hour old.

"We're not building a camp," Marcus said to nobody. Joel was inside. Sarah was updating the roster. Tommy was checking the turbine because forty-nine people drawing power was different from fifty and Tommy checked everything.

He said it again, quieter, to the stars and the railing and the amber light on the snow.

"We're building a town."

The system was quiet. No notifications, no point awards, no achievements. Some things weren't worth points.

Marcus picked up his coffee and drank it. It was cold and bad. Sarah hadn't made this one.

He went inside.
