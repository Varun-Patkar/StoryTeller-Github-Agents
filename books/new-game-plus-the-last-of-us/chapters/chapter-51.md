# Chapter 51: The Signal

## — Marcus —

**June 2033. Crystal Falls, Colorado.**

The radio room was in the basement of the old maintenance building, below the generator housing and behind a steel door Helen Park had reinforced twice. Three relay stations connected Crystal Falls to the federation network. A fourth, boosted through the Redstone station and a chain of repeaters Tommy had installed across the Great Plains, reached as far east as Pittsburgh. On clear nights, further.

Marcus was in the carpentry shop with Noah when Tommy came through the door.

"Eastern channel picked up something overnight," Tommy said. "You need to hear it."

Noah looked up from the dovetail joint he'd been cutting for thirty minutes. Eighteen years old. Tall, quiet, with steady hands and the kind of patience that made Frank Garvey call him the best apprentice he'd ever had. He read Tommy's face and set down the chisel without being told.

"Go ahead, Dad."

Marcus followed Tommy across the compound. Mid-morning. The greenhouse crew was rotating shifts. Three kids kicked a soccer ball on the field Sarah had leveled behind the school in 2028. Bread smell from the mess hall. A patrol team coming through the north gate, rifles slung, talking about nothing in particular.

Crystal Falls at 637 people. Eighteen settlements in the federation. Running on routines that had been refined for twenty years. A normal Tuesday.

Except for the way Tommy was walking. Fast, hands in his jacket pockets, no small talk.

...

The message had come in on the eastern relay at 2 AM, bounced through Pittsburgh and decoded by Helen's overnight team at six. It was from a contact named Vasquez, a former Firefly supply runner who'd deserted in 2029 and settled in Hartford with a trading post. Marcus had cultivated him for four years. Monthly supply drops of antibiotics and ammunition, shipped through three intermediate handoffs, in exchange for intelligence on Firefly movements east of the Mississippi.

Tommy handed Marcus the transcription. Helen had written it out in her clean block letters on a sheet of lined paper.

PRIORITY. BOSTON QZ. GIRL FOUND. BITTEN THREE WEEKS AGO. DID NOT TURN. MARLENE AWARE. FIREFLY PERSONNEL MOVING EAST TO WEST ALONG I-90 CORRIDOR. MEDICAL TEAM MENTIONED. SALT LAKE CITY REFERENCED AS DESTINATION. TIMELINE UNCLEAR BUT ACCELERATING. SECOND SOURCE CONFIRMS. VASQUEZ OUT.

Marcus read it three times.

Three weeks. Ellie Williams had been bitten three weeks ago. She hadn't turned. Marlene knew.

He'd been watching this clock for seven years. It had just started counting down.

"That's her," Marcus said.

Tommy nodded. He'd been briefed on the retrieval operation two years ago, when the planning had moved from Marcus and Sarah's private file to a small operational circle. Tommy didn't know about the meta-knowledge or the alternate timeline. He knew what he needed to know: there was an immune girl in Boston, the Fireflies would kill her for a vaccine, and Crystal Falls had spent years preparing to prevent it.

"Salt Lake," Tommy said. "That's their hospital. Marlene's been running medical research out of there since at least 2028. If she's assembling a team and moving people west..."

"She's going to transport Ellie overland to Salt Lake City. Boston to Salt Lake is about 2,400 miles."

"How long would that take?"

"Depends on route and speed. Four to eight weeks if they're careful. Less if they push. But she has to organize first. Security detail, supplies, medical staff. That takes time."

"How much time?"

"Days. Maybe two weeks. I don't know."

Tommy leaned against the radio desk. He was fifty. Silver at the temples, lean as cable, and still the person who kept every turbine, pipe, and relay station in the federation running. His knees complained when the weather changed and he pretended they didn't.

"You need Joel," Tommy said.

"And Sarah."

"Then go."

---

Marcus found Sarah in the council room, reviewing agricultural projections with Lena. The quarterly report was thick. Crystal Falls exported surplus grain to three neighboring settlements now. Lena managed a staff of fourteen and grew things Marcus had never heard of before the apocalypse.

"Got a minute?" Marcus said from the doorway.

Sarah looked at him. Twenty years of reading his face. She didn't need two seconds.

"Lena, let's pick this up after lunch."

Lena gathered her papers without questions. She'd learned that particular tone a long time ago.

They walked to the powerhouse office. The same room where Sarah had asked Marcus to tell her everything seven years ago. Same desk. Same window overlooking the river. Same lock on the door that Sarah turned without being asked.

She sat on the edge of the desk. Waited.

"Ellie," she said.

Marcus handed her the transcription. She read it in five seconds. Read it again.

"Bitten three weeks ago. Didn't turn. Marlene has her." She set the paper down. "It's real."

"It's real."

Sarah opened the bottom drawer and pulled out the RETRIEVAL PLANNING file. It had grown from twelve handwritten pages in 2026 to sixty-three. Intelligence reports from four contacts across the eastern seaboard. Route maps confirmed by long-range scout teams. Boston QZ layouts reconstructed from trader descriptions and two Firefly defectors Marcus had interviewed in 2030 and 2031. Marlene's known associates. Dr. Jerry Anderson's last confirmed location in Salt Lake City. Supply calculations for a cross-country round trip. Radio frequency schedules for every relay between Colorado and the Atlantic. Contingency plans A through D.

Seven years of preparation for a morning like this one.

Sarah flipped to the tracking page. Marcus opened the system interface beside her.

**[Quest: Prepare for the Retrieval]**
**[Sub-objectives:]**
**[— Establish intelligence contacts in Boston QZ: 3/3 ✓]**
**[— Map a viable cross-country route: 1/1 ✓]**
**[— Assemble a retrieval team: 0/1]**
**[— Prepare contingency extraction plans: 2/2 ✓]**

"One left," Sarah said. "Team assembly."

"That's me and Joel. It's been the plan since we started."

"It's been the plan. Now it's an operation." She pulled out the route map, a hand-drawn sheet traced from six different scout reports. A line from Crystal Falls to Boston, marked with resupply points, known hazards, and settlement contacts. The western half, Colorado to the Mississippi, had been scouted by Marcus's long-range teams over the past three years. Confirmed terrain. Known settlements. Cleared corridors. The eastern half was reconstructed from radio intercepts and trader reports. Less certain. More dangerous.

"Western route is solid through Topeka," Sarah said. "Beyond the Mississippi, you're relying on the Pittsburgh contact and whatever Vasquez has on the I-90 corridor. It's thinner."

"I know."

"Travel time. You can cover ground faster than any vehicle now. If you ran the open stretches alone, you could reach Boston in..."

"Nine days. Maybe eight."

"Joel can't keep that pace."

"No. I've been thinking about that for three years. We take the armored truck to the Mississippi. That's 900 miles of mostly clear road through federation territory and the Kansas corridor we scouted in 2031. Four days, maybe five. After the Mississippi, we go on foot. Joel's in good shape for fifty-three. He can manage twenty to twenty-five miles a day."

"That's still three weeks from the river to Boston."

"If we leave in two days and Marlene takes two weeks to organize her departure, we arrive before she moves Ellie."

"If."

"If."

Sarah closed the file. She looked at the window. The river running below, the compound beyond it, the settlement that had grown from four people on a ridge in 2013 to 637 people who went about their Tuesdays without knowing what was coming.

"Two days is tight," she said. "Joel needs to hand off the militia schedule. Tommy needs to be briefed on full operational command while you're both gone. Helen needs to adjust supply logistics for two months without you."

"Can you do all that in two days?"

Sarah gave him a look. The same look she'd given him seventeen years ago when he asked if the council needed him.

"I'll have it done by tomorrow night. Get Joel."

---

## — Joel —

He was at the militia range when Marcus found him. Morning sessions, three days a week, same as the last fifteen years. Twenty-two regulars and Keiko's advanced team of eight. Joel didn't run the drills himself anymore. He stood at the back with his arms folded and told people what they were doing wrong. Most of the time, that was enough.

"Walk with me," Marcus said.

Joel handed the clipboard to Keiko without a word. She took it without asking. Twenty years of that.

They walked the south perimeter. The old gravel bar from Day 53, where Marcus had fought seven raiders and taken a bullet through the shoulder without slowing down, was a stone wall now. Eight feet tall, reinforced gate, sensor grid that Tommy maintained. The granite shelf where Joel and Helen had built the first observation point twenty years ago held a permanent watch station with a roof, a heater, and a radio.

Marcus talked. Joel listened.

Ellie Williams. Fourteen years old. Bitten three weeks ago in the Boston QZ. Didn't turn. The only known immune person in the world. Marlene, the Firefly leader, had confirmed her immunity and was assembling a medical team. Salt Lake City. Dr. Jerry Anderson. A surgical procedure to extract the Cordyceps growth from Ellie's brain.

A procedure that would kill her.

Joel kept walking while Marcus laid it out. His boots on the packed trail. The river below, the same sound it always made. Patrol flags on the watchtowers. A woodpecker on a pine somewhere up the ridge.

He'd known about Ellie for three years. Marcus had told him in March 2030, the morning after whatever Tier 5 had done to him in the mine tunnel. A ten-year-old girl. Immune. Fireflies who'd kill her for a vaccine. Joel had said he'd be ready when the time came.

Now it was the time. And the girl was fourteen, and the people who claimed to be saving the world were moving to cut her open.

"Three weeks ago," Joel said. "She got bitten three weeks ago and she didn't turn."

"No."

"That's when Marlene would've found out."

"Yes. The intelligence says Firefly personnel are already moving west along I-90. Marlene's organizing transport and a medical detail."

Joel put his hands on the stone wall. The river below. The compound behind him. The school bell ringing for the end of first period, faint from this distance.

Fifty-three years old. His knees ached in the mornings. He stretched before militia sessions now, something he'd have laughed at ten years ago. His hands were slower than they used to be and he could feel the difference even if nobody else could see it. He was still the best shot in Crystal Falls and the best close-quarters fighter after Marcus, but the gap between himself and the younger militia members was narrowing year by year.

None of that mattered.

A fourteen-year-old girl in a city two thousand miles away was about to have her life ended by people who thought they were saving humanity. She was alone. She had nobody coming for her. The people who should have been protecting her were the ones planning to kill her.

Joel had been that girl's age once. Then he'd been a contractor, a father, a man who lost everything on a highway in Austin. He'd spent twenty years building something worth keeping. He had a daughter, a son-in-law, a grandson, a town full of people who called him by his first name and asked him to tune their guitars.

He could stay. Nobody would blame him. He was fifty-three and he'd earned the right to sit on his porch and drink tea and watch the sunset.

He thought about the porch. The tea. Captain, who'd finally died in his sleep last winter at sixteen, curled up on Joel's feet the way he had every night for a decade. The empty spot where the dog used to lie.

He thought about Sarah on Outbreak Night. Bleeding on the ground. Joel running with her body because Tommy was yelling and the truck was moving and there wasn't time. Believing she was dead for fourteen minutes before a stranger appeared with bandages and impossible knowledge and a refusal to let her go.

Fourteen minutes. That's how long Joel had lived in the worst version of himself. The version where Sarah was dead and nothing mattered and the world was just a thing to survive, not a place to live. Marcus had given him back his daughter in fourteen minutes. Some men would've taken a lifetime to climb out of that hole. Some men never would have.

Joel knew which man he would have become. He didn't need Marcus to tell him.

"When do we leave?" Joel asked.

"Two days."

"Then we go get her."

He pushed off the wall. Started walking back toward the compound. His pace was steady. Unhurried. The walk of a man who'd made his decision and didn't need to make it twice.

"Joel."

He stopped. Turned.

"This girl," Marcus said. "Ellie. She doesn't know us. She doesn't know anybody's coming for her. She's going to be scared and angry and she's probably going to fight us before she trusts us."

"She's fourteen and alone and the world wants to use her up." Joel looked at Marcus. "I know how to talk to scared kids. I raised one."

He walked back toward the range. Keiko was running the next drill without him. She didn't need him there. Nobody needed him there. That was the whole point of the last fifteen years of training.

But a girl in Boston needed someone to come get her. And Joel Miller was going.

---

## — Marcus —

That night, Marcus sat on the dam walkway.

The stars were out. Crystal Falls glowed below. Greenhouse lights, the school, the clinic, the mess hall where someone was playing guitar. Joel had taught half the settlement over the years. Some of them were better than he was now, not that he'd ever admit it.

The system notification sat at the edge of his vision, patient.

**[Quest Update: Prepare for the Retrieval]**
**[— Assemble a retrieval team: 1/1 ✓]**
**[All sub-objectives complete.]**
**[Status: ACTIVE — Departure imminent.]**
**[Advisory: Hostile actors in transit. Expedite.]**

All four sub-objectives green. Seven years of work reduced to a checklist with four check marks.

Marcus at 14,206 points. Three years of clearing runs, passive multipliers, and a federation that generated achievement bonuses just by existing. The number didn't mean what it used to. In the first year, every point was survival. Now points were maintenance. Insurance. The currency of a man who'd already bought everything the system offered except the two items he couldn't yet afford.

He thought about the Tier 5 catalog. Three items. Apex Physiology, purchased. System Integration at 12,000 points, within reach. Legacy Protocol at 20,000, still out of range. He didn't know what Legacy Protocol did. The system had redacted the description the same way it had redacted the third Tier 5 requirement for six years.

It could wait. The girl couldn't.

...

Noah found him on the walkway an hour after dinner.

His son sat down on the concrete ledge without speaking. They watched the lights together for a while. The river below. The ridge above. The same view Marcus had been watching for twenty years, and still hadn't gotten tired of.

"You're leaving," Noah said.

"Day after tomorrow. Joel and I."

"I know. Mom told me."

Noah was quiet for a while. He had his mother's habit of building what he wanted to say before he said it. Sarah tapped her thumb against her finger. Noah went still. Same instinct, different expression.

"The girl in Boston," he said.

"Ellie. Her name is Ellie Williams."

"Mom said she's immune. That the people who are supposed to be protecting her are going to kill her instead."

"Yes."

Noah looked at the compound below. The carpentry shop where he'd spent the afternoon. The school where he'd grown up. The south gate where he'd learned to stand watch on Saturday mornings with Keiko's patrol team.

"When you found me," Noah said, "I was under a floor. My mom was next to me. She'd been dead for two days, maybe three. I don't remember exactly."

Marcus said nothing. Noah almost never talked about the crawl space. Five, maybe six times in thirteen years.

"You cleared the building above me. You could hear me through the floor. You pulled the boards up and reached in and I bit your hand because I thought you were one of them." Noah looked at his own hands. Carpenter's hands now, with calluses in the same places Frank's had. "You didn't let go."

"No."

"You had no reason to check under that floor. You were on a clearing run. Points and infected. That's what those runs are for."

"I heard you breathing."

"And you stopped."

The wind picked up along the ridge. The greenhouse panels caught the moonlight. Down by the gate, the watch team was changing shifts. Quiet voices, a flashlight beam, the creak of the reinforced hinges.

"Go get her," Noah said. "And bring her home."

He stood up. Squeezed Marcus's shoulder the way Sarah did. Then he went inside. The door closed behind him.

Marcus sat alone on the walkway. Twenty years since a stranger's apartment in north Austin. Twenty years since he'd run toward a highway because he knew a twelve-year-old girl was going to die, and he couldn't let that happen.

Now there was another one. Different city. Different girl. Same thing that mattered.

Two thousand miles east, in a quarantine zone that was tearing itself apart, Ellie Williams was fourteen years old and immune and alone. She didn't know what Marlene was planning. She didn't know that a man in Colorado had been carrying her name since before she was born. She didn't know that two days from now, two men she'd never met were going to start walking toward her.

Marcus went inside. He opened the hall closet and pulled out the pack he hadn't used since the northern clearing run three years ago. Set it on the bed. Looked at it for a moment.

Then he opened the RETRIEVAL PLANNING file to page one and started converting seven years of preparation into a two-day departure checklist.
