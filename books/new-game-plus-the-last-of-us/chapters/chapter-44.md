# Chapter 44: The Quiet After

## — Marcus —

Two weeks after the horde, Crystal Falls smelled like pine smoke and fresh dirt.

They'd burned the bodies on the road north of the barricade, a three-day job that required every adult who could hold a shovel. The pyre left a black scar on the asphalt that nobody wanted to talk about. Tommy had a crew resurfacing it with gravel and packed earth, and Dale's patrol planted wildflower seeds along the shoulders because Lena said the soil was good now and something should grow there. Nature didn't care about the violence. Nature just wanted phosphorus and nitrogen, and the dead had plenty of both.

Marcus sat on the porch of the residence at six in the morning, coffee in hand, boots on, nowhere to go.

Strange feeling. For eight years, every morning had started with a list. Clear this sector. Buy this from the Mall. Check the map. Run the perimeter. Plan the next clearing operation. Today the list was short. The valley was empty. The Snowmass Beacon was dead. The horde was ash. For the first time since Outbreak Day, the 25-mile radius around Crystal Falls showed zero contacts.

Zero. Not a single orange dot.

He sipped the coffee. Chicory and ground acorn, the blend Lena had perfected last winter. Not good. Familiar, which was close enough.

Noah padded out through the screen door in bare feet. Six years old, hair uncombed, Captain the dog trailing behind him, a crayon in one hand and a piece of paper in the other.

"Dad."

"Yeah."

"I drew the horde."

He held up the paper. Dogs again. Dogs fighting other dogs. Dogs with swords and shields and what appeared to be a catapult. The "horde" was represented by a large brown blob with teeth.

"That's very detailed," Marcus said.

"The brown one is the big one."

"The Bloater?"

"The big one. Can I have breakfast?"

"Yeah. Go ask Mom."

Noah went back inside. Captain followed, his tail hitting the door frame on both sides. The dog had gotten fat since the chicken coop went up. Too many scraps. Sarah said it built character. Joel said it built a dog that couldn't chase anything.

Marcus opened the system display.

**[Points: 8,592]**
**[Tier 4: Personal Enhancement — Active]**
**[Available Upgrades:]**
**[Enhanced Durability — 1,200 points]**
**[Enhanced Strength II — 800 points]**
**[Accelerated Healing II — 600 points]**
**[Neural Processing — 1,500 points]**

He'd been looking at Enhanced Durability for three months. The description was clinical: "Subcutaneous reinforcement of dermal and muscular tissue. Increased resistance to ballistic and blunt force trauma. Tier 4 enhancement, permanent."

Bullets can't easily pierce his skin. That's what it meant, in plain terms.

He thought about the Bloater on the road. The way it had grabbed him and thrown him fifteen feet and his shoulder had dislocated but not broken. Thought about the raider's bullet that had gone through his shoulder on Day 53. Clean through. Enhanced healing had closed the wound in hours, but the bullet had still punched through muscle and bone like he was made of ordinary meat. Because he was.

One thousand two hundred points. He had eight thousand five hundred and ninety-two.

He bought it.

**[Enhanced Durability — Purchased]**
**[Points: 7,392]**

The change was immediate and uncomfortable. A deep ache that started in his forearms and moved inward, like someone had injected something thick under every inch of his skin. Not pain exactly. Pressure. The feeling of tissue becoming denser, of muscle fibers layering and interlocking, of something that used to be ordinary becoming something else. It lasted about ninety seconds.

Marcus flexed his hands. They looked the same. Felt the same. But when he pressed his thumb hard against the porch railing, the wood dented before his skin did.

"Huh," he said.

Sarah appeared in the doorway with Noah on her hip and a wooden spoon in her free hand. Twenty years old. Her hair was longer now, tied back with a strip of cloth. The scar on her left hip was hidden by her jeans, but she still favored that leg on cold mornings.

"You're making that face," she said.

"What face?"

"The face you make when you buy something and you're waiting for me to notice."

"I bought Enhanced Durability."

She looked at him for a moment. Set Noah down. Pointed the spoon.

"If you tell me you're bulletproof, I'm going to test it."

"Not bulletproof. Bullet-resistant."

"Great. I'll use a smaller gun." She went back inside. Noah followed her, asking about eggs.

Marcus looked at his hands again. Seven thousand three hundred ninety-two points. Still more than he'd ever had before the horde. Enough for a second Tier 4 upgrade, or a massive infrastructure purchase, or a combination of both. The system Mall had a way of making everything feel insufficient no matter how much you had. Like the internet used to. Infinite catalog, limited budget, the persistent feeling that you should be spending smarter.

He closed the display and went inside for breakfast.

---

The kitchen was small and loud.

Noah sat at the table with Captain wedged under his chair, drawing on the back of an old council agenda. Marcus made oatmeal with dried apples and the last of a jar of honey Dr. Reyes had traded from the Basalt apiary. Sarah stood at the counter reviewing the week's labor assignments while eating a piece of toast with one hand.

"Frank needs Carson for the gate expansion tomorrow," she said, not looking up. "But Tommy needs Carson for the west outbuilding wiring. Both say it's urgent. Both are wrong."

"Who gets him?"

"Tommy. The wiring's a safety issue. Frank can use Paul and Ana for the gate frame. The actual metalwork doesn't happen until Wednesday."

She made these decisions forty times a day. The community had 349 people now. Two more families had arrived the week after the horde, drawn by the smoke they'd seen from the valley. Joel had screened them. A young couple from Glenwood Springs, and a woman traveling alone with a toddler who'd walked sixty miles from Redstone. Sarah had put them in the east bunkhouse and added their names to the meal rotation before they'd finished their first bowl of soup.

That was her gift. Not the organization, though she was the best organizer Marcus had ever seen. It was the speed. She saw a problem and solved it in the same breath. No committees. No deliberation. Just the right answer, delivered before anyone else had finished framing the question.

Joel came through the back door with sawdust on his shirt and a coffee mug he'd apparently been carrying around the compound all morning.

"Mornin'," he said.

"Morning."

He sat down at the table across from Noah, looked at the drawing, and nodded with the seriousness of an art critic.

"That a tank?"

"It's a catapult."

"Hm. Needs wheels."

"It has wheels."

Joel squinted. "Those are wheels?"

"Those are wheels."

"Alright." Joel drank his coffee. His eyes moved to Marcus over the rim. Reading. Joel was always reading people. Eight years of living together hadn't changed that. He assessed everyone in every room, every morning, as a reflex.

"You look different," Joel said.

"Bought an upgrade."

"Which one?"

"Enhanced Durability."

Joel considered this. "Meanin' what."

"Meaning my skin's harder to get through."

"Bullets?"

"Bullets. Blades. Bloater fists."

Joel set the mug down. Looked at Sarah, who was still reviewing her papers and clearly listening to every word.

"So if someone shot you point blank?"

"It'd hurt. Might bruise. Wouldn't go through."

"Hm." Joel picked the mug back up. "Useful."

That was the Joel review. One word. Sentence fragment. The man had watched Marcus fight two Bloaters barehanded two weeks ago, and his response to bullet-resistant skin was "useful." Like Marcus had mentioned a new brand of boot.

Sarah turned from the counter. "Council meeting at ten. Beacon briefing and coalition timeline. Both of you, please."

"Yes ma'am," Joel said. No irony.

Marcus finished his oatmeal. Noah had abandoned the catapult drawing and moved on to a dog wearing a hat. Captain snored under the chair.

An ordinary morning. Coffee, oatmeal, drawings, task lists, Joel being Joel. If you ignored the system display in the corner of Marcus's vision and the fact that the world outside the valley had ended eight years ago, it could have been any kitchen in any small town on any Tuesday.

That was the point. That had always been the point.

## — Sarah —

The council met in the kitchen of the main lodge, around the pine table Frank had built in Week 7. Seven seats. Five occupied. Helen took minutes on a yellow legal pad she'd been rationing for two years, writing in handwriting so small it required reading glasses to decipher.

Sarah ran the meeting. She'd been running them since Day 54. Since she'd taken the clipboard and the title and the scheduling authority that nobody else wanted. The council had grown from five seats to seven when Dr. Reyes and Lena joined, and the Monday meetings had evolved from panicked improvisation into something approaching routine governance.

"Beacon threat assessment," Sarah said. "Marcus."

Marcus stood. He didn't need to. He stood because Joel had once told him that people listened better when you were upright, and Joel was right about most things involving how humans worked.

"We've confirmed two Beacons in our region. Carbondale, killed April 18th. Snowmass, killed April 24th. Both were Stage 5 stationary emitters that attracted infected from a wide radius. The Snowmass Beacon had been active for at least two years and accumulated sixty-three infected in its immediate vicinity, with over two hundred and sixty more in transit from beyond my map range."

"The horde," Tommy said.

"The horde. Killing the Beacon released the infected from its pull, but they kept moving on their existing trajectory. Downhill. South. Toward us."

The table was quiet. Everyone at it had been in Crystal Falls for the battle. Helen had counted the bodies. Four hundred and twelve.

"How many more Beacons are out there?" Lena asked.

"I don't know. My map range is twenty-five miles. Beacons show up as dense clusters that don't disperse. I can identify them within range, but anything beyond that is blind."

"So there could be Beacons in every direction."

"Yes."

"And each one is building a horde."

"Potentially."

Tommy leaned back. "We need scouts. Way beyond the twenty-five. Pairs, going out fifty, sixty miles. Looking for clusters."

"That's dangerous," Joel said from the end of the table. Arms crossed. Standard Joel posture for meetings. "Stalker country past Aspen. Stalker country east of Glenwood. You send scouts that far out, they're on their own."

"Which is why we need the coalition," Sarah said.

She opened her clipboard. The notes were organized by settlement, by distance, by estimated population, by leadership contact. Kate Schultz in Basalt. Glenwood Springs, contact pending. Willow Creek, contact pending. The Redstone group, thirty people, no formal leader, talked to Helen's patrol twice.

"Basalt Station voted yes to the shared defense agreement four days ago. Kate Schultz sent a runner. They're in. That gives us coverage about twelve miles south through their patrol area. If we bring Glenwood Springs into the network, we extend northwest. Willow Creek covers the east valley."

"And if none of them have Beacons in their zones?" Dr. Reyes asked.

"Then we got lucky. If they do, we find them before they mature. The Carbondale Beacon was maybe six months old when Marcus killed it. Snowmass was two years. The difference between nineteen infected and two hundred sixty. Early detection is everything."

The council voted. Unanimous. Accelerate outreach. Sarah would visit Glenwood Springs by month's end. Tommy would take a team to make contact with Willow Creek. Joel would train a long-range scout unit for deep-valley patrols.

The meeting lasted forty minutes. Sarah took notes in the margins of Helen's legal pad because she'd run out of her own paper three days ago. Marcus bought her a new notebook from the Mall after the meeting. She didn't ask how much it cost.

"Twelve points," he said anyway.

"Frivolous."

"You're welcome."

## — Marcus —

The evening came slowly, the way May evenings did in the mountains.

The sun dropped behind the western ridge around seven-thirty, leaving the sky in layers. Orange at the horizon, purple above, dark blue at the top where the first stars were already out. The temperature fell fast once the light went. Mountain air didn't hold heat. By eight o'clock, you wanted a jacket and a fire.

The main lodge had the only real fireplace in Crystal Falls. Stone hearth, pine mantel, a chimney Frank had relined with salvaged flue pipe last winter. It drew well. The lodge served as the community's gathering space, the meeting hall, the living room for anyone who wanted company after the day's work was done. Most evenings, twenty or thirty people. Cards, conversation, Lena's tea, someone's kid running through.

Tonight it was quiet. The horde had changed the community's rhythms. People went to bed earlier. Stayed closer to their quarters. The lights-on, doors-open confidence of the last year had pulled inward. It would come back. Sarah was already planning the next community dinner. But for now, the lodge was mostly empty.

Marcus sat in the big chair near the fire. Sarah was on the couch with her feet tucked under her and a book she'd been reading for three months, two pages at a time, because she never had more than ten minutes before someone needed something. Joel sat on the stone hearth with his back to the flames, a tin mug of chicory in his hands, staring at nothing in particular. Tommy had the other chair, boots off, socked feet on a stool, looking more relaxed than anyone Joel Miller had ever produced.

Noah was in bed. Captain was in bed with him. The compound was quiet except for the generator's low hum and a coyote calling somewhere up the valley.

Joel's guitar leaned against the hearth. Frank's guitar, technically. Frank had found it in a vacation cabin in 2016, reglued the bridge, replaced three strings with fishing line and three with actual strings Marcus had bought from the Mall, and given it to Joel when Joel mentioned he used to play. Joel had carried it from room to room for a week before touching it. Then he'd played "Wish You Were Here" at the harvest festival and made two hundred people cry.

He hadn't played since the horde. Two weeks. The guitar leaned against the hearth and gathered dust and nobody mentioned it.

"Can I?" Marcus asked.

Joel looked at him. Then at the guitar. Then back.

"You play?"

"Some."

Joel picked up the guitar and handed it across. No ceremony. No questions about when Marcus had learned or where. Joel didn't ask questions when the answer didn't matter to the moment.

Marcus took it. The neck was warm from the fire. The strings were mismatched. The fishing-line ones had a different tension than the real ones, which gave the instrument a strange, uneven quality. Bright on the top, dull on the bottom. Not bad. Just specific.

He adjusted his left hand on the frets. He hadn't played guitar in his previous life, but Marcus Webb's body had coordination that the system had refined over eight years. Guitar was physical. His fingers could manage the patterns.

He played the first few notes and found the shape of it.

A fingerpicked melody. Simple, built on open strings and a descending bass line that moved down in steps. No chords, really. Just notes, one after another, with spaces between them that mattered as much as the sounds. The kind of melody that didn't try to impress. It just arrived and sat down and waited for you to feel something.

Marcus knew it from a screen in another life. A loading screen, a menu, a quiet moment between acts of violence and grief. The theme that played while Joel walked through a ruined world carrying a girl who trusted him.

Here, in this room, it was just music. A man playing a guitar by a fire while the people he loved sat nearby and the mountains outside held their breath.

The fire popped. A log shifted, sending a small fountain of sparks up the chimney.

Sarah looked up from her book. She didn't close it. She just looked, with her thumb still marking the page, and listened.

Joel set his mug down on the hearth. His hands went still, which for Joel was the equivalent of someone else leaning forward. He was listening with his whole body. His head tilted slightly, like he was trying to place the melody. Like he'd heard it somewhere and couldn't remember where.

He hadn't. He'd never heard it. Nobody in this world had. It was written for him, about him, in a place that didn't exist. A composer had sat at a desk and tried to capture what it felt like to lose everything and keep walking anyway. He'd found these notes. They'd traveled through a screen and into Marcus's memory and now out through fishing-line strings into a firelit room in Colorado, and Joel Miller was hearing his own theme song without knowing it.

Tommy was watching the fire. Not Marcus, not the guitar. The fire. His jaw was loose, his eyes half-closed. He looked ten years younger when he wasn't thinking about wiring.

Marcus played it through once. The melody took about two minutes. He let the last note hang, a natural harmonic on the open string that rang and thinned and faded into the sound of the fire.

Nobody spoke.

The coyote called again, farther away now. The generator hummed. A log settled in the grate. Small sounds. The kind the world makes when it isn't ending.

"What's that called?" Tommy asked.

"Doesn't have a name."

"Where'd you learn it?"

"Nowhere. Just something I know."

Joel was still looking at the guitar. His face was the Joel mask, the one that meant something was happening underneath that he wasn't going to show. Eight years of living with the man had taught Marcus to read the blank expression as clearly as a shout. Joel feeling things was Joel going quiet. Joel going quiet was Joel being honest.

"Play it again," Joel said.

Marcus did.

The second time through, he let himself slow down at the turn. The part where the bass line dropped and the feeling shifted from sadness to something that wasn't hope, exactly. More like endurance. The feeling of putting one foot in front of the other because someone behind you needed you to keep going.

Sarah closed her book. She leaned her head against the arm of the couch and pulled her knees up and watched the fire.

Joel's eyes were bright. He didn't wipe them. He didn't look away. He just sat on the hearth with the warmth at his back and the music in front of him and let it be what it was.

The melody ended. Marcus set the guitar down gently against the chair.

"That's a good one," Joel said. His voice was rough. "That's a real good one."

"Yeah."

"You play that for me again sometime."

"Sure."

Sarah reached over and took Marcus's hand. Her fingers were warm from the fire. She didn't say anything.

Tommy yawned. Stretched. Put his boots back on. "I'm gonna check the turbine before I turn in. Night."

"Night."

He left. The screen door closed. His boots crunched on gravel, then faded.

Joel stood. Picked up his mug. Looked at Marcus and Sarah on the couch, their hands together, the firelight on the wall behind them.

"Good night," Joel said.

"Night, Dad."

"Night, Joel."

He walked to the door. Stopped. Turned back.

"You know," he said. "I spent eight years buildin' a place where my daughter could be safe. Walls and gates and watchtowers and trainin' people to shoot straight." He paused. "That thing you just played. That's the part I forgot to build for."

He left before either of them could answer.

Marcus and Sarah sat by the dying fire. The lodge was empty. The compound was quiet. Three hundred and forty-nine people, sleeping behind walls that Joel built and lights that Tommy kept running and food that Lena grew and order that Sarah maintained. A town that shouldn't exist, in a world that shouldn't work, kept alive by a system nobody understood and people too stubborn to let it fall apart.

Sarah put her head on his shoulder. Her breathing slowed. She might have been falling asleep or just resting in the space between thoughts. Either way.

Seven thousand three hundred ninety-two points. Seven of eight enemy classes eliminated. A coalition forming across the valley. Beacons to hunt. A world to fix, one piece at a time.

But not tonight. Tonight was the quiet after. The part where nobody fought anything and the fire was warm and Sarah's hand was in his and a guitar with fishing-line strings leaned against a chair and hummed faintly in the cooling air.

He closed his eyes.

Tomorrow there would be work. There was always work.

But the fire was warm.
