# Chapter 60: No Catch

## — Ellie —

**July 2033. Crystal Falls, Colorado.**

Ellie woke up before sunrise because her body still thought walls meant schedules.

She lay still and listened.

No boots on a tile floor. No metal doors running through their lock cycles. No PA system giving the six-AM notice that sounded less like information and more like a reminder that someone owned the building you slept in.

Just wood settling. Wind against the glass. Somewhere downstairs, a pan hitting a stove.

She sat up.

The room was still there. Same room. She'd been doing this check every morning in Room 204, confirming the walls had stayed put, that nothing had been moved or rearranged in the night as a message. The room here was bigger. The ceiling had a crack running northeast from the window frame. The bookshelf was pine, empty except for one folded paper Noah had shoved on the middle shelf the night before.

She unfolded it.

Stick figures with bad proportions and oversized teeth. Labeled in block letters. One said ELLIE. One said JOEL, with a guitar as big as the body holding it. A lumpy blob at the bottom said CAPTAIN, which might have been a dog or might have been a potato with legs. Hard to say.

She snorted, folded it back, and set it on the shelf.

The lock on the door was on the inside. Just like they'd promised.

She unlocked it anyway, opened it a crack, and listened to the hallway. Quiet. Morning light from the stairwell. Coffee smell coming up, and bread, and something clean that took her a moment to identify. Cedar, maybe. Not the chemical smell of disinfectant trying to cover something worse underneath.

Just clean, the way a place smelled when someone had been keeping it that way for a long time.

She went back in, tucked the knife in her pocket, and went downstairs.

---

The kitchen looked the same as yesterday.

Same long table with the grain running east-west. Same dog under it, chin on his paws, eyes tracking the room without his head moving. Same old man at the stove with the expression of someone doing something that mattered.

Joel looked over. "Mornin'."

"You alive," Ellie said.

"Last I checked."

Sarah was at the counter, hair tied back, moving through breakfast like she'd done it ten thousand times. She probably had. Noah was on a stool with a piece of toast in one hand and a comic book in the other, technically reading at the table in the way people technically did things when they thought no one was paying attention.

"You sleep okay?" Sarah asked.

"Room didn't explode," Ellie said. She leaned against the doorframe. "So yeah."

"High bar."

"I have standards."

Noah looked up. "School after breakfast. You have to come."

"Do I."

"New-person rule. You meet everyone before they make up stories." He took a large bite of toast. "I made up three already. Two were good."

"What were the two good ones?"

"Bear fight and prison break."

"Those are both better than what actually happened."

Joel pushed a mug toward her without being asked. She picked it up and smelled it.

"Half coffee," he said. "Half milk. You're fourteen."

"That's oppression."

"You'll survive it."

She drank it. It was better than she expected, which was annoying.

Eggs appeared in front of her. Toast, apple slices, a square of something that turned out to be goat cheese. Nobody asked where she'd been or what she'd done to get here or what she was worth to the people she'd left behind. Nobody said anything about the Fireflies or Marlene or the scar on her forearm she hadn't mentioned yet.

Captain came out from under the table and pressed his nose against her knee. She gave him half a piece of toast without thinking about it.

"Don't tell Joel," she said.

"I can hear you," Joel said.

"Don't tell Joel," she said again, to the dog.

Captain took the toast and went back under the table.

---

By nine-thirty she'd done two full laps of the settlement's main path with Noah and built a mental map of everything that mattered.

Clinic on the north side, close to the residential cluster. Armory west of the main barricade, locked, militia access only. Primary gate had two watch positions. Secondary access near the greenhouse had one, and the angles were bad from the south if you were trying to cover the full approach. Tripwire line running through the riverbank blind spot below the fence.

She'd figured out all of this while Noah explained the marker color system, which buildings had water access, who made good food decisions and who made bad ones, and the complete history of a disagreement between Frank and Sarah about the size of Sarah's desk that apparently still came up at dinner sometimes.

"Blue means any route," Noah said, pointing at a stake. "Yellow means you check in first. Red slash means militia only, no exceptions, and if you cross it without clearance Frank gives you the speech about the perimeter."

"What's the speech?"

"Long. About trust and community infrastructure. You'll hear it eventually."

Ellie noted the gap between the two southern fence posts. Somebody had driven a stake there with a yellow marker, but the stake was leaning. Either it hadn't been checked in a few days or the ground was soft there.

"Who does the weekly maintenance walk?" she asked.

Noah blinked. "Donna. Tuesdays and Fridays."

"That stake over there is leaning."

He looked at it. "I'll tell her."

She watched him write it in the small notebook he kept in his back pocket. He had a system for the notebook that he'd explained at length, which apparently involved different ink colors for different categories. He was eleven.

She didn't know what to do with a kid who had a system for his notebook.

School was in the converted tool shed. Four desks, a chalkboard, maps covering the south wall. Crop rotation, water filtration, first aid basics, structural load calculations. Things a QZ kid never got because the QZ assumed you'd stay inside it until it or you stopped working.

Marta had been a middle school science teacher before the outbreak. It showed in the way she explained things, moving between the chalkboard and the desks without filler, not asking if anyone had questions when nobody had questions.

She introduced Ellie with one sentence.

"This is Ellie. She's new. She's not here to be interrogated."

The younger kids lost interest in twenty seconds. Ellie sat in the back row next to Noah and worked through a map-reading exercise while Noah argued with a nine-year-old about compass calibration with the intensity of someone who had personal stakes in the outcome.

There were seven kids in the morning session. Most of them somewhere between eight and twelve, a few older. They were loud and distracted and they'd clearly been in this room long enough that its particular smell, chalk and old wood and something that might have been pine pitch, had become background noise.

One of the older ones watched Ellie the whole morning. Sixteen, broad shoulders, arms usually crossed, a face that was doing something specific.

She knew that face. She'd used it. The face that was deciding where someone new fit on the chart of who was useful and who was a problem.

His name was Bryce. Noah had mentioned him on the marker walk.

She let him watch.

---

After class came work assignments. Frank handed Ellie a shelf plank and a piece of sandpaper and showed her the grain direction with one pass of his thumb.

"Long strokes," he said. "Not short. Not against."

She started sanding.

The workshop yard had a rhythm to it. People moving between tasks with the focused ease of people who'd been doing this long enough that the steps were automatic. Someone repairing a gate hinge with a mallet and a drift pin. Two kids hammering pegs into a joint frame. Noah had gone back to the water line. Sarah was across the yard with a woman named Helen, working through some kind of inventory count.

The afternoon had the feel of a place that knew what it was doing. Not urgent. Not waiting for the next disaster. Just working, the way places worked when the worst thing happening right now was a slightly leaning fence stake.

Ellie kept waiting for the thing that would break it.

Bryce was fitting wall brackets ten feet away. He'd been watching her since the workshop opened and at some point, she could tell by the shift in his posture, he made the decision she'd known was coming.

"You one of Marcus's special projects?" he asked.

Not subtle.

Ellie kept sanding. "What's that mean."

"People show up here sometimes. Get a room, get fed, get treated like they're important." He set a bracket down with a little more force than he needed to. "Then they leave or they cause problems and everybody acts surprised."

Noah was by the water barrel. Very still.

Ellie set the plank on the bench.

"You mad I'm eating your food?" she asked.

"I'm mad everyone's doing the welcome-home act for someone who showed up yesterday."

"So this is a heads-up."

"Yeah."

She looked at him for a moment. Then she shoved him in the chest.

Not hard. Hard enough.

Bryce shoved back. She caught him under the ribs with a short punch, they went into the dirt, all elbows and bad angles, and it lasted about twenty seconds before Noah yelled and Joel came around the corner of the main lodge at a pace that crossed forty feet like it was nothing.

He grabbed Ellie's shirt with one hand and Bryce's wrist with the other and separated them with the ease of someone who'd broken up exactly this kind of thing before.

"Enough," Joel said.

The yard went still.

Ellie tested the grip once, out of habit. Then stopped.

Bryce spat dirt. "She started it."

"Yeah," Ellie said. "I did."

Joel looked at her. Not angry. Just waiting.

"Why," he said. Not a question. A space he left open for an actual answer.

Ellie wiped her mouth with her sleeve. "Because this part I know."

The river sound came through.

Sarah stepped in. Her voice stayed even. "Bryce. Pump line and fence check with me." She looked at Ellie. "You. Wash up. Then Frank in the shop."

No speech. No public verdict.

Ellie looked at Joel. He wasn't asking her to argue. She looked at Frank, who was studying a wall bracket like it had gotten interesting.

"Fine," she said.

---

When the yard started moving again, Sarah fell in beside Joel for a few steps.

"She picked a familiar fight," Sarah said.

Joel's jaw moved once. "Yeah."

"That's not the worst sign."

"I know."

He watched Ellie cross toward the water pump. Her back was straight. She wasn't looking at anybody.

"She's not running," he said.

"No," Sarah said. "She's not."

---

By dinner the yard had settled into something like normal.

Bryce apologized for the "special projects" comment because Sarah asked him to say it plain and he did, no edge in it, just the words.

Ellie apologized for the shove because Joel asked her to say it plain and she did the same way.

Neither apology was complete yet and both of them knew it. But neither flinched through it either, and Ellie had expected at least one of them would.

After dinner she took a stack of comics Noah had found in the library annex. Savage Starlight, issues three through nine, water-damaged but readable. She read until the light in the room went from orange to grey, then set the books on the shelf and lay back.

The frame had a slight wobble when she shifted weight to the left side. Not bad. Just there, the way secondhand furniture was always there.

She woke up twice in the night. Both times she already had the knife before she knew where she was. Both times she lay back down and waited out her own heartbeat.

By the second time she'd accepted she wasn't going to sleep through the night in a new room for a while. That wasn't news. She hadn't slept through the night in Room 204 either.

You got used to it.

## — Joel —

**July 2033. Crystal Falls, Colorado.**

He found Frank in the workshop at ten-thirty. Frank was planing a board by lantern, long easy strokes, not in any hurry.

He looked up when Joel came in.

"Need help," Joel said.

"Tonight?"

"Tonight."

Frank set the plane down and waited. Joel had learned to appreciate that about him. He didn't ask a bunch of questions first.

"Her bed frame," Joel said. "The one in the room. It's fine. It's not right."

There was a difference between those two things. Joel had built enough furniture over twenty years to know it. You could build something structurally sound and still completely wrong for the person using it. Wrong reach. Wrong proportions. Right for a generic room. Wrong for a specific person.

The frame in Ellie's room was fine. Square, solid. Someone had put it together fast before she arrived, which was the right call for the timeline. But it had no shelf lip near the pillow for books. No underframe drawer for a knife and a flashlight and whatever else a kid kept within arm's reach when sleep was uncertain. No headboard brace to stop the rattle when someone woke up hard in the dark and sat up before they were awake enough to remember where they were.

Joel had slept in enough improvised beds to know which details mattered.

He described what he needed. Frank pulled a pencil from behind his ear, took a piece of brown paper off the supply rack, and sketched it out. Shelf lip along the headboard, low. Side rail drawer with a flush pull, nothing to catch on anything. Brace across the back.

Frank looked at the sketch. "Two hours."

"Yeah."

They pulled lumber from the side rack, cut the joints, worked mostly quiet. Frank understood quiet the way good workers did, you communicated through the work itself, through the choice of where to put the next cut, through the way the tool moved.

By midnight the frame was done. Clean joints. No extra decoration. The kind of piece that would last thirty years if you kept it dry.

They carried it upstairs together.

Ellie's door was cracked. No light. Joel listened at the gap.

Steady breathing.

They swapped the frames in five minutes, slow and careful, setting each piece down before picking up the next. One soft curse when Joel's knuckle caught the headboard brace. Frank went still. The breathing in the room didn't change.

Joel slid a small stack of sanded shelf boards beside the bookcase before they left. Future project. Good to have something to do with your hands when you didn't have the right words for a thing.

At the door he looked back once.

She was on her side. One hand under the pillow, where the knife probably was. Captain had moved to the foot of the bed at some point without Joel hearing him do it. The old dog had his own ideas about where he should sleep, and most of the time those ideas involved whoever in the house was sleeping the least well.

Joel pulled the door to. Not shut.

## — Ellie —

**Later that night.**

She woke up to the smell of cut pine.

She was already sitting up, knife in her hand. Listened. Heard nothing wrong. Just the settlement in its late-night rhythm, gate callout from the north, wind in the valley.

Then she registered the bed under her.

Different.

Same room. Different frame. No wobble when she shifted her weight. A shelf running along the headboard where there hadn't been one. A drawer under the side rail.

She pulled it open.

Empty. Just waiting.

She sat with her knees pulled up for a while. Looked at the shelf. At the empty space above her pillow. At the drawer still open beside her.

She didn't move anything yet.

A knock at the half-open door.

"You awake?" Sarah asked.

"Kinda."

Sarah leaned on the frame, not coming in. She had two mugs and the look of someone who'd decided what they wanted to say but hadn't locked in the words yet.

"Mint tea," she said. "If you want."

Ellie took one.

Sarah sat on the floor just inside the doorway. Back against the frame, feet flat on the floor. Not in the room exactly. At the edge of it, the way you sat when you understood something about not crowding a space.

They didn't say anything for a while. The tea was good. Ellie hadn't had mint tea in a long time. There'd been a woman in her building in Boston who grew dried mint in a window box until someone took the window box.

"No catch," Sarah said, eventually.

Ellie looked at her.

"You keep waiting for one," Sarah said. "I can see it."

Ellie looked down at the mug. "People don't just do things."

"You're right that it's not free," Sarah said. "Chores. Classes. Safety rules. If you start a fight, you scrub pans until your hands give you a speech." She shifted against the doorframe. "But the part you keep waiting for, the part where we decide you're only worth keeping if you perform right, that part isn't coming."

Ellie rubbed her thumb around the rim of the mug.

"Marlene said stuff like that," she said. "About purpose and sacrifice and what mattered."

Sarah nodded. "I'm not Marlene."

"Yeah. I noticed."

The settlement was quiet outside. A gate callout, far off. Captain's breathing from the foot of the bed.

"You don't have to trust all of us at once," Sarah said. "Pick one thing. One person. One routine. Start there."

Ellie looked at the drawer still sitting open beside her. At Noah's drawing on the regular bookcase, the one she hadn't moved yet.

"Noah asked me to help him with some old handheld game tomorrow," she said.

"He's been talking about it for three days."

"He's weird."

"He really is."

Ellie took another sip. Held the mug in both hands.

"Thanks," she said. "For the room."

Sarah stood. Took her empty mug. Left Ellie's.

"It's your room," she said. "Goodnight, kiddo."

The word landed harder than Ellie expected. It wasn't a big word. It wasn't a promise. Just a word people said when they meant something without saying the whole thing.

After Sarah left, Ellie sat for a few more minutes.

Then she got up, crossed to the regular bookcase, and moved Noah's drawing to the new headboard shelf. She set it against the wall where she'd see it from the pillow.

She went back to the bed. Picked up the knife. Put it in the drawer. Closed it.

Not because she trusted the whole town. Not yet.

Because she trusted one room. And because the drawer had fit the knife exactly right, which meant someone had thought about her specifically when they built it, and that was the kind of detail that was hard to fake.

That was enough for tonight.

The door stayed unlocked.
