# Chapter 42: The Signal

## — Sarah —

The road to Basalt Station hadn't been maintained since before the outbreak, and the forest was doing its best to take it back.

Aspens crowded the shoulders of Highway 82, their trunks close enough to brush the sides of the truck if Tommy hadn't trimmed them on fuel runs. The pavement buckled in three places where root systems had pushed through from below, and a rockslide near the old Snowmass turnoff forced them to detour through a gravel service road that hadn't seen grading in eight years. Helen drove. Sarah rode passenger with a map on her lap and a clipboard on the dashboard. Behind them, Keiko and Paul sat in the bed with rifles, watching the tree line out of professional habit rather than genuine worry. The valley had been clear for months.

Twelve miles. Thirty minutes in the old world. An hour and a half now, because the old world hadn't required driving around collapsed overpasses.

Sarah had been planning this visit for two weeks. The quarterly council meeting. Her own words, echoing back: "Exceptions either grow or get swallowed." She'd meant it. Crystal Falls was 347 people in a world that was getting worse. The Firefly packet had confirmed what she'd suspected. Every QZ east of the Rockies was failing or already gone. The infection was accelerating. The settlements that survived the next decade would be the ones that built connections, not walls.

Basalt Station was the obvious first contact. Sixty-three people in a cluster of buildings around the old Basalt Town Hall and an elementary school gymnasium. They'd been trading with Crystal Falls since late 2019. Canned goods and scavenged hardware heading south, fresh produce and antibiotics heading north. Professional. Cordial. Arms-length.

Sarah wanted to change the length.

"What's their leader's name again?" Helen asked, steering around a pothole that could have swallowed a tire.

"Kate Schultz. Former town administrator, pre-outbreak. She ran the emergency response when FEDRA pulled out of the region and just kept going."

"Competent?"

"Competent enough to keep sixty people alive for eight years without a dam or a system." Sarah caught herself on the last word. The system was Marcus's secret. The council knew he had resources. Helen tracked inventory and didn't ask where things came from, which was its own kind of competence. But Sarah still slipped sometimes. Old habits.

"Without a power source," she corrected. "Basalt runs on wood fires and a single solar array they pulled off a vacation home."

Helen nodded. "So they need us more than we need them."

"That's not how I'm going to frame it."

"I know. But it's worth knowing which side of the table has the leverage."

Sarah looked out the window. Aspens. More aspens. A creek running fast with snowmelt, crossing under the road through a culvert that was half-clogged with branches. Spring in the Rockies. Everything thawing, everything moving, everything waking up.

"I'm not going to Basalt to negotiate leverage," Sarah said. "I'm going because we need each other, and someone has to say it first. If I walk in there with a list of things we can offer and a list of things we want, Kate's going to see a trade deal. I want her to see a neighbor."

Helen didn't argue. She just drove.

---

Basalt Station looked like what it was. Survivors making do.

The town hall anchored the compound, its brick facade intact but the windows boarded with plywood and sheet metal. The gymnasium next door served as housing, its high ceiling divided into cubicles with hanging blankets and salvaged room dividers. A vegetable garden filled the old parking lot, rows of potatoes and onions and kale planted in soil mixed with compost and hope. A water collection system made of rain barrels and PVC pipe ran along the gym roof. Functional. Ugly. Working.

Kate Schultz met them at the gate, which was a school bus parked sideways across the road with a gap just wide enough for a vehicle to pass through. She was in her fifties, short grey hair, a flannel shirt rolled to the elbows, reading glasses hanging from a cord around her neck. The kind of woman who'd been organizing committees and managing budgets her entire life and had simply continued doing so when the world changed the budget items from road repair to not dying.

"Sarah Miller." Kate extended her hand. "I've been expecting this visit for about six months."

Sarah shook it. "You have?"

"A settlement your size doesn't send its community coordinator to pick up a trade shipment. You're here to talk about something bigger." Kate looked past Sarah at the truck, at Keiko and Paul climbing down with their rifles, at Helen with her clipboard. "Come inside. I've got coffee. Real coffee. Found a case of vacuum-sealed bags in a storage unit last fall."

"I haven't had real coffee in three years."

"Then today's your lucky day."

They sat in Kate's office, which was the town hall's old records room. Filing cabinets along one wall, a desk made from a door on two sawhorses, a window that looked out at the garden. Kate poured coffee from a stovetop percolator. It smelled like civilization.

"You want a coalition," Kate said, before Sarah had finished her first sip.

Sarah set the cup down. "I want a conversation about one."

"Same thing, different packaging." Kate sat in her chair and leaned back. She had the ease of a person who'd survived by being realistic rather than optimistic. "I've been watching Crystal Falls grow for two years. You've got power, medicine, organized defense. You've also got something we don't have, which is whatever Marcus does to produce supplies that don't come from anywhere visible."

Sarah kept her face neutral. "Marcus is resourceful."

"Marcus is impossible. But I didn't survive eight years by demanding explanations for things that keep me alive." Kate sipped her coffee. "What are you proposing?"

"Shared defense commitments. If Basalt is threatened, Crystal Falls responds. If Crystal Falls is threatened, Basalt responds. Joint clearing operations in the corridor between us. Shared intelligence on infection patterns and human threats. A messenger route, checked twice weekly."

"And in exchange?"

"The same thing. We're not offering tribute or demanding it. We're two settlements twelve miles apart who've been pretending the space between us doesn't matter. It does. An infected horde that hits Basalt is twelve miles from Crystal Falls. A bandit group that passes through your territory is heading toward ours."

Kate was quiet for a moment. She looked at the garden through the window. An older man was working the potato rows, moving slowly, bending with care.

"The last group that offered us 'shared defense' wanted twenty percent of our food and said they'd handle security," Kate said. "They lasted four months before their leader started calling himself Governor and shooting people who disagreed."

"That's not what this is."

"I know it's not. Because you showed up with a coordinator, a logistics officer, and two militia members who haven't pointed their rifles at anyone since they arrived. Not a single person in your delegation outranks me, and you haven't once mentioned Marcus by name in your pitch." Kate set her cup down. "You're careful. I appreciate careful."

"My father taught me that."

"Joel Miller?"

"You've heard of him."

"Everyone in the Roaring Fork Valley has heard of Joel Miller. The man who built your gate and screens every new arrival like they're applying for a security clearance." Kate smiled. The lines around her eyes deepened. "Tell you what. I'll bring it to my people tonight. Shared defense, joint clearing, messenger route. No food percentages, no governance changes, no flags. If they vote yes, and I think they will, we start with the messenger route and build from there."

Sarah picked up her coffee. It really was good coffee. "That's all I'm asking."

"One condition."

"Name it."

"I want Marcus to clear the infected cluster at the old Snowmass resort. It's been growing since last winter. We can't handle it, and we've lost two people to Runners that wandered down from the resort grounds."

"I'll have him out there within the week."

Kate nodded. She refilled Sarah's cup without asking.

They talked for another hour. Logistics, population, food production, medical needs. Kate's settlement had a midwife and a veterinarian who'd been learning surgery from a textbook. No real doctor. Sarah offered Dr. Reyes for a monthly clinic visit. Kate's eyes went bright at that. A doctor. A real one, who made house calls.

By the time Sarah left, the sun was past noon and the aspens were gold with afternoon light. Helen drove. Sarah held the clipboard against her chest and looked at the mountains.

Sixty-three people. That was a start. If Basalt joined Crystal Falls' network, they'd be at 410. A hundred and twenty from Glenwood Springs, seventy from Willow Creek, thirty from the Redstone group. If even half of them came into the coalition, the Settlement Index would cross 500.

Tier 5 for Marcus. Whatever that meant.

But that wasn't why she was doing this. She was doing it because Gene Pruitt had died in his sleep in February, and a community that only grew when strangers stumbled through the gate was a community waiting to shrink. You had to reach out. You had to build the connections before you needed them, because by the time you needed them it was too late.

She was doing it because her father had spent twenty years learning that lesson the hard way, and she'd learned it by watching him.

"Went well?" Helen asked.

"Went well. She wants Marcus to clear Snowmass."

"He'll like that."

"He'll love it. He gets to hit things and call it diplomacy."

## — Marcus —

The map showed something wrong.

Marcus had noticed it three days ago, the morning after the quarterly council meeting. He'd been checking the area scan out of habit, the daily review of contacts within his 25-mile range, when a cluster northeast of the dam caught his eye. Fourteen contacts grouped in a tight formation near the old Carbondale crossroads, roughly eighteen miles out. Nothing unusual about that. Runners congregated in buildings, especially as temperatures dropped. Clusters of ten to twenty were common.

What wasn't common was the movement pattern. Over three days, the cluster hadn't dispersed. Infected drifted. They wandered through buildings, followed sounds, spread outward from population centers as food sources dwindled. A cluster that stayed in one place for three days was a cluster that had a reason to stay.

By the fourth day, the cluster had grown to nineteen.

That was wrong. Infected didn't recruit. They didn't gather. They didn't grow unless new hosts wandered in and got bitten, and nobody was wandering near Carbondale in April.

Marcus left at dawn on Day 4, alone. Joel was running militia exercises with Keiko. Tommy was elbow-deep in the phase-three rebuild. Sarah was on the road to Basalt. Nobody to argue with about going solo, which was convenient.

He ran northeast through the valley, past the cleared zones, past the Crystal River lodge, past the hot springs where sulfur still leaked through cracked pipes. Eighteen miles in mountain terrain. Fast for a normal person, comfortable for him. His boots hit the old Highway 133 just as the sun cleared the eastern ridge.

Carbondale had been a small town. Ski resort supply hub, some ranches, a downtown strip with restaurants and art galleries. Population around six thousand before the outbreak. Now it was buildings and silence and infected.

Marcus slowed to a walk at the town limits and opened the system map.

Nineteen orange dots, tightly grouped, centered on a building two blocks south of the main intersection. He zoomed in. The building was large. Rectangular footprint, high roof. He cross-referenced with pre-outbreak memories. An art gallery? A community center?

A church. The dots were in a church.

The outer streets were empty. No wandering Runners, no Stalkers in alleys. Nothing between the town perimeter and the church. In any other nest, he'd have encountered stragglers on the approach, outliers who'd drifted from the main group. Here there were none. Every infected in the area had gone to the same building and stayed there.

That wasn't behavior. That was a summons.

Marcus circled the block. The church was a modest building with a peaked roof and a small bell tower. Wood siding, white paint flaking to grey, double doors at the front. All the windows were intact. Closed, some with curtains still drawn. No obvious entry points except the front doors.

He stood across the street and listened. Enhanced hearing. At this range he could pick up individual heartbeats, breathing patterns, the shuffle of feet on floorboards.

He heard the Runners. Nineteen of them, bunched together, their breathing a collective rasp. Shuffling in place. Not wandering, not bumping into walls, not doing the aimless circuits that Runners did in buildings. Standing. Waiting.

And underneath the breathing, something else. A low sound, almost subsonic, at the edge of what even Tier 4 hearing could detect. A rhythmic pulse. Not a heartbeat. Deeper, slower, like a vibration transmitted through the floor and walls of the building itself.

The spore count on his system display spiked. Not the ambient background level that existed everywhere. A directed concentration. The spores were thickest near the church and thinned with distance, a gradient pattern with the building at its center.

Something in that church was producing spores at a rate he'd never seen, and every infected in the area had walked toward it and stopped.

Marcus checked his knife. Checked the handgun. Drew both.

He went in through the front doors.

---

The nave was dark. Curtained windows filtered the morning light to a grey wash. Wooden pews in two columns, an aisle down the center, a raised platform at the far end where the altar had been. Hymnals still sat in the backs of pews. A bulletin board near the entrance still held a printed schedule. Sunday services at 9 and 11. Wednesday Bible study at 7. Potluck on the last Friday of every month.

The Runners stood in the pews.

Not sitting. Standing. In rows, facing forward, like a congregation waiting for a sermon that would never come. Nineteen of them. Men, women, a teenager in a soccer jersey. Their faces were the usual horror of Stage 1 infection. Fungal tendrils at the temples, eyes milky, mouths open. But their posture was wrong. Runners lurched. Runners twitched. These Runners stood with their weight balanced, heads slightly tilted, bodies oriented toward the front of the church with an attention that infected didn't have.

They were listening.

The sound was louder in here. Not sound, exactly. A vibration that Marcus felt in his teeth and his sternum, a deep rhythmic pulse that seemed to come from the walls and the floor simultaneously. The spore count on his display had gone red. The air was thick with it, a faint haze visible in the filtered light, drifting in slow currents toward the altar.

No. Not toward the altar. From it.

Marcus looked past the Runners to the front of the church.

It was behind the pulpit. Where the altar should have been, against the back wall, partially hidden by a curtain that had been pushed aside by growth.

An infected. Or what had been one. A human body fused to the wall, arms spread, legs absorbed into the baseboard. The fungus had consumed everything below the waist and most of the torso, replacing flesh with thick ropes of mycelium that extended into the drywall and the studs behind it, branching outward like tree roots. The head was intact but unrecognizable, encased in a mass of fungal tissue that bulged outward from the skull in overlapping plates, each plate pocked with small openings.

The openings were breathing. In rhythm with the vibration. Each exhale released a thin stream of spores that caught the grey light and drifted outward in a slow, deliberate pattern.

The system notification was immediate.

**[New Enemy Class Detected]**
**[Classification: Beacon]**
**[Stage: 5 — Stationary Emitter]**
**[Threat Level: Low (direct) / Extreme (indirect)]**
**[Behavior: Produces concentrated spore signal that attracts infected within a multi-mile radius. Does not attack. Functions as a territorial anchor.]**

**[Threat Diversity: 7/8]**

Stage 5. Not a fighter. An organizer. The fungus hadn't just been building bigger monsters. It had been building infrastructure.

The Runners turned.

Nineteen heads swiveled toward the door in unison. Not staggered, not one at a time. Together. The Beacon's rhythm shifted, a faster pulse, and the Runners oriented on Marcus like dogs responding to a whistle.

He moved.

The first three came down the center aisle. Marcus met them halfway. Knife through the temple of the first, handgun to the second's forehead, sidestep and blade across the third's throat and then up into the skull. Two seconds. Clean.

**[+39 Points]**
**[Streak: 3]**

The rest came in a wave. Not the disorganized stumbling of normal Runners. Coordinated. They funneled down the aisle in pairs, using the pews as barriers, pressing him from both sides simultaneously. The Beacon's pulse quickened again and the Runners moved faster, like the signal was feeding them urgency.

Marcus let training take over. He'd cleared hundreds of infected over eight years. Rooms, buildings, nests, corridors. His body knew the geometry of close-quarters combat the way a carpenter knew the grain of wood. Pivot, cut, step, cut. Don't retreat. Don't let them surround you. Keep the wall on one side and the knife in the other.

He killed four more in the pew rows, dropping them between the wooden benches. A woman in a park ranger's jacket. A man in coveralls. Two in civilian clothes so degraded they were more fungus than fabric. The handgun barked twice when spacing got tight. Headshots at contact range.

**[+68 Points]**
**[Streak: 7]**
**[Mixed Kill Environment: +12]**

The remaining twelve charged together. The Beacon's pulse hit a frequency Marcus could hear now, a low buzzing drone that vibrated the pews and set dust falling from the ceiling. The Runners were faster. Angrier. Moving with a purpose that regular Runners never had.

Marcus didn't slow down. He waded into them.

Knife and gun. Two weapons, twelve targets, a church nave that smelled like mildew and spores and old hymnals. He took a hit. A Runner grabbed his left forearm and bit down. The teeth skidded off enhanced skin. Tier 4 durability. The bite would have broken a normal person's arm. Marcus felt pressure and nothing else. He killed the biter with a thrust under the jaw and kept moving.

Thirty seconds. Twelve more dead.

**[+147 Points]**
**[Streak: 19]**
**[Nest Engagement Bonus: +28]**

The church was quiet. Nineteen Runners on the floor between the pews, in the aisle, slumped against the walls. Hymnals scattered. A potluck sign hanging crooked on its nail.

The Beacon pulsed on.

Marcus walked up the aisle to the front of the church. The fused body on the wall continued its slow exhalation, spores drifting from the openings in the fungal plates. Up close, he could see that the mycelium roots extended deep into the wall structure, probably into the foundation. The thing had been growing for months, maybe years, anchoring itself to the building like a tree to soil.

It didn't react to him. No head movement, no sound, no defensive posture. The system had classified its direct threat level as Low, and it was right. The Beacon couldn't fight. Couldn't move. Couldn't do anything except sit there and broadcast.

But it had pulled nineteen infected to a single point and held them there like a magnet holds iron filings. And the signal range, the multi-mile radius the system had mentioned, meant it could pull more. Could pull dozens. Could pull hundreds, given enough time and enough infected in the area.

Marcus drove the knife into the base of the skull where it met the fungal mass. The body shuddered. The pulse stopped. The spore streams from the plate openings slowed, sputtered, ceased.

**[Beacon Killed]**
**[+310 Points]**
**[First Kill Bonus: New Enemy Class — +200 Points]**
**[Threat Diversity: 7/8]**

**[Points: 4,275]**

**[Tier 5 Progress: 68%]**

Six percent gain from one engagement. The system weighted the Beacon kill heavily. Not because it was hard to kill, but because it was rare. Important. A category of threat that changed the calculus.

Marcus pulled the knife free and wiped it on a pew cushion. He stood in the empty church, breathing the thick air, and thought about what he'd just seen.

The fungus was building signal towers.

Not randomly. Not as a mutation. As a strategy. A Beacon established itself in a building, grew roots into the structure, and began broadcasting a spore signal that attracted infected to its location. The infected came. They stood. They waited. And when a threat appeared, the Beacon directed them like an air traffic controller, coordinating their movements through frequency changes in the pulse.

It was a hive. Not an insect hive with a queen, but a fungal network with relay nodes. The Beacon was the node. The Runners were the response team. And somewhere out there, other Beacons were probably doing the same thing in other buildings, other towns, other churches and schools and community centers.

Building an army that didn't know it was an army.

Marcus left the church. The morning was bright and cold outside. He stood on the street and breathed clean air and let his heart rate settle.

One enemy class left. The Rat King, if the games were any guide. A fused amalgamation of multiple infected bodies, decades old, found only in the deepest and most heavily infected locations. That was a problem for another day.

The immediate problem was closer.

If Beacons could form this close to Crystal Falls, eighteen miles from the dam, then the valley's safety margin was thinner than anyone thought. A Beacon established downwind of the settlement could draw every infected in a fifty-mile radius toward the dam. Not a raid. Not a wandering group. A horde.

He started running home.

## — Marcus —

Joel was cleaning rifles in the workshop when Marcus arrived. The door was open, spring air flowing through, oil and sawdust smells mixing. Noah's latest drawings were tacked to the wall above the workbench. Dogs, still. Dogs with wings, dogs with hats, a dog driving a truck. Captain sat under the bench, watching Joel's hands with the intensity of a creature who believed gun oil was a food group.

"You look like you found somethin'," Joel said without looking up.

"I found something."

Marcus sat on the bench across from Joel and told him. The cluster pattern on the map. The church. The Runners standing in rows. The Beacon on the wall, pulsing, drawing them in. The system classification. Stage 5.

Joel listened. He didn't interrupt. He set the rifle down and gave Marcus the full weight of his attention, which was considerable. Joel's attention was like a spotlight. Uncomfortable, thorough, impossible to hide from.

"How many of these things could be out there?" Joel asked when Marcus finished.

"No way to know. The map only covers twenty-five miles. Beyond that, I'm blind. The Firefly intel didn't mention anything like this, but the Fireflies aren't exactly running lab studies on infection evolution."

"Eighteen miles." Joel picked up the cleaning rod again. Ran it through the barrel with slow, even strokes. "That's inside our buffer zone."

"I know."

"If one sets up closer. Ten miles. Five. And it starts pullin' in every Runner between here and Aspen."

"Then we get a horde. Not ten or twenty. Hundreds."

Joel was quiet. The cleaning rod moved. Captain yawned under the bench.

"We've never fought hundreds," Joel said.

"No."

"The militia's good. Keiko's team is sharp. But they've drilled for raids. Ten, fifteen infected at a time. Maybe twenty. Not hundreds."

"I know."

Joel set the rod down. Looked at Marcus. "What do you want to do?"

"Expand the clearing radius. Push it past twenty miles in every direction. Find any Beacon that's forming and kill it before it matures. If we can keep them from establishing near the valley, we keep the horde from forming."

"That's a lot of ground."

"It is. I'll need Keiko's team for the extended runs. Three-day sweeps, rotating."

"And if we miss one?"

Marcus looked at the wall. Noah's drawings. Dogs in a row. A family in front of a house.

"Then we fight hundreds."

Joel nodded. Not agreement. Acknowledgment. The nod of a man who'd been surviving for eight years and knew that every solution was temporary and every threat was patient.

"I'll talk to Keiko. Set up a sweep rotation. Three teams, staggered. South, east, north." Joel stood. Collected the rifle parts. "Tommy needs to know about the signal range. If that thing can pull infected through the valley, the dam's perimeter plan needs updating."

"I'll brief him tonight."

"And Sarah?"

"She's at Basalt. Back tomorrow."

Joel paused at the door. "She'll want to know everything."

"She always does."

A ghost of a smile. Joel walked out.

...

Sarah returned the next evening with real coffee, a handshake agreement with Kate Schultz, and a request to clear the Snowmass resort.

Marcus told her about the Beacon over dinner. Noah was upstairs with Captain, drawing. Joel had taken Tommy through the perimeter implications an hour earlier. Tommy had said three words: "I need wire."

Sarah listened. She held her coffee mug with both hands, the one luxury she'd brought back from Basalt, and didn't speak until Marcus was done.

"So the fungus is networking," she said.

"Yeah."

"Building relay stations. Concentrating forces. Not randomly, but as a system."

"That's what it looks like."

Sarah set the mug down. "How long before the next one forms?"

"I don't know. The one I found had been growing for months, maybe longer. The mycelium roots were deep into the building's foundation. It takes time."

"But it could be happening right now. Outside your map range. In Glenwood, in Aspen, in a hundred buildings we've never checked."

"Yes."

Sarah was quiet. Through the wall, Joel's hand plane resumed its rhythm on wood. The turbine hummed under their feet.

"Then we need more eyes," Sarah said. "Not just our militia. Basalt's people, the Glenwood groups, anyone who patrols their own territory. A shared early warning network. If anyone sees infected congregating instead of dispersing, they report it."

"You just pitched Kate Schultz on a coalition this morning."

"And now I have a reason to make it urgent." She picked up her pencil. Started writing. The planning reflex, as automatic as breathing. "Monday council meeting. You brief everyone on the Beacon. I present the Basalt agreement and push for Glenwood and Willow Creek visits this month. Joel updates the perimeter plan. Tommy does whatever Tommy does with wire."

Marcus watched her work. The woman who'd asked for a room with an east-facing window and built an alliance around it instead.

"Kate's people make sixty-three," he said. "If they formally join the network."

"Four-ten total. And Glenwood has at least a hundred. Willow Creek, seventy."

"That puts us past five hundred."

Sarah looked up from her notes. She knew what five hundred meant. She'd read the Tier 5 requirements over his shoulder two nights ago.

"One problem at a time," she said. "First we make sure there isn't a Beacon growing five miles from the dam. Then we worry about progress bars."

She went back to her notes. Marcus finished his coffee. It was real coffee. It tasted like something from before the world ended, warm and bitter and ordinary.

Upstairs, Noah's heartbeat held steady. Sixty beats per minute. Captain beside him, slower. The sounds of a house at rest.

Outside, the string lights on the dam walkway clicked on. Tommy's timer, same time every evening. Three hundred and forty-seven people under those lights, living in the brief and fragile space between one threat and the next.

Four thousand two hundred and seventy-five points. Seven of eight enemy classes. Sixty-eight percent.

The system was patient. Marcus was learning to be.
