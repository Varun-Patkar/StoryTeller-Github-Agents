# Chapter 49: Thirteen Years

## — Marcus —

**September 2026. Crystal Falls, Colorado.**

Thirteen years since Outbreak Day.

Marcus stood on the dam walkway and watched the morning shift change below. Keiko's patrol team coming in through the south gate, rifles slung, laughing about something. The greenhouse crew heading out with wheelbarrows and a flatbed cart. Two kids chasing Captain between the storage buildings while Martha Cope yelled at them to slow down. The old dog couldn't run anymore, not really, but he tried. He was grey all over now, arthritic in the back legs, and still convinced he was the fastest thing in the valley.

Five hundred and twelve people. Helen Park had run the census three times because she didn't trust round numbers.

The system had noticed.

**[Settlement Population: 512]**
**[Tier 5 Requirement Updated: 500+ population ✓ (512/500)]**
**[Tier 5 Progress: 89%]**
**[Requirements: 8/8 enemy classes ✓ | 500+ population ✓ | [REDACTED]]**

Two of three. The third requirement stayed blank. Marcus had stopped guessing what it was sometime around Year 10. The system revealed things when it wanted to.

He closed the notification and leaned on the railing. The concrete was warm. Below, the river ran steady and clear, the same way it had every morning since Tommy first got the turbine spinning on Day 46. A hawk circled over the eastern ridge. The air smelled like pine and the compost heap behind the greenhouse, which Lena kept swearing she'd move further from the main path.

Thirteen years. Some mornings Marcus still expected to wake up to a phone alarm and traffic noise. Those mornings were getting rarer.

...

Noah was at the kitchen table when Marcus came in, eating oatmeal with dried apples and reading a book about animal tracking that Frank Garvey had found on a scavenging run in Carbondale.

He was eleven now. Tall for his age, with the quiet watchfulness of a kid who'd spent his first five years learning that silence kept you alive. He'd loosened up in the last two years. He laughed at dinner. He argued with the other kids about patrol techniques. He'd joined Keiko's junior training program on Saturdays, and he was good at it in the careful, patient way that suited him.

"Morning," Marcus said.

"Morning. Mom left your coffee on the counter."

Marcus picked up the tin mug. Still warm. Sarah had been up for at least an hour already.

"You're up early," Marcus said.

"Keiko's running a field exercise at seven. Track and identify."

"Track and identify what?"

"Whatever she puts out there. Last week it was raccoon prints mixed with Runner drag marks from the archive photos. Three kids couldn't tell the difference."

"Could you?"

Noah gave him a look.

"Yeah," Marcus said. "Forget I asked."

---

The federation council met quarterly, rotating between Crystal Falls, Basalt Station, Glenwood Springs, and Willow Creek. Today was Crystal Falls' turn. The meeting room was the old mess hall, expanded twice since the original build, with a proper table that seated twenty and a wall-mounted map Tommy had drawn on a sheet of plywood.

The map showed everything. Five years ago, Crystal Falls had been a single dot with three uncertain lines pointing to neighboring settlements. Now it showed eleven communities across a two-hundred-mile corridor from Aspen to Grand Junction, connected by patrol routes, messenger trails, radio relay stations, and shared clearing zones. The Western Colorado Federation. Sarah had named it. Nobody had come up with anything better.

Sarah ran the meeting. She sat at the head of the table with a leather journal, two pens, and an agenda printed on the community's hand-cranked press. Her hair was pulled back. She wore a flannel shirt that used to be Joel's and boots Donna had resoled twice. Twenty-five years old and the most competent person in a room full of people twice her age.

"Census update," Sarah said. "Crystal Falls proper: five hundred and twelve. Federation total across all eleven communities: two thousand, three hundred and forty-one. Up from one thousand, nine hundred and seven at last quarter's count."

Kate Schultz from Basalt tapped the table. "That's eighteen percent quarterly growth. We're going to hit resource strain if we don't expand the farmland agreements."

"Already on the agenda. Item four."

Diane Pryor from Glenwood Springs raised a hand. She'd spent three years refusing to join the coalition before finally signing on in 2024. Now she attended every meeting and took more notes than Sarah. "Beacon status?"

Tommy answered from his seat. "Last confirmed Beacon was February. Northeast of Aspen, close to the I-70 corridor. Marcus and the strike team cleared it. Fourteen months without a new detection in our territory."

"That doesn't mean they've stopped growing," Joel said from the back wall. He never sat at the table during federation meetings. Leaned against the doorframe with his arms crossed, listening to everything, participating only when security came up. Forty-two years old and still the most dangerous person in any room he walked into. "It means they're growin' outside our patrol range."

"Agreed," Sarah said. "Which is why item five is expanded long-range reconnaissance. But we'll get there."

The meeting ran for two hours. Marcus approved three Mall purchases during breaks in the discussion. Replacement solar cells for Willow Creek. Antibiotics for Basalt's clinic. A new radio for the Redstone relay station. Three hundred and forty-five points, barely a dent. The "supply cache" logistics fiction that Helen managed had evolved into an airtight system of backfilled requisition forms and plausible sourcing explanations. It worked because Helen made it work, and because nobody wanted to look too hard at the system that kept eleven communities fed and equipped.

Marcus at 11,847 points. Thirteen years of clearing runs, achievement chains, and passive multipliers stacking in the background. He earned faster than he could spend on anything below Tier 5.

After the meeting, people filed out in groups, talking about trade routes and farm yields and the things that made a federation run. Sarah caught Marcus's eye across the room and tilted her head toward the side door. Small gesture. Nobody else noticed.

He followed her.

---

## — Sarah —

She took him to the old powerhouse office. Tommy's original workspace from when the dam needed daily attention. Now the turbine ran itself and the office had become a storage room with a desk, a window overlooking the river, and a door that locked.

Sarah locked the door.

Marcus looked at her. "Everything okay?"

She sat on the edge of the desk. Looked at him the way she'd been looking at him since a stranger appeared on a highway outside Austin with bandages that came from nowhere and knowledge he shouldn't have had.

Thirteen years of data points.

"I've never asked you to explain the impossible things," she said. "You know that."

"I know."

"I'm asking now."

She watched his face. After all this time, she could read him without effort. The slight change in his breathing. The way he looked at a spot on the wall while his brain sorted through options. He was deciding how much to tell her. She'd seen that calculation a hundred times.

"All of it, Marcus. Not the version. Not the cache story. All of it."

He sat down on the floor across from her, back against the wall. He always did that when a conversation was going to take a while.

"You're going to think I'm crazy," he said.

"I watched you tear a machine gun off a military vehicle with your bare hands. We're past crazy."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't.

"I'm not from this world," he said. "Not the way you think."

"Okay."

"Before Outbreak Day, I lived a different life. No apocalypse, no infected, no system. I knew about this world because it was a story. A game. Something people played on screens. I knew your name. I knew Joel's name. I knew Tommy's name. I knew the date of the outbreak, the city where it started, and the highway where you were going to die."

The room was quiet except for the turbine hum through the concrete walls.

"You were going to die on that highway," Marcus said. "A FEDRA soldier was going to shoot you, and Joel was going to hold you and believe you were dead, and he was going to spend the next twenty years as a broken man who couldn't let anyone in because he'd lost the one person who mattered. That's the story I knew. That's the version where I didn't exist."

"And you came to change it."

"I woke up in a body that wasn't mine on the night the world ended. I didn't choose any of it. But I knew what was going to happen, and I knew I could stop it."

"So you ran to the highway."

"I ran to the highway."

Sarah looked at him. Thirteen years of data points, clicking into place. The supplies from thin air. The impossible knowledge of where infected would be. The way he'd known to head for Colorado before anyone mentioned it. The way he talked about the future like he'd already seen it. Her journal entry from Day 55, written in the firelight while Mia's crayon drawing dried on the kitchen wall: "He acts like he's playing a game he's already beaten."

Not a metaphor. A description.

"The system," she said. "The points. The Mall."

"It came with the body. I don't know who built it or why. It rewards me for killing infected and building things. It doesn't reward me for killing people. That's all I understand about the rules."

"And the meta-knowledge. You knew everything that was going to happen."

"I knew the original story. The one where I didn't exist. A lot of it doesn't apply anymore because I changed things. Joel was supposed to spend twenty years in the Boston QZ as a smuggler. Tommy was supposed to build Jackson on his own. You were supposed to be dead."

Sarah's voice was even. The same tone she used in council meetings when someone brought a problem she'd already worked through.

"What else do you know? What hasn't happened yet?"

Marcus was quiet for a moment. She waited. Patience was the first skill the apocalypse had taught her, and she was better at it than anyone.

"There's a girl," Marcus said. "In Boston. Her name is Ellie Williams. She's about seven years old right now. In about seven or eight years, she's going to be bitten by an infected, and she won't turn. She's immune. The only known immune person in the world."

Sarah didn't interrupt. She listened.

"A woman named Marlene, the Firefly leader, is going to find out about Ellie's immunity. And Marlene is going to decide that the only way to make a vaccine is to remove the Cordyceps growth from Ellie's brain. Which kills her. A fourteen-year-old girl, killed on an operating table because the adults in charge decided one life was worth less than a cure."

"In the original story."

"In the original story, Joel stopped them. He killed the surgeon. He killed Marlene. He saved Ellie. And then years later, the surgeon's daughter tracked Joel down and murdered him in front of his family."

The room was very still.

"I've been planning for this since before Ellie was born," Marcus said. "Every Firefly intelligence report, every trade contact in the eastern settlements, every radio intercept about Boston QZ politics. I know where she is. I know the timeline. And I know that in about seven years, I need to go get her before Marlene does something that can't be undone."

Sarah was quiet for a long time. She looked at the window. The river. The compound beyond it, where five hundred and twelve people were going about a Tuesday afternoon without any idea that the man who'd built their town had been carrying a dead girl's name in his head since before she was born.

When she spoke, her voice was the same as always. Practical. Clear. A little rough at the edges.

"You saved my life because you knew I was going to die."

"Yes."

"You built all of this, the dam, the town, the federation, because you knew what was coming and wanted to be ready."

"That's part of it. The other part is that people needed help and I had the tools to provide it."

"And Joel. You saved my dad from becoming whatever he was supposed to become."

"I tried. He did most of that work himself."

She looked at him. Thirteen years of questions, answered in ten minutes. And the answer was exactly what she'd known since a stranger knelt over her bleeding body on a highway in Austin and pulled bandages out of thin air.

"I don't care how you knew," Sarah said. "You knew I was going to die, and you saved me. You knew my dad was going to lose everything, and you gave him a reason to build instead. You knew this girl was going to be killed by people who think they're saving the world, and you're going to stop that too."

She slid off the desk.

"So what do we need?"

## — Marcus —

They talked for two hours.

Marcus laid out everything he had on the Boston QZ. The political situation between FEDRA and the Fireflies. Marlene's likely location. The timeline of Ellie's bite, around age 13 or 14, so 2032 or 2033. The route, roughly two thousand miles through hunter territory, dead zones, and whatever remained of FEDRA's eastern infrastructure. The option to extract Ellie before the bite or after.

Sarah asked questions. The kind that cut straight to the operational reality.

"How long is the trip?"

"Three to four weeks each way. Faster if I go alone. Slower with a team."

"You're not going alone."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Joel goes with you."

Marcus nodded. In the original story, Joel and Ellie's bond was forged on the road from Boston to Salt Lake City. Twenty years of grief and violence, melted by one stubborn kid. If Marcus was going to give Ellie a family, Joel needed to be part of the retrieval. Not just for Ellie's sake.

"Does Joel know any of this?" Sarah asked.

"No. You're the first person I've told."

"Why now?"

The answer was simpler than he wanted it to be. "Because I can't do the next part alone. Building the federation, clearing Beacons, handling FEDRA. I could manage all of that with the system and the council. But getting a child out of a city two thousand miles away and bringing her home safe? I need a partner for that. A real one. Not someone I'm keeping half the story from."

Sarah tapped her thumb against her index finger, one beat per second. She did that when she was building a plan in her head. Marcus had watched that gesture across a thousand council tables.

"Seven years," she said.

"Give or take."

"That's enough time. We build the intelligence network. We establish contacts in the eastern settlements. We map the route. We figure out the Firefly situation before it becomes a crisis. And when it's time, you and Joel go east and bring her home."

"And if the timeline shifts?"

"Then we adjust. That's what we do."

She reached across the desk and took his hand.

"One more thing."

"Yeah."

"In the original story. The one where I die. What happens to my dad?"

Marcus looked at her. She deserved the truth.

"He becomes the toughest, most broken man alive. He survives on violence and stubbornness for twenty years. He meets Ellie and she saves him, slowly, the way only a kid can save a man who's given up on everything. And then he dies protecting her. And it's the most unfair thing in the entire story."

Sarah was quiet. She looked at the desk. At the scratches in the wood that Tommy had made with his wrenches back when this room was an engineering workshop.

"He plays guitar at community dinners," she said. "He builds furniture for people. He trains the militia and complains about it and then stays late to help the ones who struggle. He's happy."

"I know."

"That's because of you."

"That's because of you. I just kept him alive long enough to get here."

She squeezed his hand. Stood up.

"We don't tell Joel about the other timeline. Not about the meta-knowledge. Not about me dying. He doesn't need that weight."

"Agreed."

"When it's time for the Boston trip, we tell him there's an immune girl who needs help and the Fireflies can't be trusted with her. That's true. He doesn't need to know the rest."

"He'll figure it out. Joel's sharper than he lets on."

"Joel is the smartest person in this town who pretends to be the simplest. But he doesn't need to know his daughter was supposed to die on a highway in Austin. Not now. Maybe not ever."

She unlocked the door. Paused with her hand on the handle.

"Marcus."

"Yeah."

"Thank you for telling me."

She left. The powerhouse office was quiet.

Marcus sat in the chair Sarah had been using and opened the system interface. He scrolled past the settlement management screen, past the federation map, past the Tier 5 progress bar.

He opened a new file in his personal log and titled it RETRIEVAL PLANNING.

Seven years. Two thousand miles. One girl.

He started writing.

## — Marcus —

Joel was on the front porch of the lodge that evening. Rocking chair, chamomile tea on the railing, Captain at his feet. The dog lifted his head when Marcus came up the steps, thumped his tail twice against the boards, and settled back down with a groan.

"Pull up a chair," Joel said.

Marcus sat in the second rocker. The porch had three now. Joel's, Sarah's, and the smaller one Noah had claimed. A spider had built a web in the corner of the porch railing, and Joel had left it alone because Lena said spiders ate mosquitoes and Joel believed anything Lena told him about the natural world.

"Good meeting today," Joel said.

"You barely talked."

"Didn't need to. Sarah had it handled."

They rocked. Captain snored. The sunset was doing its work on the ridgeline, turning the rocks from grey to orange. Down by the greenhouse, someone was laughing. A hammer rang from the carpentry shop where Frank was probably building another bookshelf for the library nobody had officially authorized but everyone used.

"She's somethin' else," Joel said. Not to Marcus. Just into the air.

"Yeah."

"I think about it sometimes. What she'd have been in the old world. College. Good soccer team. Business degree, maybe. She'd have run a company or a city council or somethin'." He sipped his tea. "Instead she runs a federation of eleven settlements in the apocalypse. I don't know if that's better or worse."

"It's different."

"Different," Joel agreed. He rocked for a while. "But she's good at it. That's the part that gets me. She's twenty-five years old and she's better at all of this than anyone I've ever seen. Better than the FEDRA administrators. Better than Marlene." He paused. "Better than you in some ways. Don't tell her I said that."

"She already knows."

Joel smiled. A small one. The kind that barely moved his face but reached his eyes.

"Yeah. She probably does."

They sat with the sunset. The valley went from orange to grey to blue. The lights came on across Crystal Falls, one building at a time, the way they had every evening for thirteen years. The schoolhouse. The clinic. The mess hall. The greenhouse. The lodge.

"Joel."

"Yeah."

"Do you ever think about what's out there? Beyond the patrol range. Beyond the federation."

Joel rubbed Captain's ear with his boot. The dog shifted and sighed.

"I think about it," he said. "I try not to."

"Why?"

"Because everything I need is right here. Sarah. Tommy. You, I guess, though don't let that go to your head." He set his mug on the railing. "It's the people out there who don't have this that I think about, when I let myself. The kids behind FEDRA walls. The settlements with no dam and no federation and no choice."

Marcus looked at him. Sometimes Joel said exactly the thing Marcus needed to hear, and had no idea he was doing it.

"Yeah," Marcus said. "Me too."

"That's a big thought for a Tuesday."

"It's Wednesday."

"It's whatever day Sarah's schedule says it is. I stopped trackin' the calendar in 2015."

They rocked in the chairs. Captain shifted in his sleep and put his chin on Joel's boot. The hawk from the morning was back, or a different one, circling the ridge in the last light.

Marcus thought about a seven-year-old girl in Boston who didn't know she was immune. Who didn't know a man in Colorado had been carrying her name in his head since before she was born. Who didn't know that in seven years, two men she'd never met were going to walk two thousand miles to find her.

He thought about Joel in a rocking chair, drinking chamomile tea because a woman who grew garlic had put it in his hands. Happy. Unbroken. Telling bad jokes about the calendar.

Seven years. Marcus had kept one secret for thirteen. He could carry another one for seven more.

The stars came out over Crystal Falls. The lights of the town burned steady against the dark. Marcus rocked in his chair and said nothing, and the river ran below the dam the way it always did, and somewhere a long way east, in a city Marcus had never visited in either of his lives, a girl named Ellie Williams was seven years old and alive and didn't know yet that she mattered more than anything.
