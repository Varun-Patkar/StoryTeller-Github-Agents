# Chapter 8: The Road West

## — Marcus —

Day six at the farmhouse. Or maybe day seven. He'd stopped counting precisely, which was either a sign that he was settling in or a sign that the days were blurring together. Both, probably.

The bird on the roof was back. Same bird, same grudge against sleep, same strangled attempt at a dawn call that sounded less like a rooster and more like a squeeze toy being stepped on. Marcus opened his eyes, stared at the Louis L'Amour books, and thought about throwing a shoe at the ceiling.

He checked the map instead.

**[LOCAL AREA MAP]**
**[Range: 15 miles]**
**[Infected detected: Cluster of 5, bearing 198° (SSW), distance 6.2 miles. Stationary.]**
**[Nearest additional: 18 miles south. Stationary.]**
**[Local perimeter (5 miles): Clear.]**

Five. A cluster of five, sitting six miles south-southwest. Stationary, which meant they were nesting. Holed up in a building somewhere, standing in the dark, twitching and waiting for a noise to chase. He'd been watching this cluster for two days. They hadn't moved. They weren't drifting toward the farmhouse. They were just there.

Which made them perfect.

Streak 5 required five kills in a single outing. He was at Streak 3. The Nest Breaker achievement required clearing five or more infected from a single location. This cluster was both. One trip, two achievements, and whatever point bonus the system decided to hand out.

He did the math while he laced his shoes. Five Runners at ten to twelve points each. Streak 5 bonus, probably fifty points. Nest Breaker, probably another fifty or more. Call it a hundred and fifty points on a good day. That would put him near seven hundred. Enough for a community water filtration system, or halfway to a generator, or a dozen other things that didn't matter yet but would matter enormously in three months.

Point farming. That's what it was. He was grinding mobs in a video game, except the mobs could tear his throat out and the respawn timer was permanent.

"Fun," he muttered, and went to find Joel.

...

Joel was in the kitchen. Already up. Already watching the window. The SIG on the counter. The same scene as every morning, like someone had photographed Day Three and set it on repeat.

"I'm going out," Marcus said. "South. There's a cluster I want to clear."

Joel turned from the window. "How many?"

"Five. Maybe six miles out."

"I'll get my boots."

"Not this time." Marcus held up a hand before Joel could argue. "Sarah's walking now. She's mobile. But she's not fast, and Tommy's one person. If something happens while we're both gone, he's got a twelve-year-old to protect and no backup."

Joel's jaw worked. The muscle under his ear flexed the way it did when he was chewing on something he didn't want to swallow. "You went with backup last time."

"Last time was three. This is five, and they're further out. Twelve-mile round trip. I'll be gone most of the day. You need to be here."

"Since when do you give the orders?"

"Since I'm the one who knows where they are."

Silence. The kitchen clock on the wall had stopped on Day One, its hands frozen at 11:47, and the stillness around it felt like everyone was waiting for it to start ticking again.

Tommy appeared in the hallway, leaning against the door frame with a cup of well water. He'd heard the whole thing. "He's right, Joel. One of us goes, one stays. And you're better with Sarah than I am if things go sideways here."

Joel looked at Tommy. Looked at Marcus. Looked at the window, where the gravel drive ran out to the main road and the sky was pale pink above the mesquite trees.

"Take the radio," Joel said. "The walkie from the barn. Channel three. You check in every hour."

"Deal."

"And if it's more than five, you come back. You don't play hero."

"When have I ever played hero?"

Joel just looked at him.

"Fair point," Marcus said.

He loaded the SIG, filled a water bottle, stuffed two MREs into the backpack he didn't need but used as cover because pulling things from thin air in front of Joel had become a recurring problem, and walked out the front door into a morning that smelled like cedar and dry grass and the faintest chemical tang from the south that nobody talked about anymore.

Tommy met him at the cattle gate. Handed him the walkie-talkie, a boxy Motorola that someone had left in the barn workshop. Batteries still good. Range, maybe ten miles on flat terrain.

"Be smart," Tommy said.

"Always."

"That's not what Joel says."

Marcus walked south.

## — Sarah —

The porch was her territory now.

Not officially. Nobody had assigned her the porch. But she'd claimed it the way cats claim windowsills, by simply being there so consistently that everyone accepted it as fact. The wooden chair with the cracked armrest, the view of the windmill and the fields, the line of mesquite trees going brown in the September heat. Hers.

She could walk. That was the news of the week. Four days ago she couldn't sit up without Joel hovering over her like she was made of wet paper. Now she could stand, walk to the bathroom, walk to the kitchen, walk to the porch. The hip hurt. It would always hurt, probably. The bullet had gone through muscle and chipped bone and the exit wound was a puckered scar the size of a quarter that pulled when she moved wrong. But she was upright. She was mobile. She was twelve years old and she'd survived being shot, which was more than most adults could say even before the world ended.

She was reading one of the Louis L'Amour westerns. Hondo. A man alone in Apache territory, making decisions with incomplete information and surviving on instinct and stubbornness. She liked it. The prose was simple. The hero didn't monologue. Things happened and the hero dealt with them and then other things happened.

Marcus had left at dawn. She'd been awake for it, lying on the couch with her eyes closed, listening to his footsteps in the kitchen, the murmur of his conversation with Joel, the front door opening and closing, the crunch of gravel fading south.

He'd gone to kill infected. Alone this time. Joel hadn't liked it. She could tell by the way he'd stood at the front window for twenty minutes after Marcus left, not moving, barely breathing, watching the gravel drive like he could keep someone safe just by staring hard enough.

Sarah set Hondo down on her knee and watched the windmill turn.

She'd been keeping a list. Not on paper. In her head. Her dad had taught her that without meaning to, just by being the kind of man who noticed everything and remembered it all. Watch people. Watch what they do when they think nobody's looking.

Marcus's list was getting long.

Item one: the backpack. Things came out of it that hadn't gone in. She'd watched him pack it twice. She knew what was inside. And then something new would appear, a bottle of alcohol, a roll of gauze, a box of ammunition, produced with a casual hand movement that was almost natural but not quite. Like a magician who was very good but not quite invisible.

Item two: the staring. He stared at empty air the way people stared at phones. Eyes tracking left to right and then down, reading, scrolling through something nobody else could see. Every time he came out of one of those episodes, he had information. How many infected were nearby. What direction they were coming from. How far away they were. Information he shouldn't have unless he could see something the rest of them couldn't.

Item three: the knowledge. He knew first aid. He knew where to find them on Outbreak Night. He knew which direction to run, which roads to avoid, where the military would be. He knew the infection was airborne in enclosed spaces before anyone had told him. He knew things a tech support guy from "somewhere up north" had no business knowing.

Item four, and this was the one that kept her up at night: he wasn't scared.

Everyone was scared. Joel was scared, even though he'd never admit it. She could see it in the way he slept with his boots on and the gun within reach and his body angled toward the door, always toward the door. Tommy was scared. He joked and cooked and did chores and kept his face easy, but his eyes moved too much and he flinched at sudden noises and once she'd seen him standing in the barn doorway at three in the morning, just standing there with the handgun, watching nothing.

Marcus wasn't scared. He was careful. He was alert. But underneath the caution there was something else, something she'd been trying to name for days and finally found a word for while reading Hondo this morning.

Confidence.

Not bravery. Not toughness. Confidence. The specific, settled calm of a person who knows how things turn out. Like someone replaying a level they've already cleared. Testing different strategies. Trying new approaches. But never really doubting the outcome.

"He acts like he's playing a game he's already won," she said to nobody. The windmill turned. A crow landed on the fence post at the end of the drive and tilted its head at her like it was waiting for her to elaborate.

She didn't. She picked up Hondo and went back to reading. But the thought sat in the back of her mind, patient and heavy, waiting for more data.

## — Marcus —

The cluster was in a feed store.

He found it at the edge of a crossroads town, if you could call it a town. A gas station, a feed store, a church with a white steeple, and a post office with an American flag still hanging limp on its pole. Population sign on the main road: 94. The kind of place where everyone knew everyone and the biggest event of the year was whose tractor broke down at the worst time.

The feed store was a metal building with a loading dock on one side and a customer entrance on the other. Both doors were closed. The windows were small and high, designed to keep heat out, which also kept sound in. No visible damage. No blood on the pavement. If you didn't know better, you'd think the store was just closed for the day.

Marcus knew better. The map showed five red dots inside, all on the ground floor, clustered near the back. Nesting.

He crouched behind the gas station's ice machine, fifty yards out, and checked his status.

**[Points: 513]**
**[Health: 91%]**

He'd bought ammo before leaving. Down from 527 to 513. Fourteen points for peace of mind.

The walkie crackled. "Check in," Joel's voice. Flat, clipped, the way Joel talked when he was doing something he didn't want to do, like sitting still while someone else did the dangerous thing.

Marcus pressed the button. "Six miles south. Found the cluster. Feed store at a crossroads. Five infected inside. Going in."

A pause. "Copy. Be careful."

"I'm always careful."

"Marcus."

"Yeah?"

"Come back."

He clipped the walkie to his belt and studied the building. Two entrances. The customer door was a single glass door, reinforced with a metal frame. The loading dock had a roll-up metal door, probably locked from inside. He could break the glass and go in through the front, but that would be loud. Five Runners in a confined space, all waking up at once, all funneling toward the same noise. Bad geometry.

He circled to the loading dock. The roll-up door had a chain lock on the outside, a padlock through a hasp. Bolt cutters from the Mall. Four points.

**[Purchase: Bolt Cutters — 4 Points]**
**[Remaining: 509]**

He snapped the padlock, set the cutters down, and gripped the bottom edge of the roll-up door. Lifted it six inches. Slowly. The metal groaned and he stopped.

Listened.

Nothing from inside. Not yet.

He lifted the door another foot. Enough to crouch under. The loading dock opened into a storage area. Pallets of feed bags stacked head-high, a forklift parked against the wall, and the smell. That smell. Wet rot and biology and the sweet, sick undertone of fungal growth that his body recognized now without the jolt of surprise. His stomach didn't clench the way it had on Outbreak Night. Progress, or just desensitization. The result was the same.

The proximity alert pulsed orange at the edge of his vision.

He slipped under the door and stood up. High ceiling, open rafters, fluorescent lights that would never turn on again. Dust drifted in the thin bar of daylight from the gap behind him. The air was warm and thick and very still.

Knife first. The SIG was in his waistband, loaded, safety off. But gunshots carried, and five shots meant five chances for something further out to come investigating. The knife was quieter. And at this point, he was getting familiar with how it felt when the blade went in.

That thought should have bothered him more than it did.

He moved between the pallet stacks toward the retail area. A doorway with a plastic strip curtain separated storage from the shop floor. Through the strips, he could see shelves of animal feed, rope, fence posts, work gloves. Normal things. Ranch supply inventory that nobody would ever buy again.

And the shapes. Four of them, visible from this angle. Standing near the back wall, swaying in the slow, arrhythmic pattern that Runners fell into when they had nothing to chase. The fifth was somewhere to the left, out of his line of sight.

He crouched and changed his angle. There. The fifth Runner was sitting on the floor behind the checkout counter, its back against the wall, legs stretched out, head rolling sideways. Its eyes were open but unfocused, staring at the ceiling. Its mouth moved in small, silent chewing motions, like it was eating something that wasn't there.

Five. Four in the back, one behind the counter. The four were clustered tight enough that engaging one would wake the others. The lone one behind the counter was separated by ten feet of open floor.

Take the lone one first. Quiet. Then the group. Fast.

He pushed through the strip curtain.

The plastic strips rattled. A small sound. Barely anything. But Runner hearing was good, and in the dead silence of the feed store, barely anything was loud.

The one behind the counter turned its head.

Marcus was already moving. Three steps across the shop floor, around the end of the counter, and the knife went into the side of its neck before it could stand. A wet sound. A shudder. Its hands came up and grabbed his forearm with the mechanical grip of a body running on fungus instead of thought, and then the grip loosened and it slumped sideways against the wall.

**[Runner Killed — 10 Points]**

He pulled the knife free and turned.

The other four were awake. Four heads snapped toward the sound. Four bodies straightening from their idle sway. For one stretched second they were all looking at him across thirty feet of feed store, and Marcus could see the faces. A woman in a sundress. A teenager in a high school football jersey. Two men in work clothes, Wranglers and boots and pearl-snap shirts stained dark with blood and fungal growth. Ranch workers. People who'd lived in this crossroads town population ninety-four and died in this crossroads town population zero.

They charged.

Runners didn't coordinate. They didn't flank or strategize or pick targets. They just ran at the nearest living thing and tried to kill it, and when four of them were doing that simultaneously in a space the size of a convenience store, strategy mattered a lot less than volume.

Marcus put the counter between himself and the first two. The woman hit the counter edge and stumbled, her momentum carrying her sideways into a spinning fall. The teenager tried to vault the counter, caught his foot on the cash register, and crashed down on Marcus's side in a tangle of limbs and jersey fabric.

Marcus stabbed him in the base of the skull. Quick. The same spot he'd learned on Outbreak Night.

**[Runner Killed — 10 Points]**

The woman was up. She came over the counter low and fast and Marcus kicked her in the chest, sending her backward into the two men who were rounding the counter's end. All three went down in a pile. Marcus vaulted the counter, landed on the other side, and drove the knife into the woman's temple before she could stand.

**[Runner Killed — 11 Points]**

The two men separated. One went left, one went right. The geometry of the aisle forced them apart, and the result looked almost like a deliberate flank.

Marcus pulled the SIG.

Two shots. One for each. The first caught the left one in the forehead and it dropped. The second hit the right one in the chest and it staggered but didn't fall, so Marcus shot it again in the head, and it folded.

The gunshots were enormous in the enclosed space. His ears rang. Shell casings tinkled on the concrete floor and rolled under the shelving.

Then nothing. Just the ringing, and the dust, and the five shapes on the floor that had been people last week.

**[Runner Killed — 12 Points]**
**[Runner Killed — 10 Points]**
**[ACHIEVEMENT: Streak 5 — Kill 5 infected in a single outing. Reward: 50 Points]**
**[ACHIEVEMENT: Nest Breaker — Clear an infected nest (5+ in one location). Reward: 75 Points + Map Upgrade: Range extended to 25 miles]**
**[Total Earned This Outing: 178 Points]**
**[Current Points: 687]**

The notifications stacked up like receipts. Marcus read them standing in a feed store in a town of ninety-four people, surrounded by five bodies, his hands shaking, his ears ringing, his knife wet.

A hundred and seventy-eight points. The streak bonus and the nest bonus stacked. And a map upgrade, range extending from fifteen miles to twenty-five. That was the real prize. Twenty-five miles of awareness in every direction. He'd see trouble coming from a full day's walk away.

He wiped the knife on a feed sack and sheathed it. His hands were still shaking. The adrenaline wanted out and there was nowhere for it to go, so it sat in his muscles and trembled.

Six hundred and eighty-seven points. A small fortune. A water filtration system was 300. A generator was 150. He could buy both and still have over 200 left for supplies.

But not yet. Not here. Later, when they had a place worth building.

He pressed the walkie button. "Five down. All clear. Heading back."

Joel's voice came through after a two-second delay. "Copy. Sarah made lunch."

Marcus stepped over the bodies and walked out into the sunlight. The American flag on the post office hung limp in the midday heat. A cat watched him from the church steps, orange tabby, completely unbothered by anything that had happened in the last week. He nodded at it, because what else do you do when a cat judges you, and started walking north.

## — Joel —

The smoke had changed.

Joel had been watching the southern horizon for six days. The first smoke was black and oily, burning buildings and overturned cars. Then grey, wider, the ash-and-debris haze of a city dying. Then it cleared for a day, and Joel had let himself think it was over.

It wasn't over.

The new smoke was white at the base and brown at the top, climbing in a column that flattened at altitude into a shape Joel had only seen in history book photographs. A mushroom. Not as big as the ones from Hiroshima, not nuclear, but big enough. Big enough to see from thirty miles away. Big enough to mean that whatever was left of Austin wasn't left anymore.

Tommy stood next to him on the front porch. Neither of them said anything for a long time.

"Thermobaric," Tommy said finally. "Not nuclear. MOAB or something like it. Military's using the big ordnance."

"On their own cities."

"On what used to be their own cities." Tommy's voice was flat. Not angry. Just facts, the way Tommy delivered facts, direct and clear, without the weight Joel would have added. Tommy had been military. He understood the math of acceptable losses. He didn't like it, but he understood it.

Joel watched the mushroom cloud climb. Thought about Sarah's school. The soccer field where she practiced Wednesday afternoons, the goal posts that leaned slightly because the maintenance guy never fixed them. The stand of oak trees along the jogging trail. The coffee shop on South Congress where the barista knew his order.

All of it was under that cloud.

"There's nothin' to go back to," Joel said.

"No."

"Even if we wanted to."

"No."

Sarah was in the doorway behind them. Joel heard her breathing, the slight hitch on the inhale that the wound gave her. He didn't turn around. He didn't want her to see his face right now, because his face was doing something he couldn't control, and Joel Miller always controlled his face.

"Is that Austin?" Sarah asked.

"Yeah, baby. That's Austin."

She was quiet. He heard the soft shuffle of her feet on the floorboards as she came closer. Her hand found his arm. Small fingers, strong grip. She leaned against him and they watched the mushroom cloud, the three of them on a stranger's porch, the last of the old world rising into the sky.

...

Marcus came back around two in the afternoon. He walked up the gravel drive with the backpack over one shoulder and the knife on his belt and that look on his face Joel had started to recognize. The post-combat look. The one that sat between relief and something quieter.

"Five," Marcus said. "Feed store at a crossroads, six miles south. All Runners. All cleared."

"You hurt?"

"No."

Joel looked at him. No visible injuries. Some blood on his jeans, already drying brown. Hands steady. Breathing normal.

"Good," Joel said.

...

That evening, they sat at the kitchen table. The last of the propane hissed under a pot of canned soup that Tommy had been rationing for two days. Tommy sliced bread from a loaf Marcus had pulled from the backpack, which Tommy no longer questioned out loud and Joel never stopped noticing. Sarah sat at the head of the table with her leg stretched out on a second chair, comfortable, present, alive. The mushroom cloud on the southern horizon had flattened into a wide brown ceiling that ate the sunset.

"We need to talk about what's next," Marcus said.

The table went quiet. Not tense. The other kind. The quiet of four people who'd all been thinking the same thing and nobody wanted to say it first.

"This place has been good to us," Marcus continued. "But it's temporary. The infected are spreading out from the cities. I cleared five today, six miles south. Last week it was three, seven miles southeast. The distance is shrinking. Eventually we'll be clearing them from the yard."

Joel set his spoon down. "What are you proposin'?"

"We go north. Northwest, specifically. Get out of Texas. Head for the mountains. Colorado, maybe Wyoming. Find a location with natural defenses. Elevation, water, something we can fortify."

"That's a thousand miles," Joel said.

"About eight hundred, if we angle through New Mexico."

"On foot. With a twelve-year-old who got shot a week ago."

"She's walking. She's getting stronger every day. We don't have to sprint. We travel slow, rest when we need to, avoid the highways. Back roads. Farm roads. Fifteen miles a day if we're careful."

Joel looked at Sarah. Sarah looked back with an expression that said "I'm sitting right here" but didn't say anything.

Tommy leaned back in his chair. "He's got a point, Joel. We can't stay. You saw the sky today. Austin's gone. The QZs are gonna be worse. Packed in with thousands of people, disease, rationing, martial law. I've seen military operations like this. The containment strategy is collapse. They'll bomb every city that gets overrun and the survivors will scatter into the countryside. In three months, this farmhouse will be in somebody's path."

"I don't like movin' Sarah before she's ready."

"I'm ready," Sarah said.

Joel turned to her. The dad look.

"I can walk," she said. "It hurts, but I can walk. And I'd rather walk toward something than sit here waiting for something to walk toward us."

"We don't even know what we're walkin' toward."

"Colorado," Marcus said. "I know it sounds random. But think about it. The Rockies are natural defenses. Low population density in the mountains. Hydroelectric dams for power. Rivers for water. Game for hunting. The infection will burn through the cities and the plains, but mountains hold. Geography does half the work for you."

"You've thought about this," Tommy said.

"I've thought about nothing else for six days."

Joel was quiet. He stirred his soup without eating it, the spoon moving in slow circles, his eyes on the table. Joel thinking was a physical process. You could watch him run through options behind his forehead, testing each one, discarding it, trying the next.

"How do we carry enough supplies?" Joel asked. Not a no. A question. Questions meant he was considering.

"I can handle supplies," Marcus said. "Food, water purification, medicine. I've got access to more than what's in this backpack." He said it carefully, watching Joel's face, offering the truth without the explanation. Joel's jaw tightened but he didn't push.

"Weapons?"

"We have two handguns and ammo. I can get more if we need it. But avoiding fights is better than winning them. We stay quiet, stay off the main roads, move at a pace Sarah's comfortable with."

"And when we get to Colorado?" Joel asked. "Then what?"

"Then we find a place and we build something. Walls, water, electricity. Something that lasts."

The kitchen was quiet except for the propane hissing and the wind on the plywood patches. The mushroom cloud had gone dark with the sunset, just a stain on the sky now, a bruise that would take days to fade.

Tommy nodded slowly. "I'm in. I'd rather be moving toward something than sitting still."

Joel looked at his daughter. Sarah looked back.

"It's not home," Sarah said. "But neither is this. Home is just wherever we decide it is. Right?"

Joel's mouth did the twitch. The almost-smile. The thing his face did when Sarah said something that landed somewhere deep.

"Alright," Joel said. "Colorado. We leave in the mornin'."

## — Marcus —

He packed in the dark.

Not because he needed to. Because he wanted a few minutes alone with the system before the last morning at the farmhouse became the first morning on the road.

**[Status]**
**[Name: Marcus Webb]**
**[Age: 24]**
**[Points: 687]**
**[Health: 92%]**
**[Attributes: Strength 6, Agility 5, Constitution 6, Perception 5, Intelligence 7, Willpower 11]**
**[Skills: None]**
**[Traits: Guardian — +15% healing effectiveness]**
**[Perks: Efficient Provider — 10% discount on consumables]**
**[Achievements: First Blood, Guardian Angel, Shelter Seeker, Streak 3, Streak 5, Nest Breaker]**
**[Map Range: 25 miles]**
**[Evaluation: Ready to move. The farmhouse was a rest stop, not a destination.]**

"Yeah," he said to the empty room. "I know."

He bought supplies for the road. Water purification tablets, twenty points. Twelve MREs, thirty points with the Efficient Provider discount. A second first aid kit, fifteen points. A compass, because he couldn't keep pretending to navigate by the sun forever, four points. A roll of duct tape, because duct tape was the answer to every question the apocalypse hadn't thought of, two points.

**[Remaining Points: 616]**

Six hundred and sixteen points. Not enough for a generator or a filtration system, but enough to keep four people fed and healthy for weeks on the road. And the points would keep coming. Every cluster of infected between here and Colorado was a deposit waiting to be collected.

He sat on the floor and leaned against the wall. The Louis L'Amour westerns watched him from the shelf. Cowboys and ranchers and men who built things in hostile territory. He wondered if Louis L'Amour had any idea how practical his books would become.

Through the wall, he could hear Joel and Sarah talking in the living room. Low voices, the murmur of a father and daughter planning tomorrow. Sarah's laugh, small and brief, interrupted by the hitch in her breathing. Joel's voice, steady, the anchor it had always been and always would be.

In the game, Joel had spent twenty years believing Sarah was dead. Twenty years of grief that turned him into something hard and hollow and dangerous. Twenty years that ended with finding Ellie and slowly, painfully, learning to love someone again, only to nearly lose her too, choosing violence over sacrifice because he couldn't bury another daughter.

That Joel didn't exist anymore. This Joel, the one talking to his living daughter through a farmhouse wall, would become something different. Better, maybe. Softer in the places that mattered. Or maybe harder in new ways, because now he had something to protect and Joel with something to protect was the most dangerous thing in any version of this world.

Marcus didn't know which. The timeline had broken on Outbreak Night when a stranger's hands pressed hemostatic gauze into a bullet wound on a highway overpass, and everything after that was unwritten.

Twenty years. That's what was coming. Twenty years of infection spreading, societies collapsing, factions forming, people dying in ways that could be prevented if someone knew they were coming.

He knew. The Clickers in the Boston museum. Henry and Sam in Pittsburgh. Tess at the State House. Riley in the mall. The surgeon in Salt Lake City. The golf club in Jackson.

Every death, every betrayal, every desperate choice. A walkthrough of the future laid out in his head, complete and terrifying and his to rewrite.

Not all of it. He couldn't save everyone. He was one person with a shopping menu and a knife and a body that got tired after walking six miles. The system was useful but it wasn't unlimited. The world was bigger than one man's memory of two video games and a TV show.

But he could save some of them. He could build a place where the people who mattered had a chance. Sarah. Joel. Tommy. And eventually, if he played it right, a girl named Ellie who didn't know she was immune yet. A woman named Abby who hadn't lost her father yet. A pair of brothers in Pittsburgh who deserved better than a hotel room and a single bullet.

Not all of it. But enough. Enough was worth walking eight hundred miles for.

The bird on the roof let out one preliminary squawk, testing its range for the morning performance. Marcus looked at the window. The eastern sky was barely lighter than the rest, a thin grey line at the bottom of the dark.

Last morning at the farmhouse. First morning of something else.

He closed the system and sat in the quiet. The crickets were loud. The windmill turned in the dark, its slow creak marking time that nobody was counting.

Through the wall, Joel's voice. Telling Sarah about Colorado.

"You ever seen snow, baby?"

"On TV."

"It's somethin'. Cold as hell, but it's somethin'."

Sarah laughed. The small one. The one that cost her a hitch in her breathing and was worth it anyway.

Marcus closed his eyes and listened to Joel Miller describe snow to his daughter, and thought about all the things he was going to build, and all the people he was going to save, and all the years of blood and impossible choices that were coming whether he was ready or not.

He wasn't ready. Not really. Not for twenty years of it.

But close enough. Close enough would have to do.
