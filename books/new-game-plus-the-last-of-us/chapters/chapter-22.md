# Chapter 22: Infrastructure

## — Marcus —

Day forty-eight.

Marcus started the morning with a list. Not a mental one. An actual list, written on the back of a feed store receipt he'd found in the glove box of the F-250.

_Solar panels. Backup power when dam needs maintenance._
_Water filtration. River water is clean but not clean enough._
_Seeds. Winter wheat, potatoes, whatever grows at 9,000 feet._
_Farming tools. Shovels, hoes, wheelbarrow._

He stared at the list. Then he stared at his point total.

**[Points: 615]**

Not enough for everything. Not even close if he went top-shelf. But the Mall had options at every price point, and Marcus had learned weeks ago that "good enough" beat "perfect" when perfect meant waiting another week.

The water came first. He'd been boiling river water for drinking since they arrived, and boiling took propane, and propane took points. A gravity-fed ceramic filtration system, the kind that backcountry hikers paid two hundred dollars for before the world ended, cost him 85 points in the Mall. It materialized behind the garage like every purchase did: a large blue barrel with a ceramic filter element, a spigot, and a hand pump for filling.

**[Purchase: Gravity Water Filter System — 85 points]**
**[Points: 530]**

He carried it to the residence kitchen and set it up on the counter. Fill from the top, clean water from the spigot. No more boiling. No more wasting propane on something a river and a ceramic filter could handle.

Next, the farming supplies. Seeds, hand tools, a thick book on high-altitude agriculture. The book alone weighed three pounds. The total came to 45 points.

**[Purchase: Farming Starter Kit — 45 points]**
**[Points: 485]**

He left the supplies on the porch for Sarah. She'd know what to do with them. She'd been talking about food production since Abilene, running the math on how many MREs the community would burn through once it grew past a dozen people. The answer was "too many" and they both knew it.

The solar panels were last. Not because they needed more power. Tommy's turbine was solid. But Marcus had seen enough "what if" scenarios play out in his head to know that backup systems saved lives. A tree falls across the penstock. Ice blocks the intake gate. A bearing seizes and Tommy needs three days to fix it. Any of those, and 168 kilowatts drops to zero.

Four 250-watt panels. A charge controller. A battery bank. The total came to 228 points, mounting hardware included, and Marcus watched the number drop with the particular discomfort of a man who'd learned to think of points as the distance between alive and dead.

**[Purchase: Solar Panel Kit (4x 250W) + Mounting + Battery Bank — 228 points]**
**[Points: 257]**

The panels appeared stacked against the garage wall. Monocrystalline, deep blue-black, heavier than they looked. Marcus dragged them into the open and yelled for Ray.

...

Ray Haywood limped out of the maintenance building with a coffee cup in one hand and a look of professional interest on his face. He'd been at the compound for two days and had already rewired the residence's branch circuits with ten-gauge wire that Marcus bought at his recommendation. Tommy's original twelve-gauge runs were "not going to burn the house down today," according to Ray, "but give it a month of continuous load and I wouldn't sleep in the building."

"Solar?" Ray said, looking at the panels.

"Backup system. If the turbine goes down, I want us to have lights and the water pump at minimum."

Ray set down his coffee and crouched beside the panels, running his hand along the junction box on the back. "These are good. Monocrystalline, probably 20% efficiency. Four of them gives you a kilowatt in full sun. Won't run the whole compound, but you can keep a fridge and some lights going." He tapped the charge controller. "MPPT controller, too. Where the hell did you get these?"

Marcus gave him the look. The one that said don't ask.

Ray had already learned not to push. In two days, he'd seen enough supplies appear from nowhere to understand that Marcus operated on different rules. Lena had asked once, carefully, and Marcus had said "I have a source" with the kind of finality that closed the conversation.

"South-facing roof of the garage?" Ray said, already planning. "Angle's about thirty-five degrees. Not perfect for this latitude in winter, but it'll work."

"Sounds right."

"I'll need sealant for the roof penetrations."

"Give me a list."

Ray gave him a list. Marcus bought it for 15 points and tried not to think about how fast the number was dropping.

**[Points: 242]**

They spent the rest of the morning on the garage roof. Ray did the electrical work, splicing panel wires into the charge controller with the confidence of a man who'd done this a thousand times before the world decided electricians were optional. Marcus handled the physical labor. Lifting panels, drilling anchor bolts, hauling the battery bank up a ladder and through a window into the garage's second-floor storage room.

Tommy came by at noon with sandwiches and stayed to help run conduit from the battery bank to the main panel. The two electricians, one military-trained and one commercial, argued about wire routing for fifteen minutes while Marcus sat on the roof and ate a sandwich and watched the valley.

It was beautiful up here. He kept forgetting that. The Crystal River ran silver-white below the dam. Pine trees climbed the ridges on both sides, still green even as October thinned the air and brought frost to the mornings. The mountains behind were gray granite capped with snow.

If you didn't know about the Cordyceps, about the cities burning, about the millions dead, you'd think this was a nice place to live.

Marcus knew about all of it. He ate his sandwich anyway.

## — Joel —

Joel spent the morning doing what Joel did best: making things harder to get into.

The compound had five buildings and a dam, arranged in a rough L-shape along the river. The access road came in from the east. One road, one entrance, natural bottleneck. That was good. The river flanked the south side. Also good. The north and west were forested ridgelines, steep but not impassable.

Joel walked the perimeter with the shotgun on his shoulder and a mental checklist that got longer with every step.

The tree line on the north ridge was too close. Fifty yards from the maintenance building to the first pines. That was knife-fighting distance for anyone who knew what they were doing. Joel marked three spots where he wanted the underbrush cleared back to a hundred yards. Not the trees themselves. Just the brush, the deadfall, anything that could hide a person.

The west approach had a game trail that came down from the ridge through a draw. Natural avenue of approach. Joel piled rocks across it at the narrowest point. Not a wall. Just an obstacle. Anything coming through would make noise on loose stone.

He spent an hour at the valley entrance, studying the road. Sarah had been doing overwatch from the boulder every morning, and it was a decent position, but it had blind spots. The curve in the road a quarter mile out meant anyone approaching from the south was invisible until they rounded the bend. Joel wanted an observation post further out. A tree stand with elevation and cover.

He mentioned it to Tommy over lunch.

"A tree stand," Tommy said. "Like for hunting?"

"Like for seeing who's coming before they see us."

"Joel, we've had exactly one group of visitors and they were a family with a six-year-old."

"And the next group might not be."

Tommy chewed his sandwich. "You're not wrong. But we've got a lot going on. Marcus has got Ray on the solar panels, I need to finish the wiring in the outbuildings, and someone needs to start thinking about food that isn't from Marcus's magic shopping cart."

"I'll build it myself."

"With what lumber?"

"I'll cut it myself."

Tommy looked at his brother. Joel's jaw had that set. The one that meant the conversation was over and the tree stand was happening whether Tommy helped or not.

"I'll get you a handsaw," Tommy said.

Joel nodded and went back outside. He picked a pine at the edge of the access road, a big ponderosa about forty feet tall with thick branches starting at twelve feet. Good sight lines east and south. He'd have the stand built before sundown. Wouldn't be pretty. Joel's carpentry was contractor-grade, not furniture-grade. But it would hold weight and give whoever sat in it a clear view of the road for a half mile.

A squirrel watched him from the next tree over, chattering. Joel ignored it.

He started cutting lumber.

## — Sarah —

Day forty-nine. The farming supplies arrived.

Sarah came out of the residence at dawn and found six bags of seed, a stack of hand tools, and a book titled _High-Altitude Farming: A Practical Guide for Mountain Agriculture_ sitting on the porch like a care package from a world that didn't exist anymore.

She picked up the book and flipped through it. Chapters on soil pH, frost dates, season extension, root cellars. A chart showing what grew above 8,000 feet: potatoes, kale, carrots, turnips, winter wheat, hardy lettuce varieties. Nothing tropical. Nothing that needed a long growing season. But food. Real food, from the ground.

Sarah hadn't thought much about farming before the outbreak. She was twelve. Thirteen now, technically, though nobody had celebrated the birthday and she hadn't reminded them. Her agricultural knowledge consisted of knowing that potatoes grew underground and that Joel killed every houseplant he'd ever owned.

But she'd been running the numbers since they arrived at the dam. The Mall could provide food indefinitely, but every MRE cost points, and points came from killing infected, and there were only so many infected within walking distance. A hundred people eating three meals a day was three hundred meals. At two points per MRE, six hundred points a day just for food. Marcus couldn't farm enough infected to cover that. Not when he was also buying tools, panels, wire, and medicine.

They needed to grow their own food. The math made the argument.

She found a patch of ground behind the residence. South-facing, relatively flat, protected from wind by the garage on one side and the ridge on the other. The soil was rocky, thin, mountain soil. Not great. But the book said potatoes didn't care much about soil quality, and kale was practically a weed.

Sarah grabbed a shovel and started digging.

Lena found her an hour later, knee-deep in a trench, arms aching, rocks piled in a growing mound beside the plot.

"What are you doing?" Lena asked.

"Garden."

"In October? At nine thousand feet?"

"The book says winter wheat can go in the ground through November if you mulch heavy. Garlic too. Potatoes wait for spring, but I can prep the beds now."

Lena looked at the book. She looked at the trench. She looked at Sarah, who was thirteen years old and covered in dirt and sweat and had the expression of someone who was going to finish this trench whether the mountain cooperated or not.

"I grew up on a farm in Oklahoma," Lena said. "Before I met Ray."

Sarah stopped digging.

"My grandmother grew potatoes in worse soil than this. The trick is amendments. Wood ash for potassium, composted leaves for organic matter, crushed eggshells for calcium. You've got a river right there for water. And if you can get your hands on lime, the pH problem mostly goes away."

Sarah handed her a shovel.

They worked side by side for the rest of the morning. Lena talked about her grandmother's garden, the difference between determinate and indeterminate plants, companion planting and crop rotation and a dozen other things Sarah had never heard of. Sarah wrote them all down in her journal between rows.

By noon they had three raised beds framed with rocks from the excavation. Not pretty. Not professional. But functional.

Mia watched from the porch steps, hugging her knees. She still hadn't spoken.

At one point, Sarah tossed a small, round stone toward the porch. It landed near Mia's frog boots with a soft click. Mia looked at it. Picked it up. Turned it over in her hands.

She didn't say anything. But she put the stone in her coat pocket, which was something.

## — Marcus —

The second group arrived on Day fifty.

Marcus was in the garage, helping Tommy test the solar battery connections, when Sarah's rifle cracked from the valley entrance. One shot. The signal for people.

Marcus grabbed the Glock and jogged to the entrance. Joel was already there, settled in his new tree stand twelve feet up the ponderosa, rifle steady on a branch rest. He'd finished it the day before. Sarah was on the boulder below, calm, watching the road.

"How many?" Marcus asked.

"Four," Sarah said. "Three adults, one teenager. Coming from the south. They've got a truck."

A truck. That was new. The Haywoods had walked.

Marcus checked his map. Four human contacts, moving slowly up the valley road. No other signatures within range.

"Same drill?" Joel called down from the stand.

"Same drill."

Marcus walked down the road to meet them. Joel covered from the tree stand. Tommy took a position by the dam with the shotgun. Sarah kept the rifle scope on the truck's windshield.

The truck was a beat-up Chevy Silverado, white, one headlight out, bed loaded with boxes, bags, and what looked like a mattress strapped down with bungee cords. It stopped thirty yards from Marcus and the engine died with a rattle that said the truck had been running on fumes and stubbornness.

The driver's door opened. A woman stepped out. Forties, heavyset, with short gray hair and a flannel shirt. She had a hunting rifle slung over her shoulder, muzzle pointed at the ground.

"We saw your lights," she said. "From the highway. Last night."

"How far?"

"Maybe fifteen miles? We were coming through from the Roaring Fork Valley. Took the pass road south. Saw the glow and figured it was worth checking."

The passenger door opened. An older man, sixties, thin, wearing glasses with one lens cracked down the middle. He moved stiffly, the way people moved when everything hurt and they'd stopped bothering to mention it. Behind them, two more people climbed out of the cab. A younger man, maybe twenty-five, with a beard and a baseball cap. And a girl, sixteen or seventeen, clutching a backpack to her chest.

The woman in flannel looked at Marcus. She looked at the compound behind him, the buildings, the lights, the dam.

"You running a settlement?" she asked.

"Starting one."

"FEDRA?"

"No."

"Fireflies?"

"No."

"Militia?"

"No. Just people."

She studied him for a few seconds. Marcus let her. First impressions went both directions.

"I'm Donna Pruitt," she said. "That's my father, Gene. The big guy's Carson. The girl's Ava. She's not related to any of us. We picked her up outside Aspen three days ago. She was walking alone."

Marcus looked at Ava. She was thin, dirty, and her eyes moved constantly. Scanning exits, measuring distances. He'd seen that look before. On Mia. On Sarah, in the early days.

"What happened in Aspen?" Marcus asked.

"Same thing that happened everywhere," Donna said. "Fell apart. The people who stayed got organized, but organized turned into controlled, and controlled turned into something else. We left. Ava left before us."

The old man, Gene, cleared his throat. "Son, we've been driving for three days on half a tank of gas and two cans of beans. If you're going to let us in, we'd appreciate it. If not, point us to the next safe place and we'll go there."

"There isn't a next safe place," Marcus said. "Not that I know of."

Gene nodded like that was the answer he'd expected.

Marcus looked at the truck, the people, the tired faces. He thought about Joel's crossfire positions and Sarah's screening rules and the list they'd drafted two nights ago. Questions to ask. A probation period. Weapons surrendered until trust was established. Separate housing.

"Drive up to the compound," Marcus said. "We'll get you fed and figure out the rest."

...

The screening went smoother than Marcus expected.

Joel ran it. That surprised everyone, including Joel. But when Marcus laid out the process, Joel had taken one look at the list, said "I'll do the interviews," and walked the four newcomers into the powerhouse one at a time.

Donna first. Ex-park ranger. Practical, direct, no visible deception. She handed over her hunting rifle without being asked. "I want it back," she said. "But I understand."

Gene next. Retired schoolteacher. History and civics. He had blood pressure medication that had run out two weeks ago and a dry cough that could be nothing or could be something. Joel told Marcus about the medication situation afterward in three words: "Old man needs pills."

Carson. Construction laborer from Grand Junction. Quiet, big, careful with his words. He'd been alone for three weeks before meeting Donna and Gene on the highway. He had a crowbar and a folding knife, both surrendered. His hands were calloused in the way that meant real work.

Ava last. Seventeen. She'd been part of a group in Aspen that had turned into something between a commune and a protection racket. She didn't give details. Joel didn't push. He came out of the interview and told Marcus, "She's clean. Scared, but clean. The others too."

Marcus bought Gene's medication. Lisinopril, 10mg, a three-month supply. 15 points. The cheapest purchase he'd made in weeks and possibly the most important.

**[Points: 227]**

By evening, the newcomers were settled in the second-floor rooms of the maintenance building. Donna had already volunteered for the watch rotation. Gene had found Sarah's farming book and was reading it on the porch with his cracked glasses. Carson was helping Tommy run conduit to the outbuildings. Ava was sitting on the steps of the residence, watching Mia chase a pinecone across the gravel.

Eleven people. Seven adults, one teenager, one child. Two families, six singles, one community.

## — Sarah —

She updated the journal that night.

_Day 50. Four new arrivals. Donna, Gene, Carson, Ava. Joel did the interviews and didn't scare anyone to death, which I'm counting as a win._

_The screening process works. Weapons surrendered on arrival, stored in the powerhouse lockup. One-week probation, separate housing in the maintenance building. Everyone gets fed regardless. Contribution expected but not forced during probation._

_Gene is a retired teacher. I haven't had a teacher in forty-nine days. I wonder if he'd be willing._

_Ava doesn't talk much. She keeps her backpack with her everywhere, like it's got the last piece of normal she owns in it. I'm not going to ask what's inside._

_Marcus bought solar panels, a water filter, and farming supplies. I've been digging garden beds with Lena. Winter wheat goes in tomorrow if the ground cooperates. Lena knows more about farming than anyone here. Her grandmother grew potatoes in Oklahoma clay, which apparently makes Colorado rock soil look easy._

_Mia still hasn't spoken. But she put a rock in her pocket today. A round one I tossed near her. That's something._

_We're eleven people now. The compound has lights, water, power, and the start of a food supply. We have a screening process and a watch rotation and a council of four that hasn't needed to vote on anything yet because Marcus just builds, Joel just fortifies, Tommy just wires, and I just dig._

_I don't know what this is yet. It's not a camp. It's not a town._

_But the lights are on. And people keep coming._

She closed the journal and looked out the east-facing window. Her window. The one she'd asked for back on the road, when a room with a view felt like the most impossible thing she could dream up.

The valley was dark except for the compound. Five buildings, lit. The porch light. The streetlight at the end of the access road. Joel's tree stand, a dark shape in the ponderosa, empty now but ready. And on the garage roof, if you caught them at the right angle, the faint blue-black glint of four solar panels.

It looked like a place people lived.

## — Marcus —

He found Joel on the dam walkway after everyone else had gone to sleep. Joel was leaning on the railing, looking at the reservoir. The water was black, still, reflecting scattered stars.

"Eleven people," Marcus said.

"I can count."

"The Haywoods came because they saw the lights. Donna's group came because they saw the lights. From fifteen miles out."

"I know where this is going."

"It's going to keep happening. Groups are going to get bigger. We need to be ready."

Joel was quiet for a while. The river below the dam churned through the spillway. An owl called from somewhere in the pines, two low notes and silence.

"You know what I keep thinkin' about?" Joel said. His voice was low, the way it got when he was being honest instead of careful. "That rest stop. The FEDRA deserters. The old man who died."

"Gerald."

"Gerald. We got there too late. Ten minutes earlier and I could've stopped that guy before he swung." Joel turned from the railing. "These people comin' in, the Haywoods, the ones today, whoever shows up tomorrow. They're all Gerald. They're all walkin' around with nobody between them and the next guy with a tire iron."

Marcus didn't say anything. He waited.

"I'm not sayin' throw the doors open," Joel said. "I'm sayin'... keep the light on."

Coming from Joel Miller, that was practically a speech.

"We'll keep it on," Marcus said.

Joel pushed off the railing. "I'm clearin' brush on the north ridge tomorrow. Don't let Tommy volunteer me for wiring."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

Joel walked toward the residence. Halfway there, he stopped and turned.

"The old man. Gene. He needs that blood pressure medicine every day. Set a reminder or somethin'."

"Already did."

Joel nodded once and went inside.

Marcus stayed on the dam. Below him, the compound glowed. Warm windows in cold dark. Eleven people sleeping behind walls that six weeks ago were empty.

**[Points: 227]**

Low. Lower than he'd been since the first week out of Austin. He'd need a clearing run soon. Push north into the mountains where his map showed infected clusters he hadn't touched yet. Restock the bank.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight the dam hummed beneath his feet and eleven people were safe.

He checked the settlement management screen.

**[Settlement: Crystal River Dam]**
**[Population: 11]**
**[Power: Dam (168 kW) + Solar (1 kW backup)]**
**[Water: River (filtered)]**
**[Food: Mall-supplied (transition to agriculture in progress)]**
**[Defenses: Perimeter watch, natural bottleneck, observation post]**
**[Morale: Stable]**

Not a town yet.

But getting there.
