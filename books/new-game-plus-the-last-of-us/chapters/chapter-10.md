# Chapter 10: The Cost of Breathing

## — Sarah —

The store was called Bauer's Feed & General. Hand-painted sign over the door, green letters on white wood, the kind of sign somebody's grandfather made forty years ago and nobody thought to replace because it still worked fine.

Sarah pushed through the screen door and it creaked like every screen door in Texas. The smell hit her first. Dust and grain and something sweet underneath, like old candy. Not the bad smell. Not the rotting smell she'd learned to identify in the first week. Just the smell of a place that had been closed up in the heat with no one to open the windows.

The shelves were half-picked. Someone had been through already, taken the canned goods and the bottled water and the batteries, left the rest. Bags of horse feed stacked against the back wall. A rack of work gloves. Rolls of baling wire. A spinning display of fishing lures that still spun when she touched it, smooth and easy, like it was waiting for a customer.

She spun it again. Watched the lures go around. Silver and red and green, with little feathered tails and hooks that caught the light from the front window. Her dad used to take her fishing at Lake Travis. Not often. Maybe three times total. But she remembered the tackle box and the way he'd tie the lures on with his thick fingers, patient and careful, squinting at the knot like it was the most important thing in the world.

She took a green one. Put it in her pocket. No reason. Just because.

The candy aisle was mostly intact. Whoever looted this place didn't care about Jolly Ranchers and expired gum. Sarah picked up a bag of watermelon Jolly Ranchers, turned it over in her hands, checked the date. Best by March 2014. Five months from now. Still good, technically. She opened the bag and put one in her mouth and the sugar hit her tongue like a small, private miracle.

Watermelon. Fake watermelon, the kind that tastes nothing like actual watermelon and everything like being twelve and alive and standing in a store where nobody was trying to kill her.

She took the bag.

The back of the store had a bulletin board. Community notices, yellowed and curling. A flyer for a rodeo in Llano. A lost dog poster: "Biscuit, tan lab mix, responds to his name, reward." Someone selling a 2004 F-150, "runs good, AC works." A church potluck schedule for October that would never happen.

Sarah read them all. She'd been doing this at every stop. Reading the paper trail of the old world. Shopping lists on refrigerators. Notes on kitchen counters. Calendar entries. "Pick up Sarah from soccer." That one had been in a house on Day Four, written in handwriting that wasn't her dad's, about a Sarah who wasn't her. She'd stared at it for a long time.

"Find anything good?" Tommy's voice from the front door.

"Candy." She held up the bag.

"Score." Tommy came in, checked the corners by habit, relaxed when the store was clearly empty. "Grab extras if there are any. Sugar's currency now."

She grabbed two more bags. Sour Patch Kids and a box of Milk Duds that had probably melted and resolidified into a single brown brick.

Outside, Joel was filling water bottles from a hand pump behind the store. The pump still worked. Marcus was sitting on the porch steps, doing the eye thing, scanning whatever invisible menu he scanned. The sun was high and white and the crossroads was quiet in the way that empty places are quiet, which is not actually quiet at all. Crickets. A fence post creaking. The pump handle squeaking as Joel worked it up and down.

Four buildings at this crossroads. The feed store, a church with the doors standing open, a house with a collapsed porch, and a metal barn with a tractor parked inside. No cars on the road. No smoke. No people.

Sarah sat on the porch and unwrapped another Jolly Rancher and thought about nothing in particular, which was getting harder to do every day.

## — Marcus —

The map changed at 11:47 AM.

He'd been running a passive scan, the kind that didn't cost anything, just the ambient awareness the system provided within the 25-mile radius. Infected showed as red dots. Animals were grey. The system didn't track humans.

Except.

**[LOCAL AREA MAP — ALERT]**
**[Movement detected: 5 unidentified contacts, bearing 195° (SSW), distance 0.8 miles. Approaching. Armed.]**
**[Note: Contacts are human. System tracking enabled due to hostile intent assessment.]**

Marcus read it twice. The system had never flagged humans before. He didn't know it could. The "hostile intent assessment" was new, a phrase that appeared in his interface like a line of code someone had just pushed live.

He stood up from the porch steps. Kept his voice level. "Tommy. Joel. We have a problem."

Joel stopped pumping. Tommy turned from the store doorway. Sarah looked up from her candy with the expression she wore when the adults got quiet, which was the expression of someone who'd learned in nine days that quiet adults meant danger.

"Five people coming from the south," Marcus said. "Less than a mile out. The system says they're armed."

Joel didn't ask how Marcus knew. He just set the water bottle down, picked up the Remington that was leaning against the pump housing, and checked the chamber. One smooth motion. Like checking the time.

"How long?" Joel asked.

"At walking pace, ten minutes. Maybe less."

Tommy was already moving. He crossed to Joel, took the rifle, and scanned south through the scope. The road ran straight for a quarter mile before curving behind a stand of mesquite. Nothing visible yet.

"Could be nothing," Tommy said. "Travelers."

"System says armed. And hostile."

"Your system been wrong before?"

"Not yet."

Joel looked at Sarah. At the porch. At the candy in her hand. The math was visible on his face, the calculation every parent makes in the space between a normal moment and a dangerous one. How fast. How far. Where to put her.

"Sarah. Inside the store. Behind the feed bags in the back. Don't come out until one of us comes for you."

"Dad..."

"Now, baby."

She went. No argument. She'd learned that too. The screen door creaked shut behind her.

Joel turned to Marcus. "What do we have?"

Marcus ran through the inventory. Joel had the SIG P226 with eleven rounds. Tommy had the Remington 700 with four in the magazine. Marcus had his hunting knife and a Glock 19 he'd purchased three days ago. Sixteen rounds.

"Thirty-one rounds between us," Marcus said. "Against five."

"Five what?" Joel asked. "Scared people with kitchen knives, or somethin' worse?"

"I don't know."

"Then we find out." Joel moved to the corner of the store, positioning himself where he could see the south road without being visible from it. "Tommy, get up on that barn roof. If it goes bad, you're overwatch."

Tommy nodded and jogged across the road to the metal barn. He climbed the tractor, then the side rail, then pulled himself onto the roof in four seconds. Military. The kind of thing you don't forget how to do even when you've been out for years.

Marcus stayed on the porch. Visible. That was the plan, or the plan that Joel laid out in the thirty seconds they had, which was: Marcus stands in the open. One person is less threatening than three. If they're friendly, nobody gets hurt. If they're not, Joel flanks and Tommy shoots from elevation.

"You good with being the bait?" Joel had asked.

"Not really."

"Good. Nervous bait is convincing."

They waited. The crickets kept going. The fence post kept creaking. The sun kept being hot and white and indifferent to what was about to happen under it.

Marcus watched the road.

## — Joel —

Joel saw them before Marcus did.

Five. Coming around the mesquite curve in a loose group, not a formation. Two in front, three behind. The two in front carried rifles, hunting models, bolt-action. The three behind had a mix. A baseball bat, a machete, and what looked like a pistol tucked into a waistband. All men. Mid-twenties to forties. Dirty. Thin. The kind of thin that meant they hadn't been eating enough, which meant they'd been taking from people who had, or trying to.

Joel had seen this before. Not in person, not yet, because it had only been ten days since everything fell apart. But he knew the type. He'd grown up around men who took things. Men who measured opportunity against risk and didn't factor in the cost to anyone else. Contractors who cut corners. Bosses who skimmed. Guys at bars who picked fights they'd already decided to win.

These men had made a decision somewhere on the road between wherever they came from and here. The decision was simple. Other people have things. We want things. The math works if we're willing.

Joel had made that math work himself, in other circumstances, in the years after Tommy left and the world got smaller and harder. He didn't think about that now. He thought about Sarah behind the feed bags and the eleven rounds in the SIG and the angle from the corner of the store that would give him a clear line on the two with rifles.

Marcus was on the porch. Standing loose, hands at his sides, the Glock tucked in the back of his waistband under his shirt. Playing the part. One guy, alone, at a crossroads. Easy mark.

The two in front stopped twenty yards out. The bigger one, thick neck, sunburned, a Remington 783 in his hands that he held like someone who knew which end the bullets came from but had never fired at a person, raised a hand.

"Hey there," the big one said. Friendly voice. The kind of friendly that had a price tag attached.

"Hey," Marcus said.

"Just the one of you?"

"Just me."

"Passing through?"

"Resting up. Heading northwest."

The big one looked at the store. At the pump. At the water bottles Joel had left on the ground. Five full bottles, lined up neat. Stupid. Joel should have stashed them. Too late now.

"Northwest," the big one repeated. "Long walk. You got supplies?"

"Some."

The five spread out. Not a lot. Just enough that they weren't clustered. The two with rifles stayed in front. The three with close-range weapons fanned to the sides. It was sloppy but it was deliberate. These men had done this before. Maybe not many times. Maybe twice. Maybe three times. Enough to have a routine.

"Here's the thing," the big one said. He shifted the rifle. Not pointing it. Not yet. Just moving it so the barrel happened to face Marcus's general direction. "We been walking six days and we ain't found much. You look like you been eating regular. That's unusual."

"I've been careful."

"Careful's one word for it." The big one smiled. Teeth were bad. "Way I see it, you share some of what you got, we keep moving, nobody's day gets worse. Fair?"

Joel watched from the corner of the store, eight feet from the nearest man. They hadn't seen him. They were focused on Marcus, on the porch, on the water bottles, on the math they'd already done. Five against one. Easy.

Marcus was quiet for a beat too long. Joel could see his face from the angle. The kid was thinking. Not scared, exactly. Processing. Running through options in his head the way he did when he stared at invisible things, except this time the invisible thing was a moral equation about five men on a road who were probably going to hurt someone today regardless of what happened here.

"I can give you some food," Marcus said. "Water too. But I need you to keep moving after."

"Sure," the big one said. "Sure, that's the deal."

It wasn't the deal. Joel had been reading people for thirty-eight years and the big one's eyes were already past Marcus, already inside the store, already counting what was on the shelves. The guy with the machete had shifted two steps to the right, toward the side of the building. The guy with the pistol was looking at the barn.

Joel moved.

He came around the corner fast and low and the SIG was up before any of them processed that the one-on-five had just become two-on-five. The nearest man, the one with the baseball bat, heard Joel's boot on gravel and turned and Joel hit him in the jaw with the butt of the pistol. Hard. The sound was wrong, a crack that shouldn't come from a face, and the man went down.

"Drop 'em!" Joel shouted. Not a warning. A command. The voice he used when there was no room between the words for negotiation.

The big one swung the rifle toward Joel.

Tommy's shot came from the barn roof. A flat crack that rolled across the crossroads and echoed off the church walls. The big one's left shoulder jerked and he stumbled sideways and the rifle clattered to the ground.

The second rifleman panicked. He brought his weapon up, not at anyone specific, just up, the barrel sweeping in a wild arc. Marcus tackled him from the porch. Not clean, not graceful, a full-body collision that drove both of them off the steps and into the dirt. The rifle went one way. Marcus's elbow went into the man's face. Once. Twice. The man stopped moving.

That left two. Machete and pistol.

Machete ran at Joel.

Joel shot him. Center mass, close range, the SIG barking once in his hand. The man folded. Fell forward. Hit the gravel face-first and didn't move.

The last one, the one with the pistol, had it halfway out of his waistband when Tommy's second shot hit the ground six inches from his feet. A warning. Deliberate. Tommy on a barn roof with a bolt-action rifle placing rounds exactly where he wanted them.

The man froze. His hand was on the pistol grip. His eyes were wide. He was maybe twenty-five, thin and pale, a scraggly beard that didn't connect at the jaw. He looked like someone who worked at a gas station two weeks ago and was now standing in a crossroads with a gun and no good choices.

"Let go of it," Joel said.

The man looked at Joel. At the SIG pointed at his chest. At his friend on the ground with the red stain spreading through his shirt. At the big one sitting in the dirt holding his shoulder, cursing, blood between his fingers.

He let go of the pistol. It dropped to the dirt.

Joel kicked it away. Picked up the two rifles. Walked to the man on the ground, the one Marcus had tackled, who was conscious but not fighting, blood running from his nose.

"Get up," Joel said.

The man got up. Slowly. He looked at Marcus, who was standing with dirt on his clothes and his hands shaking, and then at Joel, who was standing with someone else's blood on his knuckles and his hands perfectly still.

The big one was trying to stand. His shoulder was wrecked. Tommy's shot had gone through the deltoid muscle and out the back. A through-and-through. He'd live, but he wouldn't be holding a rifle for a long time.

"Here's how this works," Joel said. His voice was quiet now. The shouting was over. Quiet Joel was worse than loud Joel. "You walk south. You keep walking. You don't come back this way. You don't follow us."

"My friend needs a doctor," the big one said through his teeth.

"Your friend needed better judgment. Take him and go."

Marcus stepped forward. Joel looked at him. Marcus had the look he got when moral calculation met practical reality. The look that said this wasn't clean and might not be right and there wasn't a better answer anyway.

"Let me bandage his shoulder," Marcus said.

"No," Joel said.

"Joel. He's not a threat anymore."

"He was a threat five minutes ago. He'll be a threat again when the shoulder heals."

"He'll bleed out on the road."

Joel looked at the big one on the ground. At the wound. At the blood. He'd seen worse. He'd caused worse. But Marcus was looking at him with something that wasn't quite judgment and wasn't quite pleading, just the steady, uncomfortable gaze of someone who believed the rules still applied even when they didn't.

"Fine," Joel said. "Make it fast."

Marcus knelt beside the big one and pulled a compression bandage from his pack. The big one watched him with the confused expression of a man being helped by someone he'd just tried to rob. Marcus packed the wound, wrapped it tight, and stood up.

"South," Joel repeated. "Now."

They went. The man Marcus had knocked down helped the big one walk. The guy Joel had pistol-whipped staggered behind them, holding his jaw. They collected the man Joel had shot.

He was still alive. Gut-shot. Walking, barely, held up between two of the others. His breathing was wrong. Short, shallow, wet.

Joel watched them go. Five became four shapes on the road, then three, then smudges, then nothing.

Tommy climbed down from the barn roof. Checked the perimeter. Came back. "Clear," he said. Just the word. Nothing else needed.

Marcus was staring at the spot where the man had fallen. The dark patch in the gravel. Already going brown in the sun.

Joel checked the SIG. Ten rounds remaining. He didn't holster it.

## — Sarah —

She'd seen everything.

Not from behind the feed bags. She'd gone there, like her dad said. Sat down in the dust behind the stacked fifty-pound sacks of horse feed and waited in the dark with her knees drawn up and the bag of Jolly Ranchers clutched in her hand like it mattered.

Then she heard Marcus say "I can give you some food" and her body moved on its own. Not to the front of the store. To the side window. The one behind the rack of work gloves, where a gap in the dusty glass gave her a clear view of the road and the porch and the five men who stood there like they owned the crossroads and everything in it.

She saw her dad come around the corner.

She saw him hit the man with the bat. The crack. The way the man folded. Like the bones in his face had been rearranged and his body needed a second to understand the new configuration.

She saw Tommy shoot from the barn roof. The distant pop and the way the big man's shoulder snapped sideways.

She saw Marcus tackle someone off the porch.

She saw her dad shoot a man in the chest.

The man fell. He fell forward, like he was trying to bow, and then he was on the ground and there was red on his shirt and he wasn't bowing, he was dying, or close to it, and her father was standing over him with the gun still up and his face was blank. Not angry. Not scared. Not sorry. Blank. Like the man on the ground was a problem and the bullet was the answer and the math was done.

Sarah had seen her dad angry before. She'd seen him argue with neighbors and yell at Tommy and slam a cabinet door so hard it came off the hinges. She'd seen him tense, the night everything happened, driving through Austin with his jaw tight and his hands white on the steering wheel.

She'd never seen him blank.

The man on the ground was making sounds. Not screams. Just sounds. Short, wet, confused sounds, like he was trying to figure out what had happened and couldn't.

Sarah's hands were shaking. The Jolly Rancher bag crinkled. She pressed it against her thigh to make it stop.

After that it went fast. Joel talked. The men left. Marcus bandaged someone. Tommy said "clear." It was over in the time it takes to eat lunch, except lunch didn't leave a dark stain in the gravel and a smell like pennies hanging in the air.

She heard the screen door creak.

"Sarah?" Joel's voice. Tight. Controlled. "You can come out."

She walked out from behind the work gloves. Through the store, past the fishing lures, past the candy rack, through the screen door and onto the porch where Marcus had stood and the wooden railing had a dent in it where someone's head had hit it.

Joel was wiping his hands on his jeans. There was blood on his knuckles. He saw her looking and stopped wiping and put his hands at his sides like he could hide them by holding still.

"You okay?" he asked.

"I saw," she said.

Joel's face did something. A very small something, a tightening around the eyes and a shift in the jaw, that on anyone else would be invisible and on Joel was the equivalent of a scream. He looked at Tommy. Tommy looked at the ground.

"You were supposed to stay in the back," Joel said.

"I know."

He stood there. Her father. Blood drying on his hands and a gun in his belt and the blankness gone now, replaced by something worse, which was Joel Miller realizing his daughter had watched him shoot a man and there was no version of the next sixty seconds that made it okay.

"Baby, I..."

"Are you hurt?" Sarah asked.

He blinked. "What?"

"Are you hurt. Any of you."

"No. We're fine."

"Then let's go."

She walked past him. Off the porch, onto the gravel, past the dark spot that she didn't look at because looking at it would make it permanent, past the water bottles and the pump and the church with its doors standing open. She walked north. The direction they'd been going. The direction that led to Colorado and mountains and something her dad had promised would be safe.

Her hip didn't hurt. She noticed that because it seemed important, the fact that her body could feel fine while the rest of her felt like someone had scooped out her insides and put them back in the wrong order.

Twelve years old. Ten days since the world ended. She'd been shot by a soldier and saved by a stranger and watched her father shoot a man who fell like he was bowing.

She kept walking.

## — Marcus —

They covered four miles before anyone spoke.

Joel was in front. Walking fast. Not his usual pace, which was measured and deliberate and designed around Sarah's hip. This was Joel walking away from something, and the something was behind them in a crossroads with a feed store and a dark stain on the gravel.

Sarah was behind him. Matching his speed without complaint. Her face was closed. Not angry, not crying, not anything Marcus could read. Just closed, the way you close a book when you're done with a chapter and not sure you want to read the next one.

Tommy walked beside Marcus at the rear. Tommy hadn't said much since the crossroads. He'd checked the weapons, redistributed the ammo, picked up the two rifles the looters had dropped. Practical tasks. The kind of thing you did with your hands when your head needed time.

"That was the first time," Tommy said quietly.

"First time what?"

"First time she saw Joel do that."

Marcus looked at Joel's back. At the set of his shoulders. At the hands that swung at his sides, cleaned but not clean, because gravel dust and dried blood had a way of getting into the creases no matter how hard you wiped.

"She would have seen it eventually," Marcus said.

"Eventually isn't today."

Tommy was right. Eventually was a comfortable word. It meant later. It meant not now. It meant someone else could deal with it. Today was a twelve-year-old at a window watching her father's face go blank, and no amount of eventually could put that back where it was.

They stopped at a creek crossing an hour later. Clear water, ankle-deep, running over flat limestone. A good spot. Tommy declared a rest and nobody argued.

Joel sat on a rock upstream and washed his hands in the creek. Slowly. Methodically. Scrubbing each finger, working the creases, rinsing, scrubbing again. He did this for a long time.

Sarah sat downstream with her feet in the water, unlacing her boots and setting them on the bank. She ate a Jolly Rancher. Then another one. Then she held the bag out toward Marcus without looking at him.

"Want one?"

"Sure." He took a green one. Apple.

They sat by the creek and sucked on candy and listened to the water move over the rocks. A kingfisher darted along the surface twenty yards downstream, a blue-grey streak that appeared and vanished so fast Marcus wasn't sure he'd seen it.

"Marcus."

"Yeah."

"That man Dad shot." Sarah's voice was flat. Conversational. Like she was asking about the weather or the road ahead. "Is he going to die?"

Marcus considered lying. Lying was easy. Lying was kind. Lying was what you did for a twelve-year-old who'd already seen too much.

"Probably," he said.

Sarah nodded. She put another candy in her mouth. Chewed it, which wasn't how you were supposed to eat Jolly Ranchers, but nobody was writing the rules anymore.

"They were going to hurt us," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Then it was the right thing."

Marcus looked at her. She was staring at the creek. At her feet in the water. At the small eddies that formed around her ankles and dissolved and formed again.

"It was the necessary thing," Marcus said. "I'm not sure those are always the same."

Sarah thought about that. He could see her thinking, the way her eyes narrowed and her jaw set, the same expression Joel wore when he was processing something he didn't want to process. The resemblance was startling sometimes. The same stubbornness. The same refusal to look away from hard things.

"Okay," she said. And that was all.

Joel appeared at the edge of the creek. Hands clean. Wet. He looked at Sarah, and the look on his face was the one Marcus recognized from the games, from the cutscenes, from the moments when Joel Miller stood at the edge of something he couldn't fix with his hands or a gun. The look of a man who was terrified of the distance between himself and the person he loved most.

"We should keep movin'," Joel said.

"Five more minutes," Sarah said. "My feet are tired."

Joel looked at Marcus. Marcus looked at Joel. Something passed between them that wasn't quite understanding and wasn't quite forgiveness. Just acknowledgment. Of what had happened. Of what it cost. Of the fact that the cost would keep coming whether they paid it or not.

Joel sat down on the bank. Not close to Sarah. Not far. The exact distance a father sits from a daughter when he doesn't know if she wants him near.

Sarah pulled her feet out of the creek and put her boots back on. Laced them slowly, carefully, the way Joel tied fishing lures, patient and precise. Then she stood up and walked to Joel and put her hand on his arm.

"Come on, Dad," she said. "Let's go."

Joel's throat moved. Once. He stood up.

They walked.

**[Status]**
**[Points: 647]**
**[Health: 89%]**
**[Map Range: 25 miles]**
**[Local Area: 0 infected within 15 miles. 0 hostile contacts detected.]**

No points. The system didn't award points for killing people. Marcus had wondered about that during the fight, a cold and ugly thought that arrived uninvited between the tackle and the second elbow, and now he had his answer. Infected were the system's currency. Humans were something else entirely.

His health had dropped five percent. Bruised ribs from the tackle, probably. A scrape on his forearm he hadn't noticed until the adrenaline wore off. Small prices.

The map was clear ahead. Fifteen miles of empty road, no contacts of any kind. Just Texas. Just the road. Just four people walking toward something they hadn't reached yet.

Ninety-seven miles from the farmhouse. Seven hundred and three to go.

Behind them, a crossroads with a feed store and a general store and a church with its doors open and a dark spot on the gravel that would dry and fade and be gone by tomorrow. In front of them, West Texas and the long flat nothing that stretched to the horizon and the mountains that were still weeks away.

Marcus checked his hands. Scraped knuckles. Dirt under his fingernails. A tremor in his right hand that hadn't been there this morning.

"How far today?" Tommy asked.

Marcus looked at the sun. Looked at the road. Looked at Sarah, who was walking beside Joel now, close enough that their arms almost touched, her stride even and steady and not limping, carrying a bag of watermelon Jolly Ranchers and a fishing lure in her pocket and something new behind her eyes that hadn't been there at breakfast.

"As far as we need to," Marcus said.

Tommy nodded. Good enough.

They walked north.
