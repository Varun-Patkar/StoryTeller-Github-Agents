# Chapter 57: Corners

## — Ellie —

**Day 32. Hunting cabin. 5:48 AM.**

She woke up before them.

That shouldn't have been possible. Joel slept like a soldier, one ear open, and Marcus apparently heard heartbeats through walls. But Ellie opened her eyes to grey light through a dirty window and two grown men still out. Joel on the floor with his back against the wall, chin on his chest. Marcus on the cabin's one cot, boots still on, arms crossed.

She lay in her sleeping bag on the floor and stared at the ceiling. Pine boards with water stains. A cobweb in the corner with nothing in it.

Three days ago she'd been in Room 204. Same position. Flat on her back, staring at a ceiling, counting stains. That ceiling had been institutional tile with a yellow water mark shaped like Florida. This one was rough pine with knotholes.

Better ceiling. Same girl.

She sat up. Her back hurt from the floor. The sleeping bag Marcus had produced from wherever his supplies came from was decent, but the boards underneath didn't care about padding.

Outside, birds. A lot of them. In the QZ you heard pigeons and crows. Here it sounded like a whole orchestra was tuning up in the trees. She didn't know what any of them were. She'd never needed to.

Ellie pulled on her boots and tied them. Grabbed her backpack. Unzipped the front pocket and checked. Savage Starlight #4. Sofia's note. The switchblade. All there.

She crept past Joel. He didn't move. Past Marcus, who also didn't move but whose breathing changed just enough that she knew he was awake and letting her go.

The cabin door opened onto a clearing surrounded by birch trees. The air was cold and wet and smelled like dirt and something sweet she couldn't name. Dew on the grass. A rusted truck axle half-buried in weeds near the tree line. A bird she'd never seen, bright blue, sitting on a branch ten feet away, looking at her like she was the weird one.

"Hey," she said to the bird.

The bird left.

"Cool. Great talk."

She sat on the cabin's front step and pulled her knees up. The forest stretched in every direction. No walls. No checkpoints. No floodlights. Just trees and sky and the sound of water somewhere nearby, a creek or a stream, moving over rocks.

She'd been outside the QZ for two days and it still didn't feel real. The world was big. Stupidly big. In the QZ, the biggest open space was the courtyard between Building C and the cafeteria, maybe sixty yards across, and you shared it with two hundred people and a guard who yelled if you got too close to the wall.

Here, she could walk in any direction and not hit a wall for miles. Maybe ever.

The door creaked behind her.

"Mornin'," Joel said.

He sat down next to her. Not too close. The same distance he'd maintained since Room 204. Far enough to give her space, close enough to reach if something went wrong.

"You always get up this early?" Ellie asked.

"Body doesn't know how to sleep past five anymore. Twenty years of first watch."

"That sounds terrible."

"You get used to it."

He had a canteen. He drank from it and offered it to her. She took it. The water was cold and tasted like metal. She drank half of it and handed it back.

"We should move soon," Joel said. "Marcus wants to cover twenty miles today."

"Twenty miles. On foot."

"We'll find a vehicle eventually. There's usually something on the highways."

"Usually." Ellie looked at the trees. "You've done this before? Cross-country?"

"Once. Other direction. Texas to Colorado, back in 2013. Took us six weeks."

"And you walked the whole thing?"

"Walked most of it. Found a truck near the end."

Ellie pulled at a loose thread on her cargo pants. "Was Sarah with you?"

Joel was quiet for a second. "She was thirteen. Healing from a gunshot wound. She rode in a wagon I pulled for the first two weeks. Walked the rest."

"She sounds tough."

"Toughest person I know."

...

They left the cabin at six-thirty. Marcus led, checking whatever invisible thing he checked every few minutes. Joel brought up the rear. Ellie walked between them, which she'd stopped complaining about.

The route cut through forest and then onto a two-lane road heading west. The pavement was cracked and buckled, grass growing through every seam. Twenty years of freeze-thaw cycles with nobody to patch the holes. A guardrail along the shoulder had rusted to orange lace.

Ellie looked at everything. Not just looking. Studying. Filing. A collapsed barn in a field. A mailbox with the flag still up. A minivan with all four doors open and a child's car seat in the back, faded to grey.

She didn't comment on the car seat. Joel noticed her not commenting and said nothing about that either.

"Hey, Marcus," Ellie said after a mile of silence. "What do you call a fake noodle?"

Marcus glanced back. "What?"

"An impasta."

Silence. A crow called from a power line.

"That's terrible," Marcus said.

"Thank you." She waited maybe thirty seconds. "What do you call a bear with no teeth?"

"I don't know."

"A gummy bear."

Joel made a sound. Not a laugh. More like a cough that had a laugh hiding inside it.

"I have more," Ellie said.

"I'm sure you do," Joel said.

## — Marcus —

**Day 32. Route 20 westbound. 11:15 AM.**

They'd covered nine miles when the map flagged contacts.

Three signatures. Orange. Runners. Clustered inside a structure half a mile ahead, just off the road. A gas station, based on the map's outline. Stationary. Dormant.

Marcus stopped. "Three infected. Gas station up ahead. We can go around."

Joel came up beside him. Scanned the road. The gas station was visible through the trees, a low concrete building with a rusted canopy over two pump islands. The pumps were gone. Stripped years ago.

"We go around, we add two miles through those woods," Joel said. "Terrain's thick. Could be more in there we don't see."

"My map shows clear."

Joel looked at Ellie. Then back at the gas station. Marcus could see the calculation happening. Not about the Runners. Three Runners was nothing. Joel could handle three Runners in his sleep, and Marcus could handle thirty. The calculation was about the girl.

"She needs to know," Joel said. Low. Just for Marcus.

Marcus understood. Ellie had lived fourteen years behind walls. She'd seen infected through chain-link and heard them through concrete. But she'd never been in a space with them, never moved through a room where they could come from any direction. If she was going to survive in the world outside the QZ, she needed to learn. And Joel was the one who taught people.

"Your call," Marcus said.

Joel nodded. He turned to Ellie.

"We're going through that gas station," he said. "Three Runners inside. I'm going to show you how to clear a room."

Ellie's eyes went wide. Then they narrowed. She pulled the switchblade from her back pocket.

"Okay," she said. Not scared. Not excited. Just ready.

"Put that away for now." Joel unholstered his pistol. Checked the magazine. "First thing. You don't go in swinging. You go in seeing. Eyes before hands. Corners before center."

"Corners before center," Ellie repeated.

"When you enter a room, you check the corners closest to the door first. That's where threats hide. Not the middle. The middle is where you look naturally. Natural is wrong. Natural gets you killed."

He pointed at the gas station. "That building has one main entrance, probably a back door, and windows. We go in through the front. Marcus takes the back door in case anything runs. You stay behind me, one step to my left. You watch my weak side."

"Your left?"

"My left. That's the side I can't cover while I'm aimin' forward. You see anything move on that side, you tell me. Don't yell. Don't whisper. Just talk. 'Left,' 'movement,' 'Runner.' Short words."

Ellie nodded. Her hand was on the switchblade in her pocket. Not holding it. Touching it. A habit Marcus had noticed in Room 204.

"One more thing," Joel said. "If I tell you to get behind me, you do it. No argument. No hesitation. We talk about why later. In the moment, you move."

"Got it."

Joel looked at her. Whatever he saw, it was enough. He started toward the gas station. Ellie fell in behind him, one step to his left.

Marcus circled around to the back of the building. The rear door was a metal fire exit, rusted shut. He could hear them inside. Three heartbeats, irregular, the arrhythmic pattern of Cordyceps-damaged cardiovascular systems. Two near the front. One in the back, near the cooler units.

He waited. Listened for Joel's approach.

The front door opened.

...

Joel went through the door like he'd done it ten thousand times, because he had. Low, fast, weapon up. He checked the right corner. Clear. Left corner. Runner.

It was crouching behind the checkout counter, head down, fungal growth along the left side of its jaw and neck. Male. Medium build. Former gas station employee, based on the faded blue polo shirt.

"Runner, left," Ellie said from behind him. Her voice was steady. Not a whisper. Not a shout. Just the word, clear and flat.

Joel fired. One round, center mass. The Runner staggered. Second round, head. It dropped.

The second Runner came from behind a shelving unit on the right, moving fast. That was the thing about Runners. They weren't smart, but they were quick. This one had been a teenager. Baggy jeans, sneakers, fungal tendrils across both eyes.

Joel pivoted. Ellie moved with him, keeping to his left side without being told. She'd listened. The pivot opened her sightline to the right side of the store and she said, "Clear right," before Joel could check.

He dropped the second Runner with one shot. Three years of militia training, fifteen years of real combat before that. The muscle memory didn't need his brain's permission.

The third Runner came from the back of the store. It hit the metal fire exit with both hands, trying to get through, then turned and charged up the aisle toward the sound of gunfire. Marcus was already inside, having forced the exit door open. He caught the Runner mid-turn, one hand on its throat, knife through the base of the skull with the other. Clean. Over in a second.

The gas station was quiet.

Ellie stood exactly where Joel had told her to stand. One step behind him, one step to his left. The switchblade was in her hand now, open, held in a reverse grip. She hadn't needed it. But she'd been ready.

"Corners before center," she said. Breathing a little fast. Eyes moving, still checking.

"Good," Joel said. He holstered the pistol. "You did good."

Ellie looked at the two Runners on the floor. The teenager in the baggy jeans. The man in the polo shirt. Real people, once.

"Does it get easier?" she asked.

Joel didn't lie to her. "The mechanics do. The rest of it shouldn't."

Ellie folded the switchblade and put it away. She stepped over the teenager's body and walked to the back of the store where a few cans had rolled off the shelves. She picked one up. Peaches, the label sun-bleached to pink.

"These still good?" she called.

"Check the seal," Marcus said. "If it's not dented or bulging, probably."

She shook the can. Something sloshed inside. She tucked it into her backpack.

"Dibs."

Marcus's system had already tallied the kills. 39 points total. He hadn't needed to fight, but Joel hadn't asked him to sit this one out. The lesson was for Ellie. Marcus was just the safety net.

They left the gas station through the front door. Ellie walked beside Joel now, not behind him. She hadn't been told to. She just did.

## — Joel —

**Day 32. A creek crossing, somewhere in western Massachusetts. 4:20 PM.**

They found the creek around mid-afternoon. Wide, shallow, cold. Clear enough to see the stones on the bottom. A concrete bridge had collapsed into it years ago, the pieces sitting in the water like stepping stones.

Marcus filtered water. Joel sat on the bank and let his boots dry. His knees were talking to him. Thirty-two days of walking and they had opinions.

Ellie was in the creek. Boots off, pants rolled to the knees, standing ankle-deep in water so cold her teeth were chattering, and she didn't care. She was turning over rocks and examining what lived underneath.

"Dude, there's crawdads in here," she said.

"Crayfish," Joel said.

"Same thing."

"Depends on where you're from."

"I'm from a concrete box in Boston. We don't have a word for them because we don't have them." She held up a rock. Something small and brown scrambled off the underside. "What's this?"

"Salamander."

"It's slimy."

"They are."

She let the salamander go and watched it disappear under another rock. Then she looked downstream where the water deepened around the bridge debris.

"Can you swim in this?"

Joel glanced at the depth. Two feet, maybe three at the deepest point. "Not really. But there are rivers out west where you can."

"I can't swim."

"You'll learn."

She looked at him. That measuring look again. "Sarah can swim?"

"Sarah can do most things."

"You keep saying that. 'Sarah can do this, Sarah runs that.' Is she actually real or is she just the perfect person you made up to get me to come to Colorado?"

Joel almost smiled. "She's real. And she ain't perfect. She burns toast every time. Can't parallel park to save her life, not that there's much parking anymore. And she argues with me about everything."

"That doesn't sound like a flaw. That sounds like she's right and you don't want to admit it."

Joel did smile then. Small. Gone fast. "You'll get along fine."

Ellie climbed out of the creek and sat on a flat rock to dry her feet. She had blisters forming on both heels. QZ boots weren't made for twenty-mile days. Marcus had offered to buy her better ones from whatever invisible store he accessed, and she'd said no. She wanted to earn the blisters.

Joel didn't understand that. But he respected it.

"Hey Joel," she said.

"Yeah."

"What's brown and sticky?"

He looked at her.

"A stick."

Joel shook his head. "That's the worst one yet."

"I'm pacing myself. I have a whole book of these. Had. Left it in Room 204. But I memorized most of them."

"God help us."

"What's the difference between a piano and a fish?"

"I don't want to know."

"You can tune a piano but you can't tuna fish."

Joel pulled on his boots. Stood up. His knees made a sound like stepping on gravel and he ignored it.

"We should keep movin'," he said. "Three more hours of light."

...

They made camp at dusk in a clearing off the road. A fire ring left by someone years ago, stones blackened and scattered. Marcus built a fire with supplies he probably didn't need to use but did because normal things mattered.

Ellie sat cross-legged by the flames, eating the can of peaches she'd taken from the gas station. She ate with her fingers, tilting the can to drink the syrup at the bottom.

"Want some?" She held the can toward Joel.

"I'm good." He was eating jerky from his own pack. Chewing slowly, the way he always did. Making it last.

"Your loss. These are amazing. I've never had peaches."

"Never?"

"We had applesauce. In packets. It tasted like the packet."

Joel looked at the fire. Twenty years of this. Kids who'd never had a peach. Kids who couldn't swim. Kids locked in rooms because they were immune to the thing that had killed everyone else. And the adults in charge of those kids deciding the best use for them was a surgical table.

He'd have killed Anderson on that highway if Marlene hadn't backed down. He knew that. Marcus knew it too. Neither of them had said it out loud.

"Joel," Ellie said. She'd finished the peaches and set the can down. The firelight caught her face, the freckles, the scar on her forearm. "Can I ask you something and you won't give me the dad answer?"

"I'm not your dad."

"I know. That's why I'm asking." She pulled at a thread on her shoe. "The gas station today. You've done that a lot. Clearing rooms."

"A lot, yeah."

"How many infected have you killed?"

Joel thought about it. He'd never counted. Marcus probably had a number from his system. Joel just had years.

"More than I want to remember."

"And people?"

The fire popped. A log shifted. Joel looked at Ellie and saw the same thing he'd seen in Sarah's eyes on a road in Texas twenty years ago, watching him shoot a man through a store window. A kid seeing the shape of the person protecting her and trying to decide if that shape was safe.

"Some," Joel said.

"Bad people?"

"People who were tryin' to hurt people I was protectin'. Whether they were bad..." He trailed off. "I don't think about it that way."

Ellie was quiet. She poked at the fire with a stick.

"Marlene said you had a good twenty years," she said. "What did she mean?"

"She expected me to be different. Harder. Angrier."

"Why?"

Joel looked at the fire, then at Marcus across the clearing, who was doing the thing where his eyes went unfocused, checking maps or systems or whatever it was.

"Because in another life, maybe I would've been."

Ellie nodded like that made sense, even though it couldn't have. She lay back on her sleeping bag and stared at the sky. Stars again. The same stars from that first night outside the QZ, thick and impossible.

"Hey Joel."

"Yeah."

"Thanks for today. The corners thing."

"You did the work. I just told you where to look."

"Still. Thanks." She rolled onto her side, facing the fire. "Nobody ever taught me anything useful before. I mean, Mrs. Patterson taught me to dodge a dodgeball, but that was mostly about not getting hit in the face."

"Sounds useful."

"Brad would disagree."

Joel shook his head. He pulled his jacket tighter and settled against the tree trunk behind him. First watch. Always first watch.

## — Marcus —

**Day 32. Campsite. 11:40 PM.**

The fire had burned down to embers. Joel was still awake, sitting against the tree, but his eyes were half-closed. Twenty years of first watch had wired him to doze without fully sleeping, one ear always listening.

Marcus lay on his back a few yards away. He didn't need to sleep as much anymore. The Tier 5 enhancements had changed his sleep cycle to roughly three hours a night, the rest spent in a light trance that let the neural processing do its work.

He heard Ellie's sleeping bag rustle. Her heartbeat had been too fast for sleep for the last forty minutes.

She sat up. Looked around. Saw Joel's silhouette against the tree and Marcus lying in the dark.

"Can't sleep?" Marcus said. Quiet.

She flinched. Then recovered. "You're creepy, you know that? Just lying there with your eyes open in the dark."

"Fair point."

She scooted closer to the dying fire. Wrapped her arms around her knees.

"It's too quiet," she said. "In the QZ there's always noise. Generators. People. Guards on the wall. Here it's just... nothing."

"Give it a week. The noise will come back. Different noise, but it fills the same space."

Ellie picked up the stick she'd been poking the fire with earlier. Drew a line in the ash.

"Marcus."

"Yeah."

"You said you've known about me since before I was born."

"I did."

"And you spent years building medical stuff so nobody would have to kill me."

"That's right."

She drew another line. A cross. Then rubbed it out with her shoe.

"Why?"

Marcus sat up. The embers gave just enough light to see her face. She wasn't asking for the strategic answer. Not the vaccine logic, not the community-building rationale. She was asking the thing underneath all of it.

"Because your life isn't a price to pay for someone else's," he said. "Not Marlene's cure. Not the world's survival. Your life is yours."

Ellie stared at the embers.

"Nobody's ever just... wanted me to be alive," she said. "Riley wanted to be with me. Marlene wanted to use me. FEDRA wanted to contain me. You're the first people who want me to just... exist."

"That's what family does."

The word hung there. Family. Ellie turned it over the way she'd turned over the rock in the creek, examining what lived underneath.

"I don't know what that means," she said. Not sad. Honest.

"You'll figure it out. You've got time."

She was quiet for a while. An owl called from somewhere in the trees, a low sound that carried across the clearing. Ellie turned her head toward it.

"What was that?"

"Owl."

"It sounds big."

"Great horned owl, probably. About two feet tall."

"Two feet." She pulled a face. "That's a lot of owl."

Marcus almost laughed. Didn't. Just let the moment be what it was.

Ellie lay back down. Her heartbeat was slowing. The quiet wasn't empty anymore. It had an owl in it, and embers, and two people who were awake because she was awake, and that was enough.

"Hey Marcus."

"Yeah."

"Tell Sarah she better have that bookshelf ready."

"I will."

She closed her eyes. Within ten minutes her breathing evened out and her heartbeat settled into the slow rhythm of actual sleep.

Joel opened one eye from the tree. Looked at Marcus.

Neither of them said anything. They didn't need to.

Seventeen hundred miles to go. And somewhere in Colorado, Sarah was waking up to start another day, not knowing that the family she'd built was about to get one person bigger.

Marcus watched the embers die and waited for morning.
