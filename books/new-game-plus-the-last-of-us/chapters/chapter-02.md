# Chapter 2: First Blood

The intersection at Lamar Boulevard was a parking lot.

Cars jammed three lanes deep in every direction, bumper to bumper, engines idling, horns blaring into the night like that would somehow help. People were out of their vehicles, standing on hoods, shouting into phones that probably weren't connecting to anything. A pickup truck had T-boned a minivan in the middle of the intersection and both drivers were screaming at each other while their radiators steamed.

Marcus stopped at the edge of it all, hands on his knees, breathing hard enough to taste copper. He'd been running for... he didn't actually know. Eight minutes? Fifteen? The sprint from the Miller house had burned through whatever cardio Marcus Webb's body had to offer, and his lungs felt like they were lined with wet sandpaper.

The highway was close. He could see the overpass from here, maybe half a mile south, elevated concrete and guardrails lit up by the glow of headlights and emergency vehicles. I-35. The road where everything happened.

But half a mile might as well have been ten with this mess in the way.

"Move!" a woman shouted from a minivan, leaning on her horn. "Just drive over the median!"

Nobody moved. Nobody could move. A Chevy Suburban had tried to jump the curb and was now wedged sideways across the sidewalk, blocking even that. The gridlock was total.

He checked the phone. 10:31 PM. Battery at 28%.

In the game, the shooting happened late. After Joel and Tommy's car crashed on the highway. After they ran through the burning neighborhood. After the soldiers. He didn't know the exact minute. It could be happening right now. It could have already happened.

"It hasn't happened yet," he told himself. The quest was still active, still glowing at the edge of his vision. URGENT. If Sarah was already dead, the quest would have failed. Probably. He didn't actually know how the system handled failed quests and he didn't want to find out tonight.

He straightened up and looked around. He couldn't go through the intersection. Going around it meant cutting through more neighborhoods, adding minutes he didn't have.

He climbed onto the hood of a parked sedan, then up to the roof. The metal dented under his boots. Nobody stopped him. Nobody even looked. Everyone was staring south, toward the column of smoke rising over downtown, lit from underneath by a fire big enough to paint the clouds orange.

From up here he could plot a route. Through the parking lot of a strip mall on the far side of Lamar. Past what looked like a church. Then residential streets all the way down to the highway.

He jumped off the sedan, stumbled on the landing, caught himself, and kept going.

---

The strip mall was a Food Mart, a nail salon, and a tax preparation office. All dark, all closed. A shopping cart lay on its side in the parking lot. Glass from the Food Mart's front window was scattered across the concrete, and the store alarm shrieked on repeat, a flat electronic wail that blended into the background noise of sirens and car horns and distant screaming.

Someone had looted the place already. Fast work. The Cordyceps was still spreading through contaminated flour and people were already breaking windows.

He cut through the lot and around the back of the building. A dumpster that smelled like old produce, a chain-link fence, and beyond it a dark stretch of trees and grass that separated the commercial strip from the houses behind it.

He grabbed the fence and hauled himself up. One leg over, then the other. His jeans snagged on a twisted wire at the top and ripped. Something sharp bit into his calf. He dropped to the other side and kept moving, barely registering the sting.

A narrow dirt path ran between the trees. He followed it until it opened onto a residential street. Houses with yards, cars in driveways. Porch lights on. Curtains drawn tight. No one outside. The whole block had that sealed-up quality, like every family had locked their doors and turned the deadbolts and was sitting in their living rooms watching the news, not yet understanding that the news wasn't going to help.

A dog was barking somewhere to his left. Not the frantic, directionless barking he'd been hearing all night. This was different. Low. Continuous. A dog that had cornered something and was warning it to stay back.

He moved faster.

Three houses down, a sedan sat in the middle of the road with both doors open. Engine still running, headlights cutting white cones into the dark. No driver. No passengers.

There was blood on the driver's side door handle. A smear, still wet, reflecting the dome light.

He circled wide around the car and picked up his pace.

The dog stopped barking.

That was worse.

---

He was two blocks from the highway when the Runner found him.

It came out of a side yard between two houses, fast, making that wet choking sound he'd heard from the first one. But this wasn't like before. Before, the Runner had been thirty yards away and he'd had time to run. This one was close. Too close.

It was a woman, maybe forty, in a bathrobe and one slipper. The other foot was bare and bloody, toes scraping the pavement as she ran. Her mouth was open too wide, jaw hanging at an angle that looked broken, and a thick dark trail ran from her nose down her chin and onto the bathrobe.

Fifteen feet away and closing.

He didn't think about it. His hand went to his pocket and came out with the folding knife. Three inches of gas station steel. He thumbed the blade open and it locked with a click that felt ridiculous. Like bringing a letter opener to a bear fight.

Ten feet.

He sidestepped right, moving on instinct he didn't know he had, and she blew past him. One arm raked across his hoodie. Fingernails caught fabric and he felt the pull across his ribs but nothing tore through to skin. She stumbled two steps past him, hit a garbage can, knocked it clattering across the sidewalk.

Then she turned. Fast. Way too fast for someone with a broken jaw and one slipper. The Cordyceps didn't care about broken jaws or missing shoes. It just made the body go.

She came again and this time she hit him square.

They went down together on someone's front lawn. Wet grass, hard-packed dirt underneath. She landed on top of him, hands grabbing at his face, his neck, fingers smearing something warm and wet across his jaw. She was trying to bite him. Her teeth snapped shut an inch from his right ear and the sound of it, enamel cracking against enamel, was the worst sound he'd ever heard in either of his lives.

He jammed his left forearm under her chin and pushed. She was strong. Way stronger than she should have been. The Cordyceps did that. It ate the brain's limiters, the safety switches that kept muscles from tearing themselves apart. A Runner didn't know what pain was. Didn't know what "too hard" meant. It just pushed until something broke.

His right arm was pinned under her body. The knife was still in his right hand, blade out, pressing uselessly against the side of her bathrobe.

He jerked his arm free. Elbow scraped raw on the ground, skin tearing on a rock or a root or something buried in the grass. He swung the knife upward and stabbed into the side of her neck.

Not deep enough. The blade was too short, the angle was wrong. She didn't even flinch. Dark fluid, not quite blood, more like ink mixed with something thicker, ran out of the wound and dripped onto his chest.

Her teeth snapped again. Closer this time. He could smell her. Like mold and copper and something rotten underneath, something organic that had already started to go wrong.

He pulled the knife out and stabbed again. Higher. Harder. He aimed for the soft spot just below her left ear, where the neck met the skull, and this time the blade sank to the handle.

She stopped pushing.

Her weight went dead on top of him, all at once, like someone had cut her strings. A hundred and forty pounds of dead thing pressing him into the lawn. He shoved her off with both hands and a sound came out of his mouth that he didn't control, something between a grunt and a whimper. She rolled onto the grass beside him and lay still.

The slipper had come off during the fight. Both feet were bare now. Her toenails were painted pink. Little flowers on the big toes.

He sat up and crawled backward until his back hit the base of a metal mailbox. The knife was still in his right hand, slick and dark. His fingers wouldn't let go of it. His whole body was shaking, fine rapid tremors running through his arms and legs and jaw.

The blue notification appeared.

**[INFECTED ELIMINATED — Runner]**
**[+8 Points]**
**[First Kill Bonus: +5 Points]**
**[Total Points: 13]**

He stared at the glowing text floating in front of his face while a dead woman lay six feet away on a stranger's lawn.

"Thirteen points," he said. His voice came out wrong. Too thin. Too high. "I killed someone and I got thirteen points."

**[Correction: Infected are no longer classified as human. The host consciousness is destroyed upon full Cordyceps integration. No moral penalty applies.]**

"Thanks," he said to the system. "That's very comforting. Thank you for that."

It wasn't comforting.

He looked at the Runner. She'd been a person this morning. She'd gone to work or stayed home or picked up groceries. She'd painted her toenails with little flowers on them. And then she'd eaten the wrong thing, or touched the wrong person, or breathed the wrong air, and now she was a body on a lawn with a three-inch knife wound below her ear.

His stomach turned and he leaned sideways and threw up into the grass. Not much came up. He hadn't eaten anything in Marcus Webb's body. Just bile, hot and sour, and then dry heaving until his ribs ached and his eyes watered.

When it passed he sat back against the mailbox and wiped his mouth on his sleeve. The quest notification was still there, pulsing soft blue. URGENT.

He couldn't sit here. He knew that. Every second he spent against this mailbox was a second Sarah Miller was running out of.

He pulled the knife out of the Runner's neck. The blade was nicked, a small V-shaped chip near the tip. Dark stuff clung to the steel. He wiped it on the grass, folded it closed, and shoved it back in his pocket.

"Status," he said quietly.

**[Name: Marcus Webb]**
**[Age: 14]**
**[Points: 13]**
**[Health: 94%]**
**[Attributes: Strength 6, Agility 5, Constitution 6, Perception 5, Intelligence 7, Willpower 6]**
**[Skills: None]**
**[Evaluation: Average civilian. First kill achieved. Adequate.]**

Ninety-four percent health. The scrape on his elbow and the cut on his calf from the fence. Minor stuff. His hoodie had a dark stain across the front that he tried not to think about.

"Mall."

The shop interface opened, categories floating in that clean blue layout. He scrolled through with his thoughts. A weird process that was half-reading, half-intuition, like navigating a touchscreen with his brain.

Medicine. Bandages: 2 points. Basic first aid kit: 8 points. Antibiotics, one course: 15 points.

Weapons. Kitchen knife: 3 points. Fixed blade hunting knife: 12 points. Baseball bat: 5 points.

He had 13 points. He couldn't afford the first aid kit and a weapon. He could get bandages and a bat. Or he could get the hunting knife and have one point left over.

The folding knife was trash. It was a gas station impulse buy designed for opening Amazon packages, not stabbing infected in the neck. If the next Runner didn't go down as easy, or the next one after that, he needed something that wouldn't chip on the first hit.

"Fixed blade hunting knife," he thought, and confirmed.

The knife appeared in his right hand. No flash, no sparkle, no light show. It was just suddenly there, like he'd always been holding it. Seven inches of blade with a matte finish and a rubber grip textured for wet hands. The kind of knife you'd find in the camping section at Academy Sports. Not tactical. Not fancy. Just a solid piece of steel that wouldn't fold in half when he needed it.

**[Purchase: Fixed Blade Hunting Knife — 12 Points]**
**[Remaining Points: 1]**

One point left. Broke again.

He dropped the folding knife on the grass next to the dead Runner and stood up. His legs didn't want to cooperate. The adrenaline crash was hitting him like a truck, turning his muscles to pudding. He leaned on the mailbox and breathed. In through the nose, out through the mouth. A technique from a YouTube video about anxiety attacks that he'd watched in a life that felt like it belonged to someone else.

The quest pulsed. URGENT.

"I'm going," he said. "I'm going."

He pushed off the mailbox and started running south.

---

The last four blocks to the highway were the worst part of the night so far.

The military was here. Not rumors of military, not distant helicopters, but boots on the ground. He could hear diesel engines ahead, the deep idle of vehicles much larger than anything civilian. A Humvee drove past on a cross street, going fast, its mounted spotlight sweeping across houses and yards. He pressed himself flat behind a garden wall and waited until it passed, the beam cutting across the bricks two feet above his head.

Gunshots, close now. Not the scattered pops he'd been hearing all night from random directions. These were controlled. Three-round bursts, then silence, then three more. Soldiers shooting at something. Infected or civilians. In the first hours of the outbreak, FEDRA didn't always check which was which.

He saw his first body that wasn't infected. A man in a polo shirt, face down on his front porch, one hand still on the screen door handle like he'd been trying to get back inside. No visible bites. Two dark wet spots on his back, spaced six inches apart. Bullet wounds.

Someone had shot this man from behind while he was walking to his own front door.

Marcus looked at the body for two seconds, then three, then forced himself to look away and keep moving. There was nothing he could do. The man was dead and Sarah was alive and the clock was running.

The streets were emptier here. The residents had either fled or barricaded themselves inside. Abandoned cars dotted the road like dropped toys, some with their doors open, some with keys still dangling from the ignition. A pickup truck had jumped the curb and buried its front end in a hedge. The engine was still ticking as it cooled. A child's car seat was visible through the rear window. Empty.

On the far end of the block, a group of Runners chased a couple across the street. The woman fell. The man kept running. He didn't look back. Marcus couldn't see what happened after that because a house was in the way, but he could hear it. He wished he couldn't.

He ducked behind a parked SUV and waited until the sounds faded. The hunting knife was in his right hand. He was gripping it too tight, knuckles aching, but he couldn't make his fingers relax.

"Come on," he whispered. "Come on, come on."

The last block was residential houses and then the ground sloped up and he could see it properly for the first time. The highway. I-35. The overpass rose above the neighborhood like a concrete shelf, six lanes of elevated road on massive pillars. Orange light from sodium vapor lamps filtered down between the columns, casting long shadows across the access road below.

The sound coming from up there was bad. Engines revving, people screaming, the steady pop-pop-pop of small arms fire, and underneath all of it, the shrieking of infected. A helicopter circled overhead, its spotlight a white cone stabbing down onto the highway surface, illuminating an ant-trail of people trying to flee on foot between the stalled cars.

The access road leading up to the highway entrance was packed with abandoned vehicles. People had tried to flee Austin on I-35 and gotten trapped. Some of them were still in their cars. He could see a woman inside a sedan, banging on her window, mouth moving behind the glass, but the sound didn't reach him over everything else.

He ran along the shoulder, dodging side mirrors and bumpers, the hunting knife in his right hand and the phone in his left. 10:49 PM.

Where would the shooting happen?

In the game, Joel and Tommy and Sarah ran into a soldier near the overpass. An off-ramp or an underpass. The soldier got an order on his radio, something about orders to shoot civilians, to shoot everyone trying to leave the zone. He raised his rifle. Tommy shot him. But it was too late. The bullet had already hit Sarah.

Marcus didn't know which overpass ramp. Didn't know which underpass. Austin had miles of highway, and this stretch of I-35 had multiple exits and access points. He could be standing a hundred yards from the right spot or half a mile away. There was no way to tell.

He stopped and looked up at the overpass. Military vehicles on the highway above, visible between the guardrails. A Humvee with its light bar flashing. Another one behind it. And on the access road below, more headlights. An armored truck at the base of the nearest on-ramp, soldiers in silhouette around it.

"Where are you, Joel?" he said to the highway. To the smoke and the sirens and the orange sky. "Which way did you run?"

The quest gave him nothing. No marker. No arrow. No glowing trail on the ground. Just URGENT, pulsing like a heartbeat he couldn't slow down.

He forced himself to think. Joel's house was north of here. Joel would have driven south, toward the highway, toward whatever evacuation route FEDRA was running. Tommy's truck. They would have hit the traffic. The car that crashed into them. Then they ran on foot.

On foot, from a suburban street, heading toward the military. Because at that point Joel still thought the soldiers were safety. Still thought the army meant help. He would have looked for soldiers, would have headed toward the flashlights and the uniforms.

The nearest underpass was a quarter mile east. He could see it from here. A dark tunnel where the highway crossed over a smaller road, concrete walls and graffiti and the harsh white beams of military flashlights sweeping back and forth on the far side. Two soldiers. Maybe three. Hard to tell at this distance.

He started running toward it.

A Runner came at him from between two parked cars. He didn't slow down. He swung the hunting knife in a wide arc and caught it across the forearm. It staggered sideways, shrieking, and crashed into a car door. He was past it before it recovered.

**[Injured Infected — Runner]**
**[+2 Points (Partial)]**
**[Total Points: 3]**

He dismissed the notification without reading it.

The underpass was fifty yards ahead now. He could see the soldiers more clearly. Two of them, rifles up, flashlights mounted on their barrels, sweeping the road and the sidewalks. They were shouting at someone, orders to stop, to get on the ground, the kind of military commands that didn't leave room for questions.

Then a sound cut through everything.

A single gunshot. Not a burst. Not automatic fire. One shot, deliberate, from somewhere past the underpass, on the far side.

A pause. Half a second.

Then two more shots. A different weapon, sharper, smaller caliber. Two quick pops, close together.

Then nothing for a beat. Three seconds of something that wasn't silence but felt like it, a gap in the noise where the helicopter and the sirens and the screaming all seemed to pull back for just a moment.

And then a man screaming. Not in fear. Not in pain. In something worse than either of those. A raw, guttural sound, the sound of someone watching the thing they love most in the world break apart in front of them.

Marcus ran.

He ran harder than the sprint from the house, harder than the sprint from the Miller house, harder than anything Marcus Webb's body was designed to do. His vision narrowed. The underpass opened up ahead of him, dark concrete and sodium light and shadows. He was through it in seconds, boots echoing off the walls, and on the other side he could see the road opening up.

Figures on the ground. A big man kneeling, hunched over, holding something. Holding someone small. Another man standing a few feet away, a handgun at his side, staring down at a soldier who wasn't moving.

The quest flared in his vision, brighter than it had been all night.

**[QUEST: Save Sarah Miller]**
**[Status: CRITICAL]**

His lungs were tearing. His legs felt like wet rope. The hunting knife was slick with sweat in his grip.

A hundred yards. Eighty. Sixty.

He could hear the big man now. He could hear words between the sobs.

"Move your hands, baby... let me see... let me see, baby girl..."

Joel.

Marcus ran faster.
