# Chapter 21: Lights in the Dark

## — Tommy —

The turbine caught at 2:47 PM.

Tommy had been lying on his back under the generator housing for six hours, hands numb, knuckles scraped raw, a wrench in one hand and a flashlight clamped between his teeth. He'd freed the intake gate that morning with a sledgehammer and a bottle of penetrating oil Marcus had bought from wherever Marcus bought things. The penstock was clear. Water was flowing.

And then the turbine shaft started turning and the whole powerhouse shook.

Not violently. A low vibration that came up through the concrete floor and into his spine and his teeth and the soles of his boots. The hum of something heavy and mechanical doing what it was designed to do. The generator windings engaged and the voltmeter on the panel jumped from zero to sixty. Then eighty. Then one-forty.

Tommy slid out from under the housing and stood up. Grease on his face, oil on his shirt, grinning like a kid who'd just hotwired his first car.

"Joel!" he yelled. "Hit the main breaker!"

Joel was at the panel. He'd been standing there for twenty minutes, waiting, one hand on the breaker handle. He pulled it down.

The overhead fluorescents in the powerhouse flickered once. Twice. Then they came on and stayed on.

White light. Steady, clean, electric light that didn't flicker or sputter or smell like gasoline. Light from a river and a turbine and copper windings that some engineer had installed in the 1930s.

Tommy laughed. Actually laughed, out loud, for the first time since Austin.

"A hundred and sixty-eight kilowatts," he said, reading the panel. "Stable. Phase three is compensating like I thought it would. We've got power."

Joel looked at the lights. Then at Tommy. He didn't smile, exactly, but something in his face softened. The hard line of his jaw relaxed half a degree.

"Good work," Joel said.

From Joel, that was a standing ovation.

Marcus was in the doorway, leaning against the frame. He had that look he got sometimes, the one where his eyes went slightly unfocused and he was reading something nobody else could see. Then he blinked and was back.

"The residence too?" Marcus asked.

"Everything on the main circuit. Residence, maintenance building, garage. I can wire the outbuildings later, but for now, anything connected to the panel has power." Tommy wiped his hands on a rag that was dirtier than his hands. "We've got hot water if the heater in the residence still works. And I saw a chest freezer in the garage."

"Hot water," Marcus repeated, like Tommy had just described the concept of flight.

"First shower in forty-five days," Tommy said.

Nobody argued about who went first. Sarah won by default because Joel said so and nobody was stupid enough to argue with Joel about Sarah.

...

That evening, the dam compound was lit up.

Not like a city. Not even close. But the residence windows glowed yellow. The powerhouse had its overheads on. A porch light over the maintenance building door cast a circle of white on the gravel. And the single streetlight at the end of the access road, the one Tommy had wired on a whim because the fixture was already there, threw a cone of light across the pavement that you could see from half a mile away.

Half a mile. Maybe further on a clear night.

Tommy stood on the dam's walkway and looked at what they'd built. Four people, forty-five days, and a whole lot of points from a system he didn't understand and had stopped asking about.

The lights reflected off the reservoir in long, wobbly lines. The river below the dam churned white in the spillway. Stars above, lights below.

It looked like a place where people lived.

He went inside to see about that hot water.

## — Sarah —

Day forty-six. Valley entrance watch. Clear sky, cold enough to see her breath.

Sarah had been on the boulder since dawn. The rifle across her knees, journal beside her, canteen wedged between two rocks where it wouldn't roll. Same routine as every morning. Scan the road, scan the tree line, write something pointless in the journal, scan again.

Nothing ever came down the road.

She wrote:

_Day 46. Turbine is running. We have power. Tommy is acting like he invented electricity. Joel took a shower and came out looking like a different person. Marcus fixed the porch light and then stood under it for ten minutes just looking at it. I think he was having a moment._

_Hot water is incredible. I forgot what it felt like. I stood under the shower for twenty minutes and used half a bar of soap._

_The lights are on. You can see them from down the road. I don't know if that's good or bad._

She closed the journal.

The lights were a problem she'd been thinking about. On one hand, electricity meant safety, normalcy, the beginning of something real. On the other hand, lights in the dark were a signal. A beacon. Come here. Something is here.

The right people would see lights and think shelter.

The wrong people would see lights and think target.

She picked up the rifle and scanned the road through the scope. Nothing. Trees, frost, the curve of asphalt disappearing behind the ridge. A squirrel on a fence post.

Then she lowered the scope and looked again with bare eyes.

Three figures. Far down the road, maybe a quarter mile past where the county road joined the valley approach. Walking slowly. One of them was small. A child, maybe.

Sarah's stomach tightened.

She put the scope back up. Three people. Two adults and a kid. The adults were carrying packs, heavy ones from the way they walked. The child was between them, holding one adult's hand. They were moving carefully, stopping every few steps to look around. Not the posture of people who knew where they were going. The posture of people who'd been walking for a long time and didn't know if the next step would kill them.

No visible weapons. That didn't mean no weapons, but they weren't carrying rifles or shotguns in the open.

Sarah set the rifle down and cupped her hands around her mouth.

"Marcus!" she yelled toward the compound.

Nothing. Too far.

She fired the rifle once into the hillside above the road. The crack echoed off the valley walls and rolled away into the mountains.

Within ninety seconds, Marcus and Joel were at the valley entrance. Joel had the SIG drawn. Marcus had the Glock and the hunting knife.

"Three people," Sarah said, pointing. "Coming up the road. Two adults and a kid. Quarter mile out."

Joel took the rifle from her and looked through the scope. He watched for ten seconds. Fifteen.

"They ain't military," he said. "Civilian clothes. Packs are stuffed, not tactical. The man's limping. Left leg."

"Weapons?" Marcus asked.

"The man's got something on his hip. Pistol, maybe. Woman's hands are free. Kid's maybe six or seven."

Marcus looked at Joel. Joel looked at Marcus.

"Could be a setup," Joel said. "Send the family ahead, gunmen in the trees behind."

Marcus checked his map. Sarah had seen him do it enough times to recognize the look: the slight unfocus, the eyes tracking something invisible, then the return.

"No other contacts," Marcus said. "System shows three human signatures. Nobody else within map range."

Joel's jaw tightened. He didn't like relying on Marcus's invisible intel, but he'd never caught it being wrong.

"I'll go meet them," Marcus said. "You and Tommy cover me from up here."

"I'll come with you," Sarah said.

"No."

"Marcus."

"Sarah."

She stared at him. He stared back. Joel watched this exchange with the expression of a man who'd learned to stay out of it.

"Fine," Sarah said. "But I'm keeping the rifle pointed at them until you wave me off."

"Deal."

Marcus walked down the road toward the three strangers. Joel positioned himself behind the boulder where Sarah had been sitting, rifle steady on the rock. Sarah moved to a second position ten yards left with a clear line of sight. Tommy, who'd appeared from the powerhouse with a shotgun and an expression that said he'd been hoping for a quiet afternoon, took a spot behind a fallen tree.

Three guns on three strangers who didn't know they were walking into a crossfire.

The apocalypse had taught them that. Trust, but verify. Verify with overlapping fields of fire.

## — Marcus —

The family saw him when he was fifty yards out. The man stopped walking. His hand went to his hip, to the pistol Joel had spotted. The woman pulled the child behind her. They stood in the middle of the road and watched Marcus approach.

Marcus kept his hands visible. The Glock was holstered. The knife was sheathed. He walked at a normal pace, not fast, not slow.

"Morning," he said when he was close enough to talk without yelling. "Or afternoon. I've lost track."

The man was maybe forty. Thin face, dark beard grown out past the point of choice. Sunburned skin. His left leg had a makeshift splint on it, a piece of wood strapped to his calf with strips of cloth. His jacket was too big for him, the kind of thing you grabbed off a rack without checking the size because you were cold and it was there.

The woman was younger. Thirty, maybe. Dark hair pulled back in a ponytail that had given up on neatness a week ago. She had a backpack that was so overstuffed the zippers were straining. Her eyes moved from Marcus to the road behind him to the trees on either side and back to Marcus.

The child was a girl. Six or seven, like Joel had guessed. Brown hair in two messy braids. She was wearing a coat that was slightly too small and rubber boots with cartoon frogs on them. She stared at Marcus with the wide, unblinking look that kids get when they've been scared for too long and don't know how to stop.

"We don't want trouble," the man said. His voice was hoarse. Like he hadn't talked in a while and his throat had forgotten how. "We saw the lights."

"From how far?"

"Maybe ten miles. Last night. We'd been walking since Glenwood Springs. Saw the glow over the ridge and figured..." He trailed off. Figured what? Figured that lights meant people and people meant either safety or death, and they'd been walking long enough to take the coin flip.

"What happened at Glenwood Springs?" Marcus asked.

The woman answered. "The QZ collapsed. Three weeks ago. FEDRA pulled out overnight. Just left. Took the vehicles, took the medical supplies, took the food reserves. By morning, people were fighting over what was left."

"How many people in the QZ?"

"Maybe a thousand when we got there. A few hundred when FEDRA left. We don't know how many are still alive."

The man shifted his weight off his bad leg. "We headed south along the river. Figured there had to be something in the mountains. Somewhere away from the roads. Then we saw your lights."

Marcus looked at the girl. She was holding her mother's hand with both of hers.

"What's your name?" he asked her.

She didn't answer. Just looked at him.

"She hasn't talked since Glenwood," the woman said quietly. "She saw things."

Marcus nodded. He didn't ask what things.

"I'm Marcus," he said. "I've got three people with me at a dam up the road. We've got power, water, shelter. We've been here about a week."

The man and woman exchanged a look. The look married people give each other when they're having a conversation without words. She raised an eyebrow. He gave a tiny shrug that said, We don't have options.

"I'm Ray Haywood," the man said. "This is my wife, Lena. Our daughter, Mia."

Marcus waved toward the compound. Three hundred yards behind him, Sarah's rifle was pointed at Ray Haywood's chest. He didn't mention that.

"Come on up. Let's get you fed."

## — Sarah —

She watched through the scope as Marcus talked to the strangers. She couldn't hear what they were saying, but she could read body language. The man was scared. The woman was defensive. The child was... still.

When Marcus waved, the all-clear gesture they'd agreed on, Sarah lowered the rifle. Joel lowered his a second later. Tommy stood up from behind the fallen tree and brushed pine needles off his jeans.

"Looks like we've got company," Tommy said.

"Looks like," Joel said. His tone carried about six different opinions, none of them enthusiastic.

They met the Haywoods at the compound entrance. Marcus made introductions. Ray shook hands. Lena nodded. Mia hid behind her mother's legs and peeked out at everyone with one eye.

Joel studied Ray the way Joel studied everything: with complete attention and visible distrust. He looked at the splinted leg. The oversized jacket. The pistol on the hip, which turned out to be a .38 revolver with three rounds left.

"Where'd you get the gun?" Joel asked.

"Found it in a car outside Redstone. Guy in the driver's seat was already gone."

Joel looked at Lena. "You armed?"

"Kitchen knife in the pack."

Joel looked at Mia. Said nothing. Looked at Marcus.

"Let's talk," Joel said.

...

They put the Haywoods in the maintenance building with food, water, and a camp heater. Lena ate like she hadn't seen a meal in days, which she probably hadn't. Ray ate slower, methodically, watching the door. Mia ate a granola bar and then fell asleep on a cot within three minutes, curled on her side with her boots still on.

The four of them, Marcus, Joel, Tommy, and Sarah, stood in the powerhouse with the door closed.

"No," Joel said.

"Joel."

"They could be scouts. Send the family ahead, see what's here, report back to whoever's behind them."

"Nobody's behind them," Marcus said. "The map shows clear for two miles in every direction. I checked three times."

"Your map ain't perfect. You said yourself it missed that Stalker in the school."

"That was a hidden enemy inside a building. Open road, two-mile range, three scans. They're alone."

Joel leaned against the wall. Arms folded. The posture he used when he'd already made up his mind and was waiting for everyone else to catch up.

Tommy cleared his throat. "Joel, the man's leg is busted. The woman weighs a hundred and ten pounds soaking wet. And the kid's six. If this is an advance scout party, it's the worst one in military history."

"I didn't say it was a good plan. I said it could be one."

Sarah had been listening. She sat on Tommy's workbench with her legs dangling, the way she used to sit on the kitchen counter back in Austin. She'd been quiet since the Haywoods arrived, watching them the way she watched everything: carefully, with that cataloguing look behind her eyes that meant she was building a picture from details nobody else noticed.

"The girl's boots have frog faces on them," Sarah said.

Everyone looked at her.

"They're rubber rain boots. Size eleven kids. She picked them out herself. Nobody grabs novelty rain boots when they're running for their lives unless the kid is screaming for them and you grab them because the kid won't move without them." She paused. "They're a family. A real one. And they walked ten miles in the dark because they saw our lights."

Silence in the powerhouse. The turbine hummed its low, steady note.

"This is going to keep happening," Marcus said. "Tommy got the turbine running. We have lights now. People are going to see them. Some of them will be like the Haywoods. Some won't be. But they're coming either way."

Joel looked at the ceiling. Then at the floor. Then at Marcus.

"What are you proposing?"

"Let them stay. Put them to work. Ray's got a bad leg but he's got hands. Lena can cook, clean, whatever needs doing. The kid's a kid. They eat our food, but they also add eyes, hands, and another adult on watch rotation."

"And when the next group shows up? And the one after that?"

"We figure it out. We set rules. We screen people. We don't let everyone in, but we don't turn away a family with a six-year-old either."

Joel pushed off the wall. He walked to the window and looked out at the maintenance building where the Haywoods were. The light in the window showed Lena sitting beside Mia's cot, one hand on the sleeping girl's back.

"If they cause trouble," Joel said.

"Then we deal with it."

"If they bring trouble."

"Then we deal with that too."

Joel stood at the window for a long time. The turbine hummed. Sarah's legs swung back and forth against the workbench.

"Tommy?" Joel said without turning around.

"I say let 'em stay. We need people if this is going to be anything more than a campsite."

Joel turned from the window. He looked at Sarah.

"Baby girl?"

Sarah hopped off the workbench. "I already told Marcus to feed them. Twenty minutes ago."

Joel stared at her.

"What?" she said. "They have a kid, Dad. And the kid has frog boots. I'm not a monster."

Joel's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile but was in the same zip code. He shook his head once, slowly, and walked out of the powerhouse without another word.

Tommy watched him go. "Was that a yes?"

"That was a Joel yes," Sarah said.

## — Marcus —

He told the Haywoods that night.

They were in the maintenance building. Ray was sitting on a folding chair with his bad leg extended, the splint off so Lena could re-wrap the compression bandage. Mia was still asleep on the cot, one frog boot hanging off the edge.

"You can stay," Marcus said. "As long as you want. We've got room, and we'll have more room once we get the other buildings cleaned up."

Ray looked at Lena. That wordless married-couple conversation again. This time it was shorter.

"What do you need from us?" Ray asked.

"Whatever you can do. We're building something here. A community. It's just the four of us right now, plus you three. Everyone contributes, everyone eats, everyone's got a say."

"Four of you have guns. We've got three bullets."

"We'll fix that. Joel can teach you to shoot if you don't already know."

"I know how to shoot," Ray said. "I just ran out of things to shoot with."

Marcus pulled a chair over and sat down. "What did you do before?"

"Before the outbreak? Electrician. Commercial buildings, mostly. I did some residential."

Marcus looked at Tommy's half-finished wiring schematic pinned to the wall. Then back at Ray.

"We have a dam," Marcus said. "A working dam. The turbine came online yesterday. We've got a hundred and sixty-eight kilowatts and about two hundred feet of wired circuit. The rest of the compound still needs electrical work."

Ray straightened in his chair. The tired, beaten look on his face shifted. Something behind his eyes woke up. "What gauge wire are you running?"

"Twelve gauge for the main lines. Tommy's doing the work but he's an operations guy, not a sparky."

"Twelve gauge on a hundred-sixty-eight-kilowatt system?" Ray's eyebrows went up. "You'll melt the insulation on anything over twenty amps. You need eight gauge minimum for the trunk lines, ten for the branches."

"See," Marcus said. "That's why we need you."

Lena's hands paused on Ray's bandage. She was looking at Marcus with an expression he couldn't quite read. Gratitude was part of it. Suspicion was another part. Hope was somewhere underneath, buried deep where it couldn't get hurt.

"The girl," Lena said. "Outside. The one with the rifle. She's young."

"Sarah. She's thirteen."

"What happened to her?"

"Same thing that happened to everyone. The world ended and she survived it."

Lena finished wrapping Ray's leg and tied off the bandage. She looked at Mia, still asleep, still in the frog boots.

"We'll stay," she said.

...

That night, Marcus stood outside the residence and looked at the compound. Five buildings, all lit. The turbine humming in the powerhouse. Smoke from the wood stove in the maintenance building where the Haywoods were settling in.

Five people.

It wasn't a community yet. It was barely a campsite with electricity. But the Haywoods had come because they'd seen the lights, and they'd stayed because someone had offered them a chair and a meal and a job.

That was how it started. Not with walls or weapons or a system that dispensed supplies from thin air. It started with lights in the dark and someone saying "you can stay."

Sarah came out of the residence and stood beside him. She had a blanket around her shoulders and her journal tucked under one arm.

"Mia's asleep," Sarah said. "She hasn't let go of her mom's hand."

"She'll be okay. Kids adapt."

"Yeah." Sarah looked at the lights. "I know."

They stood there for a while. The river was loud tonight, running high from snowmelt somewhere up in the mountains.

"The lights are going to bring more people," Sarah said.

"I know."

"Some of them won't be families with frog boots."

"I know that too."

Sarah pulled the blanket tighter. "We need rules. Before the next group shows up. Screening process, probation period, designated housing away from the main buildings until we trust them. Joel's right about that part even if he's wrong about everything else."

"Joel's not wrong. He's careful. That's different."

"He's careful because he's scared. Don't tell him I said that."

Marcus didn't argue. She was right. Joel's caution came from a place of loss, not logic. Every stranger was a threat because every stranger could take away the people Joel had left.

"We'll write it down," Marcus said. "Rules for new arrivals. You, me, Joel, and Tommy. We agree on them together."

"And when we disagree?"

"We vote. Four people, majority rules."

Sarah snorted. "Joel's going to love being outvoted."

"Joel's going to love having a system where his vote counts. He's spent six weeks watching me pull things out of thin air with no explanation. Giving him a vote is the first normal thing I've done."

Sarah looked at him. That look she had, the one that was too old for thirteen. The one that said she'd already figured out what he was going to say before he said it.

"You're building something," she said.

"Trying to."

"Not just the buildings. Not just the walls. You're building a... a thing. A place where it matters how we do things, not just what we do." She frowned, frustrated with her own words. "I don't know how to say it."

"A civilization," Marcus said.

"Yeah." She looked at the lights again. "One family at a time."

She went back inside. Marcus stayed.

The porch light buzzed faintly above him. An insect circled it. Even at nine thousand feet in October, something always found the light.

**[Points: 615]**

He hadn't spent a single point today. Hadn't killed a single infected. Hadn't bought anything or cleared anything or farmed anything.

And somehow, today felt like the biggest point gain of all.

Five people. A working dam. Lights in the dark.

He went inside and slept better than he had in weeks.
