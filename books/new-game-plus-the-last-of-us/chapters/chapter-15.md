# Chapter 15: The Checkpoint

## — Marcus —

The road was empty for two days after the crossroads.

Not map-empty. Actually empty. No red dots, no blue dots. Just asphalt and fence lines and the occasional dead animal in a ditch. A stretch of West Texas that had been sparsely populated before the world ended and was completely unpopulated now. The wind blew from the northwest and tasted like dust and dried grass and nothing else.

Marcus liked it. Not in a way he'd admit out loud, but there was something calming about walking through a landscape where the map showed zero contacts in every direction. No decisions. No encounters. No moral calculations. Just four people and a road and the slow accumulation of miles.

They'd covered thirty since the crossroads. The terrain was flattening further, the Hill Country fully behind them now, replaced by the long shallow rolls of the Edwards Plateau. The vegetation was scrub mesquite and prickly pear and patches of buffalo grass that had gone brown in the October heat. A hawk circled overhead, riding a thermal, unconcerned with the end of civilization.

Joel was walking point. He'd been quiet since the crossroads. Not his usual quiet, which was the baseline quiet of a man who didn't waste words. This was a different quiet. Thinking quiet. Pete Vasquez quiet. The quiet of a man who'd been forced to interact with strangers and was now processing the fact that he hadn't hated it as much as he'd expected to.

Sarah walked behind Joel, close enough to talk if she wanted to, which she didn't right now. She had her journal out, writing while she walked, a skill she'd developed in the last week that involved holding the notebook against her forearm and writing in a slanted scrawl that only she could read. Marcus had asked what she was writing once. She'd said "none of your business" with enough humor to make it a boundary and not a wall.

Tommy was at the back with Marcus today. They'd swapped. Tommy had the Remington across his shoulders and was scanning the horizon with the habitual attention of someone who'd done it for a living.

"How far to Abilene?" Tommy asked.

Marcus checked the map. "About ninety miles. Four or five days at our pace."

"We going through it or around it?"

"Around. Way around. Any city that size is going to have concentrations. Infected, FEDRA remnants, or both."

"Agreed." Tommy adjusted the rifle strap. "New Mexico after that?"

"Yeah. Through the Panhandle, across the state line, then up through northeastern New Mexico into Colorado. The dam locations I marked are all in the western part of the state. Another four hundred miles after Abilene, give or take."

"Give or take." Tommy smiled. The smile was tired but real. "Four hundred miles. You say it like it's a grocery run."

"At this point it kind of is."

"Fair."

They walked. The hawk circled. The wind blew dust across the road in low sheets that looked like fog but tasted like sand.

Marcus was thinking about the Mall. He had 788 points and the settlement starter kit was 735. If he bought the kit now, he'd be at 53 points. Broke, essentially. No buffer for emergencies, no margin for the unexpected. But the kit included everything they'd need to get the dam operational: tools, a portable generator, welding equipment, water purification tablets, a full set of building hardware. Buying it now and carrying it in his inventory made sense logistically. Being broke in the apocalypse did not.

So he'd wait. Farm some more infected along the route. Build the buffer back up. Buy the kit when they were close enough to the dam that it mattered.

"Marcus." Tommy's voice changed. Not loud. Harder.

Marcus looked up. Tommy had stopped walking and was looking northwest, off the road, toward a cluster of structures maybe a mile away. From this distance they were shapes against the flat landscape. Rooflines. A tall pole. Something that might have been a sign.

"What do you see?"

Tommy already had the Remington scope to his eye. "Buildings. Looks like a rest stop. Maybe a gas station and a diner, something like that. Roadside setup." He adjusted the scope. "There's vehicles. Two... three. One's military."

"Military."

"Humvee. Tan. Standard Army issue. There's a truck next to it, civilian, a white pickup. And a van. Blue."

Marcus opened the map. Three blue dots at the location. And four more, slightly offset. Seven contacts total.

No red dots. All human.

"Seven people," Marcus said. "Map shows seven."

Joel had stopped. He'd heard Tommy's tone shift and turned around without being called. He walked back to them in that efficient, purposeless-looking stride that covered ground without appearing to rush.

"What."

"Buildings off the road, mile northwest," Tommy said. "Military vehicle plus two civilian. Seven contacts."

"FEDRA?"

"Humvee says so. Could be National Guard, could be deserters, could be a patrol."

"This far from a QZ?"

"That's what's interesting."

Joel took the Remington from Tommy and looked through the scope himself. He stood still for fifteen seconds. Twenty. Watching. Reading the scene the way he read rooms and roads and strangers' faces, with the patience of someone who knew that the difference between alive and dead was usually information.

"There's people on the ground," Joel said. His voice was flat. "Two of 'em. Sitting against the wall of the building. Hands behind their backs."

"Tied up?"

"Looks like it."

"The other contacts?"

"Three standin'. Armed. One near the Humvee, two by the door of the building. They're wearin' gear. Vests, helmets, the whole thing."

Sarah had drifted back to them. She didn't ask what was happening. She looked at her dad's face and then at Tommy's face and then at Marcus and read the answer from all three.

"Soldiers?" she asked.

"Probably," Joel said. He lowered the scope. Handed the rifle back to Tommy. "Soldiers shakin' down civilians. Takin' their vehicle, their supplies, whatever they got."

"Or protecting them," Tommy said. It was the optimistic read and Tommy knew it was unlikely even as he said it. But someone had to say it. That was Tommy's job. Float the possibility so Joel could shoot it down with evidence instead of assumption.

"People with their hands tied behind their backs ain't bein' protected," Joel said.

Nobody argued.

Marcus looked at the map again. The seven dots. Three clustered near the building entrance. Two against the wall. Two more inside the building. The ones inside weren't moving.

"We go around," Joel said. "South. We lose an hour, we stay out of it."

Marcus didn't say anything. He was staring at the blue dots against the wall.

"Marcus."

"Yeah. I heard you."

"We go around."

"There are people tied up over there, Joel."

"There's people tied up everywhere. Every day, somewhere, someone is gettin' robbed or beaten or worse by someone bigger and meaner. That's the world now. We don't walk into every bad situation between here and Colorado."

"We walked into the gas station."

"The gas station had a family with kids who needed food. This has armed soldiers with a Humvee. That's a different math problem."

Joel was right. Marcus knew Joel was right. Three armed soldiers in tactical gear with a military vehicle against two men with pistols, a hunting knife, and a bolt-action rifle. The math was bad. The smart move was to circle south, stay low, keep walking, and let whatever was happening at that rest stop be someone else's tragedy.

The smart move was also the move that let people die.

"Tommy," Marcus said. "What do you see?"

Tommy took the scope again. Longer look this time. Marcus watched his face change. Not dramatically. Tommy was too controlled for dramatic. But the muscles around his eyes tightened and his jaw shifted left, which Marcus had learned was Tommy's version of "I don't like what I'm looking at."

"One of the guys on the ground is bleeding," Tommy said. "Head wound. He's slumped. The other one's a woman, older, she's sitting upright but she's not moving. The three soldiers are relaxed. They're not on alert. They think they're alone out here."

"They are alone out here," Joel said. "Except for us. And we're goin' around."

"Joel." Tommy lowered the scope. "Those are deserters. No unit patches, no formation discipline. The gear is mix-and-match. One of 'em's in full kit, the other two are wearing tactical vests over civilian clothes. This isn't a patrol. This is three guys who took a Humvee and a bunch of guns and decided to play warlord."

"That changes nothin'."

"It changes one thing. They're not trained. Not properly. They grabbed the gear but they haven't got the discipline. Look at their positioning. All three are on the south side of the building. Nobody watching the north approach. Nobody on the road."

Joel stared at his brother. The stare lasted five seconds. In the stare was a conversation that Marcus couldn't fully follow because it was built on thirty years of being brothers and twenty of being adults and the specific shorthand of two men who knew each other's limits.

"You want to do this," Joel said.

"I want to not walk away from it."

"That's the same thing."

"I know."

Joel looked at Sarah. Sarah looked back. Her face was the face she'd been wearing more often lately. Twelve going on forty. A kid who'd seen too much in three weeks and processed it by becoming quieter and sharper and harder to read.

"I'll stay here," she said. Before Joel asked. Before the argument started. "I'll watch the packs."

"You'll stay behind the rise and you won't move."

"I know, Dad."

Joel turned back to the rest stop. A mile of flat open ground between them and three armed men with a Humvee. No cover except scrub brush and the gentle folds of the terrain. One window of surprise before it became a gunfight.

"Tommy, you set up on that rise." Joel pointed to a low ridge about four hundred yards from the rest stop. "You can see the south face of the building from there. If it goes bad, you put the first round into whoever's closest to the Humvee. That's the one who can get to the mounted weapon, if there is one."

"Copy."

"Marcus, you're with me. We come in from the north. The building blocks their line of sight. We get close, we get quiet, and we figure it out from there."

"Figure it out?"

"I don't have a plan past 'get close.' Plans are for people who know what they're walkin' into. We don't. We get close and we read it and we act."

It wasn't a great plan. Marcus knew that. Joel knew that. But Joel also knew that overplanning a situation with incomplete information was worse than underplanning it, because a rigid plan broke at the first surprise and left you standing there trying to recalculate while someone shot at you. A loose plan bent.

"Okay," Marcus said. "Let's go."

## — Joel —

They circled north.

It took twenty minutes. The terrain cooperated better than Joel expected. A shallow drainage ditch ran northwest from the road, deep enough to stay below the sight line of anyone at the rest stop, and they followed it for half a mile before cutting east through waist-high mesquite. Joel moved first. Marcus behind him. The kid was getting better at moving quietly. Not good. Not the kind of good that came from years of practice. But better than the stumbling, twig-snapping mess he'd been in the first week. He placed his feet where Joel placed his feet and kept his breathing even and didn't talk.

The rest stop was bigger than it looked from the road. A diner, closed, with a sun-faded sign that read "Sweetwater Junction — Steaks, Burgers, Pie." A gas station next to it, two pump islands under a flat metal canopy. A small cinder-block building between them that might have been a bathroom or a storage shed. A parking lot of cracked asphalt with painted lines that nobody would ever park between again.

The Humvee was parked at the gas station, nose out, facing south. The white pickup was next to it, doors open, contents scattered on the ground. Clothes, blankets, canned food, a red gas can, a cardboard box spilling shoes. Someone's life, dumped on the pavement for sorting.

Joel could see the soldiers now. Close. Fifty yards. He was pressed against the north wall of the diner, in the shadow of the building, where the afternoon sun didn't reach.

Three men. Tommy's read was right. Not a proper unit. The one by the Humvee was the closest thing to military. Full tactical kit, helmet, M4 rifle slung across his chest, boots that actually matched. Late twenties, maybe. Lean face, sunburn, a posture that said "I've been trained but I've decided the rules don't apply anymore." The other two were civilians playing soldier. Tactical vests over jeans and work shirts. One had a shotgun. The other had a handgun on his hip and was holding a tire iron.

The people on the ground were against the wall of the gas station. The man was maybe fifty. Balding, heavyset, wearing a plaid shirt dark with blood on the left side. A head wound, like Tommy said, but Joel could see now that it was worse than a cut. The man's left ear was torn. Someone had hit him with something hard, probably the tire iron, and the blow had ripped skin and cartilage. He was conscious but barely. Eyes half-open, head lolling, the kind of semi-awareness that came from a concussion and blood loss competing for which would shut him down first.

The woman next to him was sixtyish. Thin. Grey hair pulled back. Her hands were zip-tied behind her back and her face was the color of old paper. She wasn't crying. She wasn't screaming. She was sitting very still with her eyes fixed on the man beside her and her lips moving in what might have been words or might have been prayer.

The third civilian was inside the gas station. Joel couldn't see clearly through the dirty glass, but a shape moved in there. Another hostage, maybe. Or another soldier. The blue van was parked behind the gas station, hidden from the road. Its rear doors were open.

The soldier by the Humvee was talking to the man with the tire iron. Joel couldn't hear the words from fifty yards. But the body language was easy to read. The soldier was relaxed, one hand on the Humvee's hood, weight on one leg. The tire iron man was laughing about something. The shotgun man was going through the scattered supplies on the ground, picking up cans and putting them in a duffel bag. He tossed a can of something, peaches maybe, to the soldier, who caught it one-handed and set it on the hood.

They were taking their time. No hurry. No fear of being interrupted. Three armed men in the middle of nowhere with nobody to stop them and nothing to worry about except how much they could carry.

Joel turned to Marcus. The kid was pressed against the wall beside him, breathing through his nose. His hand was on the Glock. His face was doing the thing it did before a fight. Not scared. Not confident. Calculating. Running numbers that Joel couldn't see.

Joel held up three fingers. Pointed left, center, right. The three soldiers' positions. Then he pointed at Marcus and held up one finger. Then pointed at himself and held up two.

Translation: you take one, I take two.

Marcus nodded.

Joel pointed at the man with the tire iron, who was closest to their position, standing near the corner of the gas station with his back half-turned. Then pointed at Marcus.

Marcus nodded again. He drew the Glock slowly, thumb on the safety.

Joel had the SIG in his right hand. He'd left the pipe at the packs with Sarah because a pipe was a close-range weapon and this wasn't going to be close-range. Not if he could help it.

He held up his hand. Wait. Counted down. Three. Two. One.

He came around the corner.

The tire iron man saw him first. Or saw movement, at least, a shape coming around the side of the building where no shape should have been. His mouth opened. His hand came up, the tire iron rising in a reflexive gesture that was half-defense and half-confusion.

"Don't," Joel said.

The man didn't listen. People never did. The tire iron man turned and shouted something, a warning to the others, and Joel shot him in the chest. The SIG kicked. The man sat down. Not dramatically. Just folded, the way people do when something important stops working. The tire iron clattered on the asphalt.

The soldier at the Humvee was fast. Trained-fast. He had the M4 up and tracking in under two seconds, pivoting from relaxed to combat with the mechanical efficiency of someone who'd done the drill a thousand times. Joel was already moving, cutting right, putting the gas pump island between himself and the rifle.

A burst of automatic fire chewed concrete off the pump island. Three rounds, controlled. The soldier knew trigger discipline, which meant he wasn't panicking, which meant he was dangerous.

Marcus fired twice from the corner of the building. The first shot missed. The second hit the soldier's vest, center mass, and the man staggered but didn't go down. Body armor. The vest caught it.

The shotgun man was running. Not toward the fight. Away from it. Toward the blue van behind the gas station. He was twenty feet from the van's open doors when Tommy's Remington cracked from four hundred yards out.

The shotgun man's right leg buckled. He went down on the asphalt, screaming, the shotgun skittering away from him. Tommy's shot had hit him in the thigh. At four hundred yards with a bolt-action, that was either exceptional marksmanship or lucky aim. Knowing Tommy, it was the first one.

The soldier behind the Humvee adjusted. He knew where Marcus was now and he knew where the rifle shot came from and he was doing the math, which was the same math Joel was doing: two shooters in front, one at range behind, body armor stopping handgun rounds. The soldier dropped behind the Humvee's engine block, which was the right move, the textbook move, the move that turned a vehicle into a fortress.

Joel circled. Fast. Around the back of the pump island, along the side of the gas station, coming at the Humvee from an angle the soldier wasn't watching. The woman zip-tied against the wall saw him pass. Her eyes went wide but she didn't make a sound. Smart or too scared to speak. Either way, it helped.

Joel came around the front of the Humvee. The soldier was crouched behind the right front wheel, M4 braced on the hood, aiming toward Marcus's position. His back was to Joel.

"Put it down," Joel said.

The soldier flinched. His head turned. For half a second, Joel saw his face clearly. Young. Maybe twenty-five. Sunburned and hard-edged but young underneath it. The face of a kid who'd probably signed up at eighteen to serve his country and ended up here, three weeks into the end of everything, robbing people on a highway because the country he signed up for didn't exist anymore.

The soldier swung the M4.

Joel shot him twice. Once in the side, below the vest. Once in the neck. The soldier dropped the rifle and fell against the Humvee's wheel and slid down and didn't get up.

Quiet.

The only sound was the shotgun man screaming near the blue van, holding his leg, and the wind pushing dust across the parking lot.

Joel stood over the soldier. The young man's eyes were open and unfocused and his breathing was the wrong kind of fast, the kind that was slowing down instead of speeding up. Joel didn't look away. He'd stopped looking away from these things years ago. Or three weeks ago. It was getting hard to tell.

## — Marcus —

The tire iron man was dead. Marcus checked. Two fingers on the neck, the way he'd seen in movies and then in real life and the difference between those two things was something he still hadn't reconciled. No pulse. Joel's shot had been center mass and the man hadn't been wearing a vest.

**[No points awarded — human target]**

The system notification appeared and disappeared. Clean. Clinical. A line of text that confirmed what Marcus already knew: the system didn't care about human violence. No reward, no punishment. Just a blank acknowledgment. You killed a person. Here's nothing.

It was, in a way, more disturbing than the kill notifications for infected.

The soldier by the Humvee was dying. Joel was standing over him but not doing anything. Not helping, not finishing it. Just standing. The soldier's breathing got slower. Then it stopped. Joel walked away without a word.

Marcus went to the people on the ground.

The woman was still sitting upright. Her hands were zip-tied and her face was white and she was shaking, a full-body tremor that she couldn't control and wasn't trying to.

"I'm going to cut your hands free," Marcus said. He knelt beside her. The hunting knife cut through the zip tie in one stroke. The woman's arms came forward and she immediately put both hands on the man beside her.

"Gerald," she said. "Gerald, they're gone. It's over."

The man, Gerald, didn't respond. His eyes were open but they weren't tracking. The head wound was bad. Joel had been right about the tire iron. The left side of Gerald's skull above the ear was misshapen, swollen, the skin split in a ragged line that wept blood into his collar. Marcus pulled up the system and scrolled to the Mall.

First aid kit. 15 points. He bought it and the kit appeared in his hands and the woman didn't notice because she was looking at Gerald's face and nothing else existed.

Marcus opened the kit. Gauze, tape, antiseptic, bandages. He cleaned the wound as best he could. Applied pressure. Wrapped the gauze around Gerald's head. The man groaned. His eyes moved. Focused on Marcus for a second, then drifted.

"Gerald," the woman said again. "Stay with me."

"Ma'am," Marcus said. "How long ago did they hit him?"

"Hours. I don't know. They stopped us this morning. Took everything. He argued with them and the one with the..." She gestured vaguely. The tire iron. "He hit Gerald. Twice. He fell and they tied us up and left us here."

"Has he been conscious?"

"Off and on. He talks sometimes but it doesn't make sense. He said my name. He called me Betty."

"Is that your name?"

"Dorothy. He calls me Dot. Betty was his mother."

Marcus checked the wound again. Under the bandage, the swelling was pronounced. The shape of the skull was wrong. Not just a cut. Not just a concussion. The bone had shifted. Depressed fracture, probably. The kind of injury that needed a hospital, a CT scan, a neurosurgeon. The kind of injury that existed in a world where those things didn't exist anymore.

He could buy Tier 3 medical equipment. A surgical kit. Maybe something that would help. But he didn't know how to use a surgical kit. Having the tools was meaningless without the training. The system didn't download skills. It gave you things. What you did with them was your problem.

Gerald's breathing changed. Slower. Shallow. The intervals between breaths stretched.

"Gerald," Dorothy said. Her voice broke. Not dramatically. A crack, like a plate that's been chipped too many times. "Please."

Marcus looked at Joel. Joel was standing ten feet away, watching. His face was unreadable. Not cold. Not indifferent. Just the face of a man who'd seen this before and knew what was coming.

Tommy arrived from his overwatch position, jogging across the lot with the Remington. He took one look at Gerald and his pace slowed. He knew too.

"The one by the van?" Marcus asked.

"Shot in the leg. He's not going anywhere. I zip-tied his hands with the ones from the gas station." Tommy knelt beside Gerald. "Sir? Can you hear me?"

Gerald's lips moved. No sound.

"He can't hear you," Dorothy said. "He's been like this since... since the last hour."

Tommy looked at Marcus. The look was the look Marcus had been seeing more often. The look that said: you have the resources. You have the system. Can you fix this?

Marcus shook his head. Not dramatically. Just a small motion that answered the question.

Tommy closed his eyes for a second. Opened them. Put his hand on Dorothy's shoulder.

"Ma'am, is there anyone else? The gas station, is anyone inside?"

"Our daughter. Claire. They locked her in the bathroom. She's pregnant."

Tommy stood and went to the gas station. Marcus heard him calling out, then the sound of a door being forced, then a woman's voice, high and shaking, and Tommy's voice under it, steady, the voice he used when someone needed to hear that everything was going to be okay even when it wasn't.

Marcus stayed with Gerald and Dorothy. He held gauze against the wound and counted the breaths. Twelve per minute. Then ten. Then eight.

Sarah appeared. She'd come down from the rise. Joel saw her and didn't send her back. Maybe because the shooting was over and the danger was past. Maybe because he knew his daughter well enough to know she'd come anyway and the argument wasn't worth having.

Sarah knelt on the other side of Dorothy. She didn't say anything for a moment. Just knelt there. Then she took Dorothy's hand.

"I'm Sarah," she said.

"Dorothy."

"How long have you been married, Dorothy?"

"Forty-one years." Dorothy's voice was a whisper. Her eyes hadn't left Gerald's face. "Forty-one years last June. He made me a cake. From a box. He can't bake worth a damn but he tries every year."

Sarah held her hand. Didn't let go.

Gerald's breathing stopped at three-seventeen in the afternoon, by the system clock. Marcus knew because he was watching the numbers, the way you watch numbers when there's nothing else to do and the system is the only thing in your life that gives you clean, precise information.

**[No system notification]**

Nothing. No points, no achievement, no status update. A man died three feet away and the system didn't register it. Gerald wasn't infected. Gerald wasn't a quest objective. Gerald was just a man who'd argued with the wrong people about his own belongings and been hit in the head with a tire iron and died in a gas station parking lot in Texas while his wife held his hand and a stranger held gauze against a wound that nothing could fix.

Dorothy didn't scream. Didn't collapse. She leaned forward and pressed her forehead against Gerald's forehead and stayed there for a long time, and Marcus and Sarah stayed with her, and nobody said anything because there was nothing to say.

## — Marcus —

They buried Gerald behind the gas station.

Joel dug. Tommy dug. Marcus dug. The ground was hard, dry clay mixed with gravel, and it took an hour with the folding shovel Marcus bought from the Mall and a garden spade they found in the gas station's storage closet. The grave was four feet deep. Not six. Not proper. But deep enough that animals wouldn't dig him up, and deep enough that Dorothy could look at the mound and know it was real.

Dorothy and her daughter Claire stood at the edge. Claire was maybe thirty, dark-haired, visibly pregnant, maybe seven months along. She hadn't spoken much since Tommy got her out of the bathroom. Her eyes were red and she held her mother's arm with both hands and stared at the grave with the specific blankness of someone whose brain had decided to process everything later because right now was too much.

There was no service. Dorothy stood at the grave and said, "He was a good man. He taught math. He liked baseball and bad jokes and he always burned the toast." Then she said, "Bye, Gerald." And that was it.

Tommy had dealt with the shotgun man while they dug. The man's name was Kyle. He was twenty-two. He'd been a mechanic in Midland before the outbreak. He'd said, between sobs and apologies and pleas, that the soldier, the real one, had found him and another guy at a truck stop and offered them protection in exchange for help. Kyle said he didn't know anyone would get hurt. Kyle said he was sorry.

Tommy zip-tied Kyle to the Humvee's bumper and left him there. The leg wound was messy but the bullet had gone through the muscle, missing the bone and the artery. He'd live. Whether he deserved to was a question nobody asked out loud.

Joel had dragged the soldier's body behind the diner. The tire iron man too. He didn't bury them. Marcus didn't argue.

Marcus gave Dorothy and Claire the white pickup. It still had half a tank of gas. He loaded it with food from the Mall, water, blankets, a first aid kit. Fifty-two points total. He was at 721 now. Below the settlement kit threshold again. It didn't matter. Not right now.

"Where will you go?" he asked Dorothy.

"Claire's husband is in San Angelo," Dorothy said. "Or was. We were trying to get there when they stopped us." She paused. "Gerald wanted to keep driving. Right through the checkpoint. I told him to stop."

Marcus didn't know what to say to that. There was no right answer. Stopping was the reasonable thing. Driving through might have gotten them shot. Not stopping got Gerald killed anyway. Every choice in the apocalypse was a coin flip between two bad outcomes, and sometimes the coin landed on its edge and both bad things happened.

"San Angelo is southwest," Marcus said. "Stay on the back roads. Avoid anything that looks military."

"I know." Dorothy looked at him. Her eyes were dry now, and clear, and the expression in them was something Marcus hadn't seen before. Not gratitude. Not grief. Something harder than both. "You killed those men."

"Yes."

"Good."

She got in the truck. Claire got in the passenger side. The engine started on the first try. The pickup pulled onto the road and turned south and drove until it was a white dot on the flat brown landscape and then it was gone.

Marcus watched it go. Behind him, Joel was loading supplies from the Humvee into their packs. Military MREs, a box of 9mm ammunition, a pair of binoculars, a road map of Texas that someone had marked with red X's over certain towns. Marcus didn't want to think about what the X's meant.

Sarah was sitting on the curb near the gas pumps. Her journal was closed in her lap. She wasn't writing. She was just sitting.

Marcus sat down next to her. The concrete was warm from the sun.

"You okay?" he asked.

"I held a stranger's hand while her husband died," Sarah said. "I'm not sure what 'okay' means anymore."

"Yeah."

"Is it always going to be like this?"

Marcus thought about the next twenty years. About the things he knew were coming. The QZs falling, the hunters in Pittsburgh, David's group in Colorado, the Fireflies and their desperate math about one girl's life against the whole species. The human capacity for cruelty when the rules disappeared and nobody was watching and the only law was who had the bigger gun.

"Not always," he said. "But sometimes."

Sarah nodded. She opened the journal. Wrote something. Closed it again.

"Dorothy's daughter is pregnant," she said.

"I know."

"Where is she going to have that baby? San Angelo? What if there's nothing there?"

"I don't know."

"You know everything else." It wasn't an accusation. It was the flat observation of a kid who'd spent three weeks watching Marcus produce supplies from thin air and navigate without landmarks and know things he shouldn't know. "You knew where the infected were. You knew about the Clicker. You knew to come find us on Outbreak Day. But you don't know what happens to one pregnant woman driving to San Angelo."

"No. I don't."

"Good."

"Good?"

"It means you're not God. You're just... you." She picked at the curb with her fingernail. Pulled a small piece of concrete loose. "That's better. Gods don't hold gauze on people's heads."

Marcus looked at the road. South, where Dorothy and Claire had gone. North, where Colorado waited with its dams and mountains and the possibility of something permanent. East and west, where the empty landscape stretched in every direction, hiding whatever it was hiding, infected and soldiers and families and desperate people and all the violence and kindness and stupidity and courage that human beings were capable of when nobody was in charge.

He thought about points. About the system that rewarded him for killing things that used to be people and gave him nothing for killing people who acted like things. About the Mall, with its neat tiers and clean prices, a vending machine for the apocalypse, everything organized and rational in a world where a man could die because he told a stranger not to take his truck.

"I've been thinking about this wrong," he said.

"What do you mean?"

"The infected. The Runners and Clickers and whatever comes next. I've been treating them like the main threat. Like if I can kill enough of them and earn enough points and buy enough gear, we'll be safe."

"And?"

"And Gerald didn't die from a Clicker. He died from a guy with a tire iron."

Sarah was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Dad's known that from the start."

Marcus looked at Joel. Joel was closing the Humvee's rear hatch, having taken what they needed. He moved with the same efficiency he always moved with. The same calm, practical motions of a man who did what needed doing and didn't waste energy on philosophy.

Joel had known. Of course Joel had known. Joel had lived through the beginning of a world where people were the real monsters, and he hadn't needed a system or meta-knowledge or 788 points to figure it out. He'd just looked at the way people treated each other when the lights went out and drawn the obvious conclusion.

Marcus had been playing a game. Farming points, buying gear, planning the build. Treating the apocalypse like a strategy problem with a clear solution: build the base, level up, win.

But you couldn't win against people. People didn't follow rules. People didn't have stages or point values or neat stat blocks. People were unpredictable and scared and capable of anything, the best and worst of it, and no amount of system upgrades would change that.

The dam in Colorado wasn't going to be protection from infected. Not primarily. The infected were a problem, sure. A constant, grinding, dangerous problem. But they were predictable. You could map them. You could study their stages. You could clear a nest and know it stayed cleared.

People came back. People adapted. People showed up at your gate with tire irons or with pregnant daughters or with both, and you had to figure out which was which before someone ended up in a shallow grave behind a gas station.

"The settlement," Marcus said. "When we build it. It's not going to be about the walls."

"What's it going to be about?"

"People. Finding the right ones. Keeping them safe. Not just from infected."

Sarah looked at him. Her expression was the expression she wore when Marcus said something she'd already figured out, which happened more often than Marcus was comfortable admitting.

"Welcome to the apocalypse," she said.

Marcus almost laughed.

They stood up. Joel and Tommy were ready. Packs loaded, weapons checked, the rest stop already behind them. Kyle was still zip-tied to the Humvee's bumper, watching them leave. Marcus didn't look at him.

The four of them walked north. The sun was still up, three hours of light left, enough to put distance between themselves and the gas station and the grave and the blood on the asphalt. The road was straight and flat and empty.

Marcus checked the map. No red dots. No blue dots. Just the four of them, moving through a landscape that didn't care about points or systems or plans.

He was at 721 points. Fourteen short of the settlement kit. He'd make up the difference somewhere between here and Colorado. Some nest, some cluster, some encounter with things that used to be people and now operated on simple, predictable, killable programming.

The human encounters he couldn't predict. Couldn't plan for. Couldn't buy his way through.

Tommy fell into step beside him.

"You're quiet," Tommy said.

"Thinking."

"About what?"

Marcus looked at the road ahead. Four hundred miles of it, give or take. On the other side, a dam, and the beginning of something that wasn't just a base or a settlement or a survival strategy. Something that needed to account for the Dorothy-and-Geralds of the world. And the Kyles. And the soldiers who decided the uniform meant they could take whatever they wanted. And the Petes who just needed someone to show them how to clear a room.

"Walls," Marcus said. "And what goes inside them."

Tommy nodded. Didn't ask for more.

They walked north. The hawk was gone. The wind pushed dust across the road. Behind them, the rest stop shrank against the flat horizon until it was just a smudge, and then a line, and then nothing.

Ahead, Colorado waited. It wasn't going to be what Marcus had imagined. It was going to be harder, and messier, and more human than any game he'd ever played.

He kept walking.
