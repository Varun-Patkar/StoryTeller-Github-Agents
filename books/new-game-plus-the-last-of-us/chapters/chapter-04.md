# Chapter 4: Inventory

Marcus couldn't sleep.

He'd tried. He really had. Sitting on the kitchen floor with his back against the fridge, he'd closed his eyes and told his body to shut down, the way you tell a computer to restart when it's frozen. His body didn't listen. His brain was running at full speed, turning over the same handful of facts like a tumble dryer full of rocks.

He was inside The Last of Us. He had a gamer system. He'd saved Sarah Miller. Joel and Tommy were in the next room. The world was ending outside. He had 487 points.

One of those facts was more immediately useful than the others.

"Mall," he thought.

The blue interface opened in front of him, casting faint light across the dark kitchen. The glow didn't reach the walls or the ceiling. It existed in a space that wasn't quite real, hovering at the edge of perception, visible to him and nobody else. A private shopping channel for the end of the world.

He'd glanced at the Mall before. Quick, panicked visits to buy bandages and a knife while people were dying around him. He hadn't actually browsed. Now, sitting in the dark with nothing to do but think, he had time.

The categories floated in neat columns. He went through them one by one.

**Food & Water.** MREs, 3 points each. Canned goods, 1-2 points depending on size. Water purification tablets, 2 points for a pack of fifty. Bottled water, 1 point per gallon. Protein bars, 1 point for a box of twelve. Instant coffee, 1 point.

"One point for coffee," he said quietly. "Good to know the system has priorities."

**Medicine.** He'd already seen this section. Bandages, antiseptic, antibiotics, painkillers. The prices were reasonable for basic stuff. A full surgical kit cost 200 points. An X-ray machine was 800. A defibrillator was 500. He didn't need any of that right now. He scrolled down. Acetaminophen, bulk bottle: 3 points. Ibuprofen, bulk bottle: 3 points. Prescription painkillers: 10 points, and they came with a warning label that said **[CAUTION: Addictive substance. Use responsibly.]**

"The system sells oxycodone and tells you to use it responsibly. That's comforting."

**Tools.** Hammer, 2 points. Wrench set, 5 points. Screwdriver set, 3 points. Duct tape, 1 point. Rope, 2 points for fifty feet. A full toolbox with everything: 15 points.

**Weapons.** Kitchen knife: 3 points. His hunting knife had been 12. A machete was 15. A baseball bat was 5. Moving up the list: a 9mm handgun was 100 points. A box of fifty 9mm rounds was 20. A pump-action shotgun was 150. A hunting rifle with a scope was 200. Body armor, basic: 300 points. Body armor, military grade: 750.

He sat with that for a minute.

A handgun and two boxes of ammo would cost 140 points. He'd still have 347 left. A handgun, ammo, and the machete would be 155. That left 332. He could arm himself properly and still have enough to buy food and medicine for days.

But that was thinking small. That was thinking about tomorrow.

He scrolled to the section he'd been avoiding.

**Enhancements.** The Tier 4 stuff. Physical stat boosts. The cheapest one was 5,000 points. A single point of Strength cost 5,000 points. One point. From 6 to 7.

"Five thousand points for one stat point," he muttered. "And I get maybe eight points per Runner. So that's... six hundred and twenty-five Runners for one stat point. Cool. Very achievable. Totally reasonable."

He closed the Enhancements tab. That was endgame. Years away. Not relevant to a man sitting on a kitchen floor with 487 points and a sleeping twelve-year-old in the next room.

He went back to the basics and started doing math in his head.

Immediate needs. Sarah needed antibiotics. He'd already bought those. She needed painkillers. 3 points. She needed clean bandages for changing the dressing. 2 points. She needed food when she woke up, something gentle on the stomach. Crackers and broth, maybe 3 points total.

That was 8 points for Sarah's immediate care. Call it 10 for a margin.

Group needs. Four people, at least two days of travel to get somewhere safe. Food: 3 MREs per person per day, that was 12 MREs per day, 36 points per day. Two days: 72 points. Water purification: 2 points for tablets, plus they'd need containers. Call it 5 points for water. Flashlights: 2 points each, three of them (Joel could carry Sarah), so 6 points.

That was 83 points for basic travel supplies. Plus the 10 for Sarah. Call it 100 points total for the immediate future.

He had 487. Minus 100 left him 387.

Weapons. Joel needed a gun. Tommy had a handgun already, but limited ammo. Marcus needed something better than a hunting knife for anything beyond arm's reach. Two handguns (one for Joel, one for Marcus) plus ammo: 240 points. That was aggressive. One handgun for Joel plus ammo: 120. Marcus could stick with the knife for now.

That left 267 points. A buffer. Not a fortune, but enough to handle surprises.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. That's a plan. Sort of."

He dismissed the Mall and leaned his head back against the fridge. It was still humming. Still keeping someone's leftover casserole at a safe temperature. He wondered how long the power would stay on. Days, maybe. The grid would fail when nobody showed up to run the plants. Then the fridge would go quiet and the food inside would rot and nobody would ever come home to clean it out.

He was thinking about spoiled leftovers during the apocalypse. His brain needed a reset.

---

The living room was dark except for a thin line of streetlight leaking through the blinds. Marcus stood in the doorway between the kitchen and the living room and watched.

Joel hadn't moved.

He was still on the floor next to the couch, his back against the base of it, one arm draped over the cushion so his hand rested on Sarah's arm. Not holding. Just touching. Making sure she was still there every time his fingers twitched.

Sarah's breathing was steady. Slow and even and real. The silver emergency blanket caught the sliver of light from the window and reflected it in tiny crinkled stars across the ceiling. Her face was turned toward Joel, half-buried in a couch cushion, her mouth slightly open. She looked like a kid asleep on a school night, except for the blood on her pajama pants and the wad of gauze taped to her hip.

Joel was talking to her.

Not loudly. Barely a murmur. Marcus wouldn't have heard it at all if the house hadn't been so quiet, if the world outside the walls hadn't paused for just a moment between sirens.

"...gonna be okay, baby girl. We're safe now. Tommy's here. You're gonna wake up and I'm gonna make you breakfast. Eggs and toast. The way you like it, with the eggs all runny and you dip the toast in. Remember? You always get the yolk on your chin and you don't even notice and I don't tell you because..."

His voice broke. A crack, right down the middle of the sentence, and he stopped talking and pressed his forehead against the edge of the couch cushion. His shoulders moved once. Twice. No sound. Joel Miller didn't cry loudly. He cried the way a man cries when he's spent his whole life being the strong one and doesn't know how to do it any other way.

Marcus stepped back into the kitchen.

Some things weren't meant to be watched. Some things were private even when the world was ending, especially when the world was ending, because the small private things were all anyone had left.

He sat back down on the kitchen floor and stared at the dark ceiling and gave Joel his moment.

Tommy was in the armchair. Marcus could hear him shifting every few minutes, the creak of the cushion, the click of the handgun's safety being checked and rechecked. Tommy wasn't sleeping either. Tommy was keeping watch the old-fashioned way, sitting in the dark with a gun and his ears open, the way people had done for thousands of years before motion sensors and alarm systems.

After a while, Tommy got up. Marcus heard the armchair creak, then soft footsteps, then Tommy appeared in the kitchen doorway. He looked at Marcus on the floor. Marcus looked at him.

"Can't sleep?" Tommy asked.

"No."

Tommy pulled out a chair from the kitchen table and sat down. He set the gun on the table, within reach. His face was tired in a way that went deeper than one night.

"How many rounds you got left?" Marcus asked.

Tommy ejected the magazine and counted by feel, thumbing the top round. "Seven. Had twelve when we left. Used three on the soldier." A pause. "Two on the infected."

"I can get you more."

Tommy looked at him. The same look from earlier, the one that said "I saw supplies appear from nowhere and I have questions." But his voice was level.

"How?"

"The same way I got the bandages."

"Which was?"

Marcus didn't answer right away. The truth was impossible. A floating blue menu in his vision that dispensed items from thin air in exchange for points earned by killing fungus zombies. That wasn't an explanation. That was a hallucination.

"If I told you," Marcus said, "you wouldn't believe me. And you'd probably think I was dangerous."

"I already think you might be dangerous."

"Fair."

Tommy stretched his legs out under the table and crossed his arms. He was quiet for a minute. Outside, something popped in the distance. Could have been a gunshot. Could have been a transformer blowing. Hard to tell anymore.

"Joel's not gonna let this go," Tommy said. "The supplies, the way you just showed up. He doesn't trust easy and he doesn't forget. Once Sarah's stable and he's got room to think, he's gonna come at you with questions."

"I know."

"So what are you gonna tell him?"

"I don't know yet."

"Figure it out." Tommy leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Because right now you're useful. And useful keeps you alive. But useful and suspicious is a bad combination around my brother."

Marcus nodded. He understood. Joel would kill him if he thought Marcus was a threat. Not out of malice. Out of the same instinct that made him carry Sarah like she weighed nothing and talk to her about runny eggs while she slept. Joel's love was a wall, and walls kept things out.

"Can you get food?" Tommy asked. "For the morning. She's gonna need to eat."

"Yeah. I can get food."

"Water?"

"Yes."

Tommy looked at him for a long time. Then he stood up, took the gun off the table, and walked back to the living room. He paused in the doorway.

"Get some sleep if you can. I'll keep watch."

Then he was gone, and Marcus was alone in the kitchen again.

...

He didn't sleep. He sat against the fridge and listened to the sounds of the house. The tick of a clock in another room. The fridge humming. Tommy's armchair creaking. Joel's breathing, slow and heavy, the breathing of a man who'd fallen asleep sitting up because his body had finally overruled his brain.

And he thought.

He thought about what he knew.

September 27, 2013. Day one. The first night. Twenty years from now, Joel would be beaten to death with a golf club in a ski lodge in Jackson, Wyoming. Ellie would be there. She'd scream. She'd spend a year hunting Abby Anderson across a ruined Seattle and lose two fingers and her ability to play the guitar Joel taught her and every relationship she'd built along the way.

That was twenty years out. A long time. A lot of chapters between here and there.

But the timeline was fixed in his head like a map printed on the inside of his eyelids. He knew what was coming. Not the dates, not the exact locations, but the shape of it. The broad strokes.

Joel and Tommy would part ways. Tommy would head to the Fireflies. Joel would end up in Boston with Tess. They'd smuggle for years. Ellie would be born in 2019. Marlene would find her. The Fireflies would transport her west. Joel would be hired to escort her. Salt Lake City. The hospital. The lie.

And then twenty years of consequence.

"I changed it," Marcus whispered to the dark kitchen. "Sarah's alive. That changes everything."

But did it? Joel was asleep on the floor of a stranger's house, his dead daughter miraculously not dead, and the world was still falling apart on schedule. The Cordyceps didn't care that Marcus had bandages. The military didn't care. The Fireflies and the Hunters and David's group of cannibals in Colorado and the Rattlers in Santa Barbara, none of them cared. The world was going to break the same way it always broke. The same people were going to die in the same ugly ways unless Marcus was there to stop it.

And he was one person. One average person with a knife and a number on a screen.

"What am I doing?" he asked the ceiling. Not the system. Just the ceiling. Popcorn texture and a water stain in the corner shaped like Florida. "What am I actually doing?"

He had 487 points. In the game, in the world outside, there were millions of infected and tens of thousands of desperate people and a military that was going to collapse into martial law and a continent-wide fungal apocalypse that no gamer system was going to reverse.

He could save Sarah. He had saved Sarah. He could save Joel. Maybe. He could save Ellie. Maybe. He could build something, a place, a community, a spot of light in twenty years of darkness. Maybe.

Or he could die tomorrow to a Runner he didn't see coming. Or a soldier who decided he looked like trouble. Or an infection from the cut on his calf that he hadn't cleaned properly.

"One thing at a time," he told himself. The same thing he'd been telling himself all night. It was starting to feel less like a plan and more like a prayer.

He closed his eyes. Not to sleep. Just to stop looking at the ceiling. Behind his eyelids, the blue glow of the system interface waited, patient and steady, the only thing in this world that was entirely his.

He left it closed.

---

Something changed.

Marcus opened his eyes and the kitchen was grey instead of dark. Pale light filtered through the window above the sink, thin and colorless, the light of a day that hadn't decided what it wanted to be yet. Early. Maybe 6 AM. He'd slept after all, or something close to it. A shallow, restless half-sleep that left him feeling worse than no sleep at all.

He stood up and his knees popped. His back ached where the fridge handle had dug into his shoulder blade for three hours. His calf stung where the fence wire had caught him, and his right elbow was scabbed over from the fight on the lawn, and his ribs were sore from where the Runner had clawed at his hoodie.

"Status," he thought.

**[Name: Marcus Webb]**
**[Age: 14]**
**[Points: 487]**
**[Health: 86%]**
**[Attributes: Strength 6, Agility 5, Constitution 6, Perception 5, Intelligence 7, Willpower 11]**
**[Skills: None]**
**[Traits: Guardian — +15% healing effectiveness when treating others]**
**[Evaluation: Average civilian. One night survived. Keep going.]**

Health down to 86. Accumulated scrapes and bruises and the kind of low-grade physical damage that came from sprinting through an apocalypse without stretching first. Nothing serious. Nothing that wouldn't heal with food and rest.

Food.

"Mall," he thought. He bought six MREs, a case of bottled water, a pack of crackers, and a jar of peanut butter. He also bought a bottle of ibuprofen.

**[Purchase: MRE x6 — 18 Points]**
**[Purchase: Bottled Water (Case of 6) — 6 Points]**
**[Purchase: Saltine Crackers — 1 Point]**
**[Purchase: Peanut Butter (16oz) — 1 Point]**
**[Purchase: Ibuprofen (Bulk Bottle) — 3 Points]**
**[Remaining Points: 458]**

The items materialized on the kitchen counter. No flash. No sparkle. They were just there, arranged neatly, as if someone had set out a modest spread for a camping trip. He cracked open a bottle of water and drank half of it in one long pull. His mouth had been so dry he hadn't even noticed until the water hit his tongue.

He tore open an MRE. Beef stew. It was room temperature and tasted like salt and vaguely beefy sadness, but it was calories, and calories were what mattered. He ate standing at the counter, watching the light change outside the window.

The sky was wrong.

Not sunrise-wrong. Wrong-wrong. The light coming through the window had an orange tint that didn't belong to morning, and the clouds were low and dark and moving too fast, pushed by a wind he couldn't feel inside the house. He pulled the blinds up.

South. Toward downtown Austin. The sky was a wall of smoke. Multiple columns, thick and dark, rising from different points across the city and merging into a single grey-brown smear that blotted out the horizon. The tops of the columns caught the early light and glowed orange. It looked like the city was smoking a cigarette. A really big, really bad cigarette.

As he watched, a jet screamed overhead. Low. Fast. The sound hit the house a second after it passed, a physical thump that rattled the window glass. Then another one, further south. Then a deep, rolling boom that came through the floor more than the air, a sound he felt in his teeth.

They were bombing the city.

Not the suburbs. Not yet. Downtown. The dense areas where the infection had spread fastest, where the hospitals and the shelters and the evacuation centers had become death traps because you packed a thousand scared people into a building and all it took was one who'd eaten the wrong bread.

Marcus watched the smoke columns and ate his cold beef stew and tried to feel something appropriate. Horror, maybe. Grief for the people still in those buildings. Something.

He felt tired. That was it. Just tired.

"Joel." Tommy's voice from the living room, low and urgent. "Joel. Wake up."

Marcus walked to the doorway. Joel was already up, moving to the window, one hand still on the couch where Sarah slept. He pushed the blinds apart and looked south.

Nobody said anything for a while. The three of them stood in the grey morning light and watched Austin burn. The jets came back, two of them, streaking east to west. Another boom, further away this time. Car alarms going off somewhere, triggered by the shockwaves.

"We need to go," Joel said. His voice was flat and level, the voice of a man who'd already processed the emotional content and was operating on pure logistics. "Now."

"Where?" Tommy asked.

Joel turned from the window. "The QZ. They're settin' up quarantine zones. I heard it on that soldier's radio last night. There's one at the convention center downtown, and probably another one up north."

"You want to go toward the people who were shootin' at us last night?" Tommy said.

"They've got structure. Medicine. Walls. She needs a doctor." Joel jerked his head toward Sarah on the couch. "A real one."

Marcus had been standing in the doorway, quiet, holding a bottle of water and an unopened MRE. He set them both on the dining table and spoke.

"The quarantine zones are going to be bad."

Joel turned. That look again. Not hostile, exactly. Harder than hostile. The look of a man who'd decided to tolerate Marcus because Marcus was useful but who hadn't decided anything beyond that.

"You got a better idea?"

"I do."

"Then let's hear it."

Marcus sat down at the dining table because standing felt too much like squaring up, and squaring up with Joel Miller was a losing proposition even in a world without fungus zombies. Tommy sat too. Joel stayed standing, arms crossed, his back to the window and the burning city behind him.

"The QZs are going to turn into prisons," Marcus said. "Military law. Rations. Curfews. They'll take your weapons at the gate. They'll separate people. You won't be able to leave."

"You seem real sure about that," Joel said.

He was sure. He was sure because he'd played the game. He'd walked the streets of the Boston QZ twenty years from now, scavenged in its ruins, seen the ration cards and the execution notices on the walls and the bodies hanging from the overpasses. But he couldn't say that.

"It's what happens," Marcus said. "Every disaster. The military takes control, and control means cages. It'll be safe for a while. Weeks, maybe months. Then the supplies run short and the rules get tighter and the soldiers start shooting people for stepping out of line." He paused. "Sound familiar?"

Tommy's jaw tightened. He got it. He'd been on the wrong end of that soldier's rifle too.

Joel's expression didn't change. "What's the alternative? Live in the woods?"

"Head away from the cities. Small towns, rural areas. Find somewhere defensible with water and shelter. A house. A ranch. Something with walls and distance from the population centers."

"With what? We got one handgun, seven rounds, and a knife."

"I can get more."

Joel's eyes went flat. Here it was. The thing Tommy had warned him about. The question that was coming.

"How?" Joel said. One word. Simple. Heavy.

"I have resources," Marcus said. "I know it doesn't make sense. I know you saw things tonight that don't add up. But I can provide supplies. Food, water, medicine, weapons. And I'm not going to explain how, because you won't believe me, and it doesn't matter right now. What matters is getting out of Austin before the military decides this whole area is a kill zone."

Joel stared at him. The clock in the other room ticked. A jet rumbled somewhere in the distance, too far to rattle the windows.

"You expect me to just trust that," Joel said.

"I expect you to look at your daughter and ask yourself whether you'd rather have answers or supplies."

It was the wrong thing to say. Or the right thing to say. Joel's face went through something complicated and fast, anger and calculation and the raw open wound of almost losing Sarah, all of it passing behind his eyes in the space of two seconds.

Tommy spoke. "He's right about the QZs, Joel." Calm. Measured. The mediator. "And Sarah can't handle being processed through a military checkpoint. Not with that wound. They see blood, they'll assume she's infected."

Joel looked at Tommy. Looked at Marcus. Looked at the couch where Sarah was sleeping. The emergency blanket had slipped and her bare feet were sticking out, small and pale, toenails painted blue. The kind of thing a twelve-year-old does on a Friday night when everything is still normal and the biggest problem is that school starts on Monday.

"Fine," Joel said. "We go north. Away from the city. But the second I think you're a threat to my family, we're done. You understand me?"

"I understand."

Joel turned away. He went to the couch and knelt down and started checking Sarah's bandages. Tommy watched him for a moment, then looked at Marcus and gave a small nod. Not a smile. Not warmth. Just acknowledgment. The stranger with the impossible supplies had won another hour of rope.

Marcus went back to the kitchen and started packing. MREs in a plastic grocery bag he found under the sink. Water bottles in another one. Crackers, peanut butter, ibuprofen in his hoodie pockets. He bought a basic backpack from the Mall, 5 points, and loaded everything into it.

**[Purchase: Backpack (30L) — 5 Points]**
**[Remaining Points: 453]**

He also bought a 9mm handgun and two boxes of ammunition. 140 points. The gun appeared in the backpack's side pocket, magazine loaded, safety on. The ammo boxes sat heavy at the bottom of the pack.

**[Purchase: 9mm Handgun — 100 Points]**
**[Purchase: 9mm Ammunition (50 rds) x2 — 40 Points]**
**[Remaining Points: 313]**

Three hundred and thirteen points. Down from 487. Still enough for weeks of basic supplies if he was careful. Not enough for anything that would change the game. Not yet.

He slung the backpack over one shoulder and walked into the living room.

Joel had Sarah in his arms. She was awake.

Not fully awake. Her eyes were half-open, glassy, the look of someone seeing the world through a window smeared with Vaseline. But conscious. Her head was against Joel's chest and her fingers were gripping the front of his flannel shirt. She was pale. Too pale. But her breathing was steady and her eyes were tracking.

"Dad?" Her voice was small. Rough. Like she'd swallowed sand.

"Right here, baby girl. I got you."

"Where are we?"

"Somewhere safe. We're leavin' soon. You just rest."

Sarah's eyes moved across the room. They found Tommy, and something loosened in her face, a recognition, an anchor. Then they found Marcus, and the looseness went away. She didn't know him. He was a stranger in a bloody hoodie with a backpack and a knife on his belt and she was twelve and hurt and scared.

"Who..." she started, then winced. Her hand went to her right hip, a reflex, and Joel adjusted his grip to keep pressure off the wound.

"That's Marcus," Joel said. The words came out careful, like he was testing them. "He helped you last night."

Sarah looked at Marcus. He could see her trying to assemble the pieces. The pain, the confusion, the stranger, none of it fitting together into anything that made sense.

"Hi," Marcus said. Same thing he'd said last night. The only thing he could think of.

Sarah didn't answer. Her eyes closed and she turned her face back into Joel's chest. Not a rejection. Just exhaustion. The effort of being conscious was more than her body wanted to spend right now.

Joel stood with Sarah in his arms. Tommy checked the front window, then the back. He nodded.

"Street's clear. Which way?"

"North," Marcus said. "Stay off the main roads. We'll pick up 183 once we're past the suburbs and follow it out."

Tommy took point. Joel followed with Sarah. Marcus walked behind, backpack heavy with supplies and ammunition and a handgun he'd never fired and a number in his head that said 313 and a timeline of twenty years of disasters that he was the only person alive who knew about.

The morning air was warm and thick with smoke. The sun was a pale disc behind the haze, too weak to cast real shadows. Somewhere behind them, another boom rolled across the sky and the ground shuddered under their feet.

Austin was gone. Not destroyed. Not yet. But gone in the way that mattered. The city they'd lived in, the city of Whataburger and UT football and Friday night lights and twelve-year-old girls who painted their toenails blue, that city was over. What was left would be military zones and quarantine walls and the slow, ugly grind of survival.

They walked north. Past houses with open doors and empty driveways. Past a school with its flag still flying. Past a park where the sprinklers were running on a timer, watering grass that nobody would mow again.

Sarah stirred in Joel's arms.

"Dad?"

"Yeah, baby?"

"Where are we going?"

Joel didn't answer right away. He looked at Tommy, who looked at Marcus. Three men and a girl on a road out of a burning city, heading north because north was the only direction that wasn't on fire.

"Somewhere safe," Joel said.

Sarah's hand tightened on his shirt. "Promise?"

Joel pressed his lips against the top of her head. "Promise."

Marcus looked away. Down the road, past the empty houses and the silent cars and the sprinklers watering nothing, the road curved north and disappeared into a line of trees. Beyond the trees, more road. Beyond the road, more country. Beyond the country, twenty years.

One chapter at a time.
