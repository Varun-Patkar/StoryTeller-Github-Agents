# Chapter 25: What He Is

## — Joel —

Day fifty-three. The south perimeter.

Joel knelt on the gravel bar beside the river and tied a length of fishing line to a rusted tent stake he'd driven into the ground between two rocks. The line was thin, nearly invisible, strung ankle-height across the bank at the narrowest point where the Crystal River curved east below the dam. Six empty soup cans dangled from it at three-foot intervals, each with a handful of pebbles inside. Low-tech. Ugly. Effective.

Helen was ten yards upriver, rigging a second line at the waterline itself. She worked quickly, her knots tight and practiced. Military knots. Not the messy square knots Joel had seen from people who learned rope-handling from YouTube.

"Two lines," Helen said. "One at ankle, one at shin height. Stagger them by eighteen inches so they can't step over one without hitting the other."

"I know how trip wires work."

"I know you know. I'm saying it out loud so we're both on the same page."

Joel pulled the line taut and tested it with his boot. The cans rattled. Loud enough to hear from the boulder fifty yards north. Maybe not loud enough to hear from the compound itself, but Donna's watch position would pick it up.

"The secondary OP," Helen said, pointing to the granite shelf above the river bank. "I want to stack some brush around the base for concealment. Anyone posted there should be invisible from the gravel bar."

Joel looked at the shelf. Good position. Natural cover, elevation, clear sight lines down the bank in both directions. He'd have picked the same spot if Helen hadn't pointed it out first, which annoyed him in a way he couldn't justify.

"Fine," he said.

They spent the next hour building a concealed observation point on the rock shelf. Loose brush arranged to look natural, a flat spot cleared for sitting, a forked branch wedged between rocks as a rifle rest. Helen tested the sight lines from every angle, walking the gravel bar below while Joel watched from above.

"Can you see me?" she called up.

"No."

"Good."

She climbed up and sat beside him. The river below was clear and shallow, running over smooth stones. A trout held in the current behind a boulder, its tail flickering. Somewhere upstream, the dam hummed its constant low note.

"This was the biggest gap," Helen said. "Anyone coming from the south would use this bank. Flat ground, cover from the ridge, no sight lines from the tree stand. Now they hit wire, make noise, and walk into a prepared position."

Joel didn't answer. He was looking south, down the river valley, at the miles of forest and rock between the compound and wherever the next threat was sleeping.

"It's good work," Joel said. It came out stiffer than he intended, but Helen didn't seem to need it softer.

"The perimeter's not perfect," she said. "But it's competent. That matters."

Joel stood up and brushed gravel off his knees. "Let's get back. Tommy wants help with the conduit run this afternoon."

They walked back to the compound in the kind of silence Joel preferred. Working silence. No wasted words. Helen kept pace without trying to fill the quiet with conversation, which put her ahead of most people Joel had met in fifty-three days.

## — Marcus —

Marcus was in the residence kitchen at ten PM, alone, running the Mall numbers.

The bourbon from two nights ago was gone. Joel had taken the bottle to his room and Marcus hadn't asked for it back. That was fine. The bourbon had served its purpose, and at twelve points, it was the cheapest political investment he'd made since the council formed.

**[Points: 431]**

He needed to do a clearing run tomorrow. South this time. The map showed a cluster of eight contacts near a trailhead four miles downstream. Standard Runners, all orange. An easy hundred points if he was clean about it.

He was reviewing the Mall's tool prices when the map flickered.

Not the Mall. The map. The overlay that existed in the corner of his vision like a transparent radar screen, showing contacts within a five-mile radius. Orange dots for infected. And now, for the first time since the crossroads general store back in Texas, something else.

Blue dots. Seven of them. Clustered tight, moving together, coming up the river valley from the south.

Blue meant human.

Marcus closed the Mall and expanded the map. The seven contacts were two miles out, moving at walking pace along the river bank. Not the road. The bank. The same approach Helen had flagged in her assessment yesterday. The blind spot that Joel had spent all day closing with trip wires and a new observation point.

Two miles at walking pace. Thirty, maybe forty minutes.

Marcus checked the time. 10:14 PM. Donna was on watch at the boulder. Helen was sleeping in the maintenance building. Joel was in his room. Everyone else was down for the night.

He grabbed the Glock from the shelf, checked the magazine, and pulled on his jacket. The hunting knife went on his belt. The hatchet went in the jacket's inside pocket, handle down.

He stopped at Joel's door and knocked once. Soft.

Joel opened it in three seconds. Fully dressed. Boots on. The SIG was already in his hand. Joel Miller didn't sleep. He waited in the dark with his shoes on.

"Seven contacts," Marcus said. "Human. Coming up the river from the south. Two miles out."

Joel's expression didn't change. "How do you know?"

"Same way I always know."

Joel stared at him for one second. Then he reached behind the door and picked up the rifle.

"Wake Tommy," Joel said.

"No. Tommy stays with the compound. If this goes wrong, someone needs to be here."

Joel considered that. He didn't like it, but he didn't argue.

They moved through the compound in the dark. Marcus stopped at the boulder and told Donna. She didn't ask questions. She shifted the rifle to her shoulder and faced south.

"I'm going to the secondary OP," Marcus said. "Helen's rock shelf. I'll have sight lines on the gravel bar."

"And me?" Joel asked.

"Tree line, east side. You'll have their flank if they make it past the wire."

"If?"

"When."

Joel disappeared into the trees without a sound. Marcus climbed the granite shelf and settled into the concealed position Helen had built that morning. The brush screening worked. From below, the shelf looked like bare rock and scrub.

He lay flat and waited.

...

They came at 10:47 PM.

Marcus saw them on the map first, then heard them. Boots on gravel. Whispered conversation that carried on the still air. The river covered some of it, but not all.

Seven men. Armed. Three rifles visible, two shotguns, and what looked like a baseball bat and a crowbar on the last two. They moved in a loose column along the river bank, staying below the ridge line, using the terrain exactly the way Helen had predicted.

The lead man was big. Six-two, heavy build, wearing a hunting jacket and carrying a bolt-action rifle with a scope. He moved with confidence, like someone who'd done this before. Not a desperate refugee. A raider. Someone who'd seen the lights from the highway and come to take what was behind them.

They were thirty yards from the first trip wire when the leader stopped. He held up a fist. The column halted.

He'd seen something. Maybe the line of cans in the moonlight. Maybe just instinct. He crouched and waved two men forward.

The scouts moved ahead, slower now, scanning the ground. The first one found the trip wire. He knelt beside it, traced the fishing line with his fingers, and turned back to the leader.

"Wired," he said. Quiet, but Marcus heard it from the shelf.

The leader smiled. It wasn't a pleasant expression.

"Cut it," he said. "Whoever's up there thinks soup cans are security."

The scout pulled a knife and cut the line. The cans dropped silently into the gravel.

Marcus watched them cut the second line the same way. Professional. Practiced. These weren't desperate people. These were people who had figured out that taking from the desperate was easier than surviving on your own.

They came forward. Past the trip wires. Onto the gravel bar below the dam. The leader was scanning the compound above, counting buildings, assessing the layout. Planning.

Marcus stood up on the rock shelf.

"That's far enough."

Seven heads turned. The leader's rifle came up fast. The two scouts fanned left. The others spread out instinctively, covering angles. These people had done this before.

"We don't want trouble," the leader said. His voice was calm, reasonable. The voice of a man who always started with the polite version. "We saw the lights. Figured there might be people who could share."

"There are people. They're not sharing."

"That's not real friendly."

"No, it isn't."

The leader took a step forward. "Look, we've been walking for two weeks. We're hungry, we're tired, and we're not asking for much. Food, water, a place to sleep. We'll move on in the morning."

Marcus had heard this script before. Not personally, but he'd played enough of the game. He'd read enough fan fiction. He'd watched enough playthroughs to know how this went. The polite request, the reasonable tone, the slow advance. And then the guns came out and the polite version ended and people died.

"You cut our trip wires," Marcus said. "You came up the blind approach at eleven at night with seven armed men. You're not asking for help. You're scouting."

The leader's smile faded.

"Walk south. Don't come back. That's the offer."

The leader looked at the man to his left. Something passed between them. A look, a nod, a decision that happened faster than words.

The scout on the left raised his shotgun.

Marcus moved.

He dropped off the rock shelf. Eight feet to the gravel bar below. He hit the ground rolling and came up inside the shotgun's effective range before the scout could track him. The hatchet was in his hand. He swung it into the stock of the shotgun, knocking the barrel sideways, and followed through with a punch to the scout's jaw that snapped his head back and dropped him to the gravel.

The second scout lunged with the crowbar. Marcus ducked under the swing, grabbed the man's wrist, twisted, and threw him into the first scout's falling body. They went down in a tangle of limbs.

Gunshot. The leader had fired. The bullet went past Marcus's left ear, close enough to feel the air pressure. Marcus closed the distance in three strides, caught the rifle barrel with his left hand, and ripped it sideways out of the leader's grip. The leader tried to draw a handgun from his belt. Marcus hit him in the sternum with the butt of his own rifle. The man folded.

Four seconds. Three down.

The remaining four had spread. One was backing away, hands up, the baseball bat dropped on the gravel. Three others were trying to find shooting angles that didn't risk hitting their own people. One of them got a shot off. It hit Marcus in the left shoulder, a hot punch that spun him sideways.

He didn't go down. The pain was there, sharp and immediate, but something else was there too. Something underneath the pain that kept his legs moving and his hands working. Not adrenaline. Adrenaline made you shaky and wild. This was steady. Focused. Like his body had decided the bullet wound was a problem for later and right now the only thing that mattered was the three men still holding weapons.

Marcus charged the shooter. The man tried to cycle the bolt on his hunting rifle and Marcus reached him before he could. The hatchet took the rifle out of his hands. A knee to the stomach bent him double. Marcus caught him by the collar and threw him sideways into the man beside him. They collided and went down.

From the tree line, Joel's rifle cracked twice. The last armed raider, the one trying to circle behind Marcus, pitched forward and didn't get up. The seventh man, the one who'd dropped his bat, was running south along the river bank as fast as his legs would carry him.

Joel didn't shoot him. Marcus didn't chase him.

Seven men. Twelve seconds. Maybe fifteen. Marcus wasn't sure. Time had gone strange. Not slow-motion, nothing cinematic. Just compressed. Like the space between heartbeats had gotten wider and he'd fit more movement into each one.

He stood on the gravel bar. Five men on the ground around him. Two from Joel's shots. Five from Marcus.

The leader was trying to get up, holding his chest where the rifle butt had hit him. Marcus put a boot on his shoulder and pressed him back down.

"I said walk south. You didn't listen."

The leader looked up at him. The confidence was gone. What was left was something raw and simple. Fear.

"How..." the leader said. "You're one guy."

Marcus didn't answer that. The question didn't have an answer he could give.

## — Donna —

Donna heard the shots from the boulder.

Three in rapid succession, then a pause, then two more from a different weapon. Rifle versus handgun. She knew the difference. Twenty years as a park ranger, most of it in bear country, had taught her what firearms sounded like at distance.

She was already moving toward the secondary OP when Carson appeared behind her, shotgun in hand, wearing a t-shirt and unlaced boots. He'd come from the maintenance building at a run.

"Stay here," Donna told him. "Cover the road."

"What's happening?"

"I don't know yet."

She went south along the ridge trail, staying high, using the tree line for cover. Tommy was behind her somewhere. She could hear his boots on pine needles, quick and controlled.

The gravel bar came into view from above.

Donna stopped. Tommy stopped beside her.

Five men were on the ground. Two more lay further out, near the tree line, where Joel's shots had dropped them. Weapons scattered on the gravel. A rifle, a shotgun, a crowbar, a baseball bat.

Marcus stood in the middle of it.

He was holding a hatchet in one hand and the leader's bolt-action rifle in the other. Blood was running down his left arm from a wound in his shoulder. His jacket was torn. His breathing was hard but even. Not panicked. Not shaking.

Donna had seen people after fights. Rangers, hunters, even a poacher who'd gotten into it with a game warden back in '09. They were always shaky afterward. The hands trembled. The eyes went wide. The body dumped adrenaline and the crash came fast and ugly.

Marcus wasn't shaking.

He was talking to the leader, who was on the ground holding his chest, and his voice was level. Like he'd finished a jog, not a fight with seven armed men.

Tommy said what Donna was thinking.

"How did he do that?"

She didn't have an answer. She'd known Marcus for three days. She'd seen him carry lumber that two men should have carried. She'd seen him walk into the forest alone to fight infected and come back without a scratch. She'd filed it under "young and fit" because that was the easy explanation.

The easy explanation didn't cover this.

From the boulder, Carson had a view of the gravel bar through the scope on the shotgun. Helen was beside him now, woken by the shots, standing in her boots and a jacket pulled over her sleeping clothes. She had the professional calm of someone who'd heard gunfire before, but her eyes were on Marcus, and they were doing the thing that Donna had seen on Helen's face during the council meeting.

Assessing.

Joel emerged from the tree line. He walked to Marcus, checked his shoulder wound, said something too quiet to hear. Marcus nodded. Joel looked at the men on the ground. Then he looked up at the ridge where Donna and Tommy were standing.

The look on Joel's face was complicated. Joel had fought beside Marcus before. He knew what the boy could do. But from up here, Donna could see that even Joel was recalculating something. The scale had shifted.

Tommy started down the ridge toward the gravel bar. Donna followed.

By the time they reached the bottom, Marcus had the leader and the three conscious raiders sitting against the river bank rocks, hands visible. Joel stood over them with the SIG drawn and the expression he used on people he was deciding about. The two men Joel had shot were being checked by Helen. One was unconscious. The other was dead.

"Six alive, one dead," Helen said. She said it the way she said everything. Factual. Measured. "The unconscious one has a shoulder wound. Needs treatment or he bleeds out."

Marcus looked at the wounded raider. He was quiet for a moment. Then he turned away from the group, and Donna saw him do something with his hands, just for a second, a small motion near his jacket. When he turned back, he had a medical kit. Gauze, tape, a packet of hemostatic powder.

He hadn't been carrying a medical kit. Donna was sure of that.

Marcus tossed the kit to Helen. "Patch him up."

Helen caught it and went to work without comment. She'd seen the same thing Donna had. The medical kit that appeared from nowhere. She didn't ask about it. But her mouth was a thin line, and her eyes moved from the kit to Marcus and back again.

## — Marcus —

Marcus's shoulder hurt.

Not badly. The bullet had gone through the deltoid muscle, in one side and out the other. Clean. No bone, no artery, nothing critical. It bled a lot, which looked dramatic, and it burned, which was annoying, but he could still use the arm. He'd need antibiotics and proper bandaging, but the immediate crisis was the six living raiders sitting against the rocks.

**[Combat Encounter Complete]**
**[Hostiles Neutralized: 7]**
**[Note: Human combatants. No points awarded.]**
**[Achievement Unlocked: Defender — Protect your settlement from a hostile group]**
**[Reward: +50 points, Settlement Defense Rating upgraded]**
**[Points: 481]**

No points for the kills themselves. The system still didn't reward human violence. But it rewarded defense. Protecting the settlement counted. Fifty points for keeping fourteen people alive.

"What do we do with them?" Tommy asked.

It was the right question. Six men, five of whom had come to rob them, one of whom was bleeding from Joel's bullet. Sending them south meant they'd come back, or they'd tell others that the compound was weak. Keeping them meant feeding them and watching them. Killing them was what the old world would have done, what Joel's face said he was considering, what the practical part of Marcus's brain understood.

"We send them west," Marcus said. "Over the ridge. Not south, not north. West, into the high country, away from the roads. They take their dead. They take enough food for three days. They don't take weapons."

Joel looked at him. "They'll come back."

"Maybe. But they'll remember what happened, and they'll tell anyone they meet what happens when you come up this river at night."

"You're countin' on fear."

"I'm counting on self-preservation. There's a difference."

Joel's jaw worked. He didn't argue. After a long moment, he holstered the SIG.

"Your call."

They sent the raiders west at dawn. Six men, one body wrapped in a tarp, three days of MREs, no weapons. The leader didn't look at Marcus as he left. None of them did. They walked into the tree line and disappeared up the ridge trail without a word.

Marcus watched them go. Then he sat down on a rock and let Helen bandage his shoulder.

"Through-and-through," Helen said, cutting his jacket sleeve away. "Missed the bone. You'll want antibiotics."

"I've got some."

"Of course you do." She pulled the gauze tight. It hurt. She didn't apologize. "I've seen men shot in the shoulder before. At Carson. Training accident, live-fire exercise. The soldier went into shock. Couldn't stand, couldn't talk, vomited twice." She tied off the bandage. "You fought five more men after taking this hit. You didn't slow down."

It wasn't a question. Marcus didn't answer it like one.

"Adrenaline," he said.

Helen looked at him. It was the same look she'd given the perimeter yesterday. Assessment. Cataloging what worked and what didn't add up.

"Sure," she said. "Adrenaline."

## — Sarah —

Sarah found out at dawn.

She'd slept through the gunshots, which bothered her more than the gunshots themselves. She was a light sleeper. She'd trained herself to wake at any sound since the farmhouse in Texas. But she'd taken one of Gene's over-the-counter sleep aids last night because her hip had been aching from two days of garden work, and she'd missed the only gunfire the compound had ever seen.

Joel told her. Briefly, the way Joel told things, with the facts arranged in order of importance and the emotions left out entirely.

"Seven men came up the river. Marcus and I handled it. One dead, six sent west. Marcus took a bullet in the shoulder. He's fine."

"He's fine," Sarah repeated.

"Through-and-through. Helen patched him."

Sarah looked at her father. Joel was cleaning the rifle at the kitchen table, Frank's pine table, running a cloth through the barrel with the practiced ease of a man who'd cleaned thousands of rifles. His face was the neutral one. The wall.

"Dad."

"Hm."

"What aren't you telling me?"

Joel set the cloth down. He looked at her. Not the neutral face now. Something else. Something she'd seen twice before. Once at the school with the Stalkers, when Marcus had moved through the hallways like he'd done it a hundred times. And once at the crossroads general store, when Marcus had stood up as bait and drawn five armed men toward him without flinching.

"He took five of 'em," Joel said. "By himself. In the dark. I got two from the tree line. But the five on the gravel bar were all him. The whole thing was maybe fifteen seconds."

"That's fast."

"Sarah." Joel's voice was quiet. The voice he used when he was being careful with words, when the truth was something he needed to set down gently. "I've been in fights. Real ones. Ugly ones. Before the outbreak and after. I've fought alongside soldiers, smugglers, hunters. People who knew what they were doin'. What Marcus did on that gravel bar last night wasn't normal. Not for a trained fighter. Not for anybody."

Sarah sat down across from him. The table was solid under her elbows. The pencil gutters Frank had carved caught the morning light.

"He's been fighting infected for fifty-three days," she said. "Every clearing run, every nest, every Stalker and Clicker. That's practice."

"Practice don't do what he did. Practice makes you good. This was somethin' else. He took a bullet and kept goin' like he'd been stung by a bee. He crossed twenty yards of open ground in the time it took a man to cycle a bolt action. I've seen men move like that exactly once, and they were on drugs that would kill a horse."

"Marcus isn't on drugs."

"I know he isn't. That's my point."

They sat in silence. The dam hummed. Outside, Tommy was talking to Carson about something. Normal morning sounds. The compound waking up as if seven men hadn't tried to take it six hours ago.

"People saw," Sarah said.

"Yeah."

"Donna and Tommy were on the ridge. Helen and Carson were at the boulder."

"Yeah."

Sarah pulled out her journal. Not to write. To think. She tapped the pencil against the page, a habit she'd picked up from Marcus.

"They're scared of him," she said.

"They should be."

"Dad."

"I'm not sayin' it's bad. I'm sayin' it's true. Whatever Marcus is, whatever that system of his does to him, he's not a regular person anymore. And now everyone here knows it."

Sarah looked at the kitchen doorway. Through it, she could see the porch, and beyond the porch, Marcus sitting on the dam walkway with his bandaged shoulder, eating an MRE one-handed. Helen was near the garage, doing something with the inventory clipboard, but her eyes kept drifting toward Marcus. Carson was on the north ridge, clearing brush with an axe, working harder than usual, the way people work when they're trying not to think about something.

Mia was on the porch with Lena. The six-year-old was drawing in the dirt with a stick, completely unbothered. Lena was watching Marcus too.

Everyone was watching Marcus. And Marcus was eating beef stroganoff and staring at the river like it was a Tuesday.

...

Sarah went to him at noon. She brought a sandwich. Turkey, from an MRE packet, on crackers. Not great. Not terrible.

She sat down on the concrete rail next to him. His shoulder was wrapped in Helen's clean bandage, already spotted with a small bloom of red where the wound was still seeping.

"Joel told me," she said.

"I figured."

"How's the shoulder?"

"Sore. I bought antibiotics this morning. It'll heal."

"Helen said you didn't slow down after getting shot."

Marcus looked at the river. A trout was holding in the current behind the same boulder as yesterday. Same trout, probably. Fish were creatures of habit.

"She also said the medical kit appeared out of nowhere," Sarah said.

"Yeah."

"And Donna told me you had a hatchet in your hand before the first shot was fired, and you dropped off an eight-foot ledge onto gravel and came up swinging. She said it took less than fifteen seconds for five armed men to be on the ground."

"I didn't count."

"Marcus." Sarah's voice was patient. It was the voice she used when she was about to say something he needed to hear, and she wanted him to actually hear it. "Everyone is grateful. Nobody's going to complain that you saved the compound. But they're also looking at you like you're a loaded weapon someone left on the kitchen table."

Marcus set down the MRE. "I'm not a weapon."

"I know that. You know that. But Carson's been on the north ridge since six AM and he hasn't stopped swinging that axe because he doesn't want to come down and have a conversation about last night. Gene took his blood pressure twice this morning. Ava hasn't come out of the maintenance building. And Helen has written four pages in her notebook since dawn."

"Helen writes four pages before breakfast. That's just Helen."

"These four pages are about you."

Marcus was quiet. The river ran below them, clear and cold. The dam hummed.

"The system makes me stronger," he said. "Not because I bought anything. I can't afford the real upgrades. But fighting does something. Clearing runs, combat practice, the repetition. My reflexes are faster than they should be. I can take more damage. I don't know if it's the system leveling me up passively or if my body is just adapting to what I'm putting it through, but I'm not the same person who could barely kill a Runner with a kitchen knife in Austin."

"I know."

"You're not scared of me."

"I've never been scared of you. Not once. Not at the gas station, not at the school, not when you told me about the system." She looked at him. "But I've known you for fifty-three days. These people have known you for three. They just watched you do something impossible, and they don't have the context I have."

"What do I do about it?"

Sarah thought about it. The same way she thought about everything. Methodically, practically, with the clear-eyed realism of a girl who'd been shot on the worst night of human history and had decided that reality was something you dealt with, not something you hid from.

"You do nothing," she said. "You don't explain. You don't apologize. You don't perform. You just keep being the guy who buys medicine for Gene and wire for Tommy and seeds for the garden. Let them watch you eat terrible MREs and argue with Joel about gate construction and fall asleep on the dam walkway like you do every third night. Let them see that the person who fought like that last night is the same person who burned the coffee this morning."

"I didn't burn the coffee this morning."

"Frank did. My point stands. Be normal. Be boring. Let the extraordinary thing they saw get buried under a hundred ordinary things."

Marcus looked at her. Thirteen years old. Running the community's social dynamics with the same precision she used on the garden beds and the food schedule.

"When did you get this smart?" he asked.

"Somewhere between the ranch and the school full of Stalkers."

She'd used that line before. She knew it, and she gave him a look that was half apology and half daring him to call her on it.

He didn't.

...

That evening, dinner happened at Frank's table again. Fourteen people, elbows touching, Gene's chair scraping against the bench. The same controlled chaos as the night before. But different.

The conversation was quieter. People laughed in the right places but the laughter came a beat late, like they were checking with each other before they committed to it. Donna talked about the watch rotation. Tommy talked about wire gauges. Frank described the stool he was planning to build for the secondary OP. Normal topics. Safe topics.

Nobody talked about the gravel bar.

Marcus sat at the head of the table because that's where Frank had put him and nobody had moved the chair. He ate with his right hand because his left shoulder was bandaged and stiff. He asked Gene about his blood pressure. He told Tommy the wire would be ready tomorrow. He listened to Lena describe the garlic she'd planted that morning.

He was being normal. Being boring. Doing what Sarah said.

It mostly worked.

But at one point during dinner, Mia dropped her cracker on the floor, and Marcus reached down to pick it up and hand it back to her. A small thing. An automatic thing. He did it with his injured arm without thinking, and the motion was smooth and easy, and Helen watched him do it from across the table.

Helen didn't say anything. She didn't write anything in her notebook. But Donna saw her watching, and Carson saw Donna seeing, and for three seconds the table was very quiet.

Then Mia said "thank you" in her small voice, and Marcus said "you're welcome," and Frank started talking about dovetail joints, and the moment passed.

After dinner, Marcus checked the settlement screen.

**[Settlement: Crystal River Dam]**
**[Population: 14]**
**[Power: Dam (168 kW) + Solar (1 kW backup)]**
**[Water: River (filtered)]**
**[Food: Mall-supplied (transition to agriculture in progress)]**
**[Defenses: Perimeter watch (3-shift rotation), natural bottleneck, observation post, south perimeter tripwire (active), secondary OP (active)]**
**[Defense Rating: Upgraded — Settlement successfully defended against hostile incursion]**
**[Governance: 5-seat council (established Day 51)]**
**[Morale: Shaken]**

**[Points: 481]**

Morale: Shaken.

He'd never seen that descriptor before. It had been "Stable" since the council formed. Now it was shaken, and Marcus knew exactly why, and it wasn't the raiders.

It was him.

He lay in bed and listened to the compound settle. The watch shifted. Donna's boots, then Helen's, then silence. The dam hummed. A coyote called from the ridge, and another answered from somewhere south.

Marcus touched his shoulder through the bandage. The wound was already itching. Healing faster than it should. Another thing he couldn't explain to anyone.

He'd protected them. All fourteen of them. And now they were afraid of the thing that had kept them safe.

"That's the job," he thought. Not the system. Not the points. The other thing. The harder thing.

Being the monster that guards the village.

He didn't sleep well.
