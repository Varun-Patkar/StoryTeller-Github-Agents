# Chapter 13: The Clicking

## — Marcus —

The map showed the warehouse at eleven miles out.

He'd been checking every thirty minutes since dawn, flipping the system overlay on and off between steps, watching the little red dots that meant infected drift across the 25-mile radius like weather patterns. Most of them were scattered. Singles and pairs, wandering through empty fields or standing motionless in abandoned houses, doing whatever Runners did when nobody was around to eat. Waiting, probably. The fungus was patient. It didn't get bored.

But the cluster at the eleven-mile mark was different. Eight dots. Maybe nine. Packed tight in what the map labeled as "Kramer Agricultural Supply" on the edge of a town called Buckner. Population 340, according to the atlas. Population zero now, most likely.

Eight Runners in one building. If the math held, that was 80 to 100 points, plus streak bonuses, plus whatever the system gave for clearing a nest. He was sitting at 610. The settlement starter kit cost 735.

One good run.

He closed the map and kept walking. The road was flat and straight, cutting through open pasture where cattle had grazed three weeks ago. The fences were still up. The cattle were gone. A water trough sat by the roadside, half full of green water and dead leaves. Grasshoppers clicked in the dry brush. The sky was wide and pale, the kind of washed-out blue that meant the heat was coming back for one more round before fall took over for good.

Joel was fifteen yards ahead. Tommy behind him. Sarah in the middle, walking with her hands in her jacket pockets. She'd been quieter than usual this morning, but not the bad kind of quiet. The processing kind. The kind where a twelve-year-old was running last night's conversation through her head and deciding how it fit into the new shape of her life.

She'd been humming something. An old song. Marcus couldn't place it.

"There's a building ahead," Marcus said. "Eleven miles northwest. Agricultural supply warehouse. I'm picking up a cluster of infected inside."

Joel didn't turn around. "How many?"

"Eight or nine."

"You want to clear it."

It wasn't a question. Joel had figured out the pattern days ago. Marcus needed to kill infected the way a car needed gas. The specifics were filed under "deal with later," but the equation was obvious: infected died, Marcus got stronger, the group got safer.

"I want to clear it," Marcus confirmed. "We're passing within a mile of the town anyway. It's on our route."

"Tommy," Joel said.

"I heard." Tommy adjusted the Remington on his shoulder. "Warehouse. Could be tight quarters inside. How big is this place?"

"Agricultural supply, so probably a large open floor. Metal building. Loading docks. Think Tractor Supply but bigger."

"Windows?"

"I don't know. The map doesn't show that kind of detail."

"We scout first," Joel said. "Before we go in. Look at the building, check exits, find a high point for Tommy. We don't walk into nothin' blind."

"Agreed."

Sarah had stopped humming. "What about me?"

"You stay outside," Joel said. The tone wasn't negotiable. It was the dad voice, the one that predated the apocalypse and would probably outlast it.

"I know. I was going to say, I'll watch the packs."

"Good."

They kept walking. The grasshoppers kept clicking. Marcus thought about 125 points and a warehouse full of things that wanted to eat him, and tried to feel ready.

He didn't. But 610 points wasn't going to become 735 by feeling ready.

## — Joel —

They reached Buckner just after noon.

The town was what Joel expected. Small. Dead. A main street with a post office, a feed store, a diner with a hand-painted sign that said "Mama Jean's — Best Chicken Fried Steak in Gillespie County." A church with a white steeple that was leaning about ten degrees to the left. A water tower with the name painted in blue letters that were already starting to fade.

No bodies. No wreckage. Just empty. The kind of empty that meant people had left in an organized way, probably headed for a quarantine zone, probably weeks ago. The windows were intact. The doors were closed. A newspaper rack outside the post office still had copies of the Buckner Gazette from September 25th. Two days before the world ended.

The warehouse was on the north edge of town, set back from the road behind a gravel lot. Joel studied it from behind a pickup truck parked across the street, sixty yards out.

Big metal building. Green corrugated walls, flat roof, two loading docks on the south side with roll-up doors. A regular entrance on the east side. One window, small, up high on the west wall. The kind of building that was dark inside even when the lights were on.

No movement visible. But the doors were closed, and whatever was inside was inside.

"One entrance on the east," Joel said. "Two loading docks, south side. One window, west side, up high. Tommy, you see anything for overwatch?"

Tommy was scanning the buildings across the road. He pointed at the feed store, which had a flat roof and a ladder bolted to the back wall. "That roof gives me line of sight on the loading docks and the east entrance. If anything comes out, I'll see it."

"Good. Get up there."

Tommy looked at Marcus. "How fresh are these infected? You can tell that?"

Marcus did the eye thing. The staring-at-nothing thing that Joel had stopped questioning and started just working around, like a coworker's weird habit that you learned to ignore because they were good at their job.

"The map doesn't show stages. Just positions. Eight dots inside, all stationary. They're not moving around much."

"Stationary could mean Runners in idle mode," Tommy said. "Could mean Stalkers. Could mean anything."

"It's been three weeks since the outbreak," Joel said. "Most of the infected this far from a city should still be Runners."

"Most," Tommy said.

"Most."

Tommy held Joel's eyes for a second. The look between them was the look of two men who'd been in enough bad situations to know that "most" was the word that got people killed. But "most" was also the best information they had, and standing in a parking lot debating probabilities wasn't going to change what was inside that warehouse.

Tommy headed for the feed store. Joel watched him go, then turned to Marcus.

The kid was checking his weapons. Hunting knife on his belt, the one he'd been carrying since Austin. The handgun Joel had given him, a Glock 19, which Marcus handled with the competence of someone who'd learned from YouTube videos rather than experience. Not bad. Not instinctive. He didn't hold it wrong, but he thought about every motion instead of letting his hands do the work.

"You go in through the east door," Joel said. "I'll come through the loading dock on the south side. Give me two minutes to get in position, then open your door."

"So they come at me and you flank them."

"If they're Runners, they'll charge whatever they hear first. I'll be behind them."

Marcus nodded. He looked nervous. Not panicking, not frozen. Just nervous the way a reasonable person should be when they're about to walk into a dark building full of things that eat people. Joel appreciated that. People who weren't nervous before a fight were either experienced enough to have processed the fear already, or stupid enough to have never felt it. Marcus was neither. He was scared, and he was going to do it anyway, and that was the most Joel could ask of anyone.

"Stay low," Joel said. "Use the shelves for cover. Don't swing the knife unless you're sure of the angle. And if something goes wrong, get out. Don't try to be a hero. Just get out."

"What if something goes wrong for you?"

Joel pulled the SIG from his waistband and checked the chamber. Round loaded. Safety off. "Then you'll hear shooting and you come find me."

"That's not a great plan."

"It's the plan we got."

Sarah was sitting on the tailgate of the pickup truck, their packs arranged around her. She had the Remington's spare rounds in her jacket pocket because Tommy had asked her to hold them, which was Tommy's way of giving her a job without putting her in danger. She was watching Joel and Marcus with the expression Joel had learned to recognize. The one that said she knew exactly what was happening and was choosing not to argue because she'd already won the argument she cared about, which was being treated like a person and not a porcelain doll.

"Be careful," she said. To both of them. No drama. Just the words, delivered flat, the way Joel would have said them.

"Always am," Joel said.

"You're really not."

He almost smiled. Didn't. Walked toward the south side of the warehouse, staying low behind the parked trucks in the lot, moving the way he moved when work was about to start. Smooth. Quiet. Every step placed on purpose.

The loading dock had a personnel door beside the roll-up gate. Joel tried the handle. Unlocked. He opened it two inches and listened.

Dark inside. The air was warm and stale and smelled like fertilizer and something else, something organic and wrong, like meat left out in a garage. He knew that smell. He'd learned it at the farmhouse on Day Seven. Infected.

He eased the door open another six inches. Waited. No sound. No movement.

He slipped inside.

## — Marcus —

Two minutes. He counted them in his head because pulling up the system clock felt like the wrong kind of multitasking right now.

The east entrance had a metal door with a push bar. A sign above it read "EMPLOYEES ONLY" in red stencil. A cigarette butt sat on the concrete step, brown and flat, smoked down to the filter by someone who'd never come back to smoke another one.

Marcus put his hand on the push bar. The metal was warm from the sun.

The map showed eight dots inside. All stationary. Spread across the floor in no particular pattern, like chess pieces left mid-game on a board nobody was playing anymore.

He pushed the door open.

The warehouse was big. High ceiling, maybe thirty feet. Metal shelving units ran in rows from left to right, stacked with bags of feed, fertilizer, seed, and pesticide. Some bags had fallen. White powder on the concrete floor. The air was thick and smelled like ammonia and that other smell, the sweet-rot smell that Marcus had started to associate with the fungus even though nobody had told him that's what it was. He just knew. The same way you knew milk was bad before you tasted it.

Light came in through the open door behind him and the small window on the west wall. Not much. Most of the warehouse was shadow. The shelving units created corridors that ran deep into the building, parallel and narrow, like aisles in a grocery store designed for forklifts instead of shopping carts.

He could hear them.

Not words. Not growling. Just... breathing. The wet, uneven respiration of lungs that were being colonized by something that didn't need oxygen but kept the host breathing anyway, because a breathing host moved faster than a dead one.

Marcus drew the hunting knife. The Glock was on his hip but he'd learned from the feed store in Chapter 8 that guns in enclosed spaces meant noise, and noise meant every Runner within earshot charging at once. The knife was quieter. Uglier, but quieter.

He moved into the first aisle.

The Runner was standing between two pallets of cattle feed, facing the wall. Female, maybe forty. Jeans and a flannel shirt, the kind of outfit you'd wear to an agricultural supply store on a Saturday morning. Her hands hung at her sides. Her head was tilted slightly, like she was reading the label on a bag of feed, which she wasn't, because she couldn't read anything anymore, because the fungus had eaten everything that made her the person who wore flannel shirts and bought cattle feed on Saturday mornings.

Marcus came up behind her. Fast. Knife into the base of the skull, the way Joel had shown him on Day Seven. One motion, in and out, hand over the mouth to catch any sound. The Runner dropped. Heavy. Dead weight is heavier than living weight and he'd never gotten used to that.

**[Runner Killed — 9 points]**
**[Points: 619]**

He moved to the next aisle.

Two more Runners. Both standing, facing different directions, not doing anything. The idle state. Waiting for a sound, a movement, a reason to activate. Marcus took the closer one first, same technique, knife to the brainstem. It dropped. The second one heard something, or felt something, and turned. Fast. The face was a man's, young, maybe twenties. A cap with a tractor logo sat crooked on his head. His eyes were bloodshot and unfocused and his mouth was open and he lunged.

Marcus sidestepped. The Runner's hands clipped his shoulder. He caught his balance, drove the knife upward under the jaw, felt the blade scrape bone and then slide into something softer. The Runner made a sound like a cough and fell sideways into a shelf, knocking three bags of fertilizer onto the floor with a series of dull thuds.

Too loud.

**[Runner Killed — 8 points]**
**[Runner Killed — 11 points]**
**[Streak 3 — Bonus: 10 points]**
**[Points: 648]**

From deeper in the warehouse, he heard them wake up. The breathing changed. Became faster. Feet on concrete. Not running yet. Searching. Three of them, maybe four, somewhere in the back half of the building where the shelves were taller and the light didn't reach.

A gunshot. Joel's SIG, unmistakable, the sharp flat bark of a 9mm in an enclosed space. Then another.

"Two down," Joel called from somewhere on the far side. "Got two more comin' your way."

Marcus moved to the center aisle. Two Runners came around the end of a shelving unit at a full sprint. He shot the first one in the chest with the Glock. It stumbled. He shot it again in the head. The second one was faster, closing the distance before he could adjust his aim. He dropped the gun arm and swung the knife in a short arc that caught the Runner across the throat. Not a kill. The Runner grabbed his jacket. He shoved it backward into the shelf and stabbed twice more, once in the shoulder, once in the neck. It let go. He finished it with the knife through the temple.

**[Runner Killed — 10 points]**
**[Runner Killed — 7 points]**

Joel appeared at the end of the aisle. Blood on his hands, the SIG in his right, a length of pipe in his left. He'd found the pipe inside, probably. Joel was always finding things he could hit people with. It was a talent.

"That's seven," Joel said. "You said eight or nine."

Marcus checked the map. One dot remaining. Deep in the warehouse. Back corner. Not moving.

"One more. Back left."

Joel nodded. They moved together now, the way they'd learned to move in the last ten days. Joel slightly ahead and to the right, Marcus behind and left. Not military formation. Not a tactic from a manual. Just the positions they'd drifted into naturally, based on Joel's speed with the pipe and Marcus's reach with the knife.

The back of the warehouse was darker. The shelves here were floor-to-ceiling industrial racks, bolted to the concrete, loaded with pallets of bagged seed and shrink-wrapped equipment. A forklift was parked in the corner, yellow paint chipped, keys still in the ignition. The air was thicker here. Warmer. The rot smell was stronger.

Marcus checked the map again. The last dot was ten feet away. Right around the next shelf.

He stepped around the corner.

And stopped.

It wasn't a Runner.

The thing standing in the corner of the warehouse was shaped like a person the way a tree stump is shaped like a person. The general outline was there. Two legs, two arms, a torso. But the head was wrong. The entire upper half of the skull was covered in fungal growth, thick and layered, like someone had poured wet plaster over a face and let it harden into ridges and plates. Where the eyes should have been, there was nothing. Just fungus. Crusted, textured, faintly damp in the thin light from the window thirty feet above.

It was facing the wall. Standing perfectly still. Not breathing the way Runners breathed. This was a different rhythm. Slower. Deeper. The chest expanding and contracting in long, deliberate cycles, like something hibernating.

Then it made the sound.

A click. Sharp. Organic. Coming from somewhere inside the ruined head, bouncing off the walls and the shelves and the concrete floor. Not loud. Not a scream or a growl. Just a click, followed by silence, followed by another click.

Echolocation. Like a bat. Like a dolphin. Like nothing Marcus had ever heard outside of a video game, and hearing it in person was so fundamentally different from hearing it through TV speakers that his brain needed a full second to connect the sound to the word.

Clicker.

He knew what it was. He'd known since the first click, because he'd spent hundreds of hours in a world where this sound meant "hide or die," and his hands were already doing what his brain hadn't caught up to yet, which was freezing in place, not moving, not breathing, because Clickers were blind and hunted by sound and the only thing keeping him alive right now was the fact that he wasn't making any.

Joel was two steps behind him. Joel had frozen too. Not because he knew what a Clicker was. Because Joel had survival instincts that predated any game or any system, and those instincts were screaming at him that the thing in the corner was not the same as the things they'd just killed, and different meant dangerous, and dangerous meant don't move.

The Clicker clicked again. The sound bounced around the warehouse. Marcus could feel it, not hear it. It landed on his skin like a pressure change, like the air in the room was being mapped, charted, read by something that didn't need eyes to see.

It turned.

Not fast. Slow. Deliberate. Rotating on its feet like a radar dish, the ruined head sweeping left to right, clicking, clicking, clicking. Scanning the room. Looking for them without looking at all.

Marcus's hand was on the knife. The blade was wet with Runner blood. His heart was slamming so hard he was afraid the Clicker would hear that too.

Joel touched his shoulder. One finger. Tap. A signal they'd never discussed but understood. Don't move. I know. Wait.

The Clicker finished its sweep. It faced them now, or faced their direction, which wasn't the same thing because it couldn't see them and didn't know they were there as long as they didn't make a sound.

It clicked three more times. Then it turned back to the wall and went still.

Marcus exhaled through his nose. Tiny. Controlled. The smallest breath he'd ever taken.

Joel leaned close to his ear. His voice was barely a vibration. "What is that."

"Clicker," Marcus breathed. "Stage three infected. Blind. Uses sound to find you. Stronger than a Runner. A lot stronger."

"How do we kill it?"

"Quiet. Get behind it. Hit the head. Don't let it grab you. If it grabs you, it's over."

Joel looked at him. In the dark, Joel's face was hard to read, but Marcus could see enough. Joel wasn't scared. Joel was recalculating. The way a contractor recalculates when he opens a wall and finds the studs aren't where the blueprint said they'd be. Not panic. Adjustment.

Joel held up the pipe. Pointed at himself. Then pointed at the Clicker. Then pointed at Marcus and mimed a stabbing motion.

Translation: I hit it, you finish it.

Marcus nodded.

Joel moved. Slow. Foot by foot. The concrete floor was dirty with spilled fertilizer and grain dust, which actually helped, because the dust muffled his boots better than clean concrete would have. He circled left, keeping a shelf between himself and the Clicker, angling toward its blind side. Which was every side, technically, but the side facing away from its head was the side where the echolocation was weakest.

Marcus went right. Knife up. Moving the way you move in a nightmare, where every step takes a year and the floor feels like it's made of glass.

The Clicker clicked. Marcus froze. Joel froze.

It didn't turn. Just a routine scan. The sound bounced off the walls and came back and apparently told the Clicker that nothing had changed, because it settled again, the breathing going slow, the clicks spacing out.

Joel was in position. Three feet behind it, slightly to the left. Pipe raised. He looked at Marcus across the dark. Marcus raised the knife.

Joel swung.

The pipe hit the Clicker in the side of the head with a sound like a baseball bat hitting a watermelon. Wet. Dense. The kind of sound that tells you something broke.

The Clicker didn't go down.

It screamed. Not a human scream. A frequency that Marcus felt in his teeth, high and ragged and wrong, like metal being torn. It spun toward Joel with a speed that shouldn't have been possible for something that had been standing still for who knew how long. Its hands came up, fingers spread, reaching for Joel's face with the blind certainty of something that had already mapped the room and knew exactly where the sound came from.

Joel stumbled backward. His heel caught a bag of seed on the floor and he went down. The Clicker lunged. Joel got the pipe up between them like a crossbar and the Clicker's hands closed on the pipe instead of Joel's neck, and for a second they were locked together on the floor, the Clicker pressing down with arms that were as thick as tree branches and Joel pushing back with everything he had, which was a lot, but maybe not enough.

Marcus moved.

Three steps. Fast. Not quiet. It didn't matter anymore. The Clicker knew they were here.

He came in from the right side and drove the hunting knife into the base of the Clicker's skull. The blade went in an inch and stopped. Fungal plating. Like hitting wood. He pulled back and stabbed again, harder, angling the blade upward, and this time it punched through the softer tissue where the fungus hadn't fully calcified. The Clicker jerked. Its grip on the pipe loosened.

Joel shoved it sideways. Marcus stabbed a third time, two-handed, putting his weight behind it, driving the knife in up to the handle. The Clicker's scream cut off. Its arms dropped. It collapsed onto the concrete floor and twitched twice and went still.

The warehouse was quiet.

Marcus stood over the body, breathing hard, hands shaking. The knife was stuck. He had to brace his boot against the Clicker's shoulder and pull with both hands to get it free, and when it came out it made a sound he was going to hear in his sleep for weeks.

**[Clicker Killed — 65 points]**
**[First Clicker Kill — Achievement Bonus: 25 points]**
**[Streak 8 — Bonus: 20 points]**
**[Nest Cleared: Kramer Agricultural Supply — Bonus: 15 points]**
**[Points: 820]**

Eight hundred and twenty.

Eighty-five points over the settlement starter kit.

He looked at Joel. Joel was sitting on the floor, the pipe across his knees, breathing through his nose in long, controlled pulls. There was a scratch on his forearm where the Clicker's fingers had scraped him. Not a bite. Just a scratch. But Joel was staring at it the way you stare at a scratch when the thing that scratched you wasn't human and you're not sure what the rules are anymore.

"It's not a bite," Marcus said. "A scratch won't turn you. Only bites and spores."

Joel looked at him. "You know that for sure?"

"Yes."

"How?"

Marcus almost said "I've played this game a hundred times." Instead he said, "Same way I know everything else. I just know."

Joel looked at the scratch for another three seconds. Then he wiped the blood on his jeans and stood up.

"That thing," Joel said, looking at the Clicker on the floor. "That wasn't a Runner."

"No. It's what a Runner becomes after a few months. The fungus grows over the head, takes the eyes. They can't see. They use sound to navigate."

"And they're stronger."

"A lot stronger. There are worse ones too. Later stages. Bigger."

Joel processed that. He didn't ask about the later stages. Joel had a talent for knowing which information was useful right now and which information would just make the current moment harder.

"You knew what it was," Joel said. "The second you saw it. You knew."

"Yeah."

"And you knew how to kill it."

"In theory. I'd never actually done it before."

Joel looked at the body again. The ruined head, the fungal plates, the hands that had almost closed around his throat. He picked up the pipe. Wiped it on a bag of seed.

"Theory worked," Joel said.

## — Tommy —

They came out the east door. Marcus first, then Joel. Both of them looking like they'd crawled through a chimney and fought a dog on the way out.

Tommy lowered the Remington. He'd heard the shots. He'd heard something else too, toward the end, a sound that carried through the warehouse walls and across the gravel lot and up to the feed store roof where he was lying prone with the scope trained on the door. A scream that wasn't a person screaming. Higher. Thinner. Like a sound from a nature documentary about something that lived in caves and never saw daylight.

"Everyone alive?" he called down.

"Yeah," Joel said.

"What was that noise?"

Joel looked at Marcus. Marcus looked at Joel. Some kind of exchange happened between them that Tommy wasn't part of, which happened more often than Tommy liked.

"New kind of infected," Marcus said. "Further along than a Runner. Stronger. Blind, but it hunts by sound."

"Blind and it almost killed you?"

"Almost killed Joel, specifically."

"I had it," Joel said.

"You were on the floor."

"I was on the floor on purpose."

Tommy climbed down the ladder. Sarah was already off the tailgate, walking toward them with the quick stride of someone who'd been sitting still and worrying for twenty minutes, which for a twelve-year-old felt like twenty hours.

"Is that blood?" she said, looking at Joel's arm.

"Scratch. It's nothin'."

"That's a lot of nothing." She took his arm and turned it to look at the scratch. It was shallow. Barely bleeding. She looked at Marcus for confirmation.

"Not a bite," Marcus said. "He's fine."

Sarah held Joel's arm for another second, then let go. "You smell terrible."

"Fertilizer."

"And something else."

"Yeah. That too."

They moved away from the warehouse. Back to the pickup truck, back to the packs. Tommy handed Joel a water bottle and Joel washed his arm and drank the rest. Marcus sat on the tailgate and did the eye thing, and this time he wasn't hiding it, wasn't pretending. He was staring at something invisible with the expression of a man who'd just checked his bank account and found more money in it than he expected.

"Good haul?" Tommy asked.

Marcus looked at him. Then he looked at Sarah. Then he looked at Joel.

"We can build the dam," he said.

Nobody asked what that meant. They all knew.

Sarah handed Joel a bandage from the pack. Basic gauze, the kind Marcus had produced from nowhere two weeks ago. Joel wrapped his arm without complaint. The scratch would heal. The scratch was nothing.

The thing in the warehouse was something.

Tommy had seen a lot of combat. Fallujah. Forward operating bases where mortar rounds came in at dinner and you ate with one hand on your fork and one hand on your helmet. He'd thought the infected were bad. Runners were bad. Fast, stupid, overwhelming in numbers. But manageable. The kind of enemy you could predict, outmaneuver, put down with a clean shot.

Whatever had screamed inside that warehouse wasn't that. That was something that hunted in the dark and shrugged off a pipe to the skull and almost ripped Joel's throat out before Marcus put a knife in it. That was something that meant the infected weren't just going to be a static problem. They were going to get worse.

He kept that thought to himself. Joel was washing fertilizer dust off his face. Marcus was grinning at invisible numbers. Sarah was repacking their bags with the focused efficiency of someone who'd been doing it for two weeks and could fit four people's lives into three backpacks.

"How far to the next campsite?" Tommy asked.

Marcus checked. "Six miles. There's a barn on a ranch road. Clear on the map."

"Let's move."

They shouldered the packs. Joel took point. Tommy behind him. Sarah in the middle, humming again, the same song from this morning. Marcus at the back.

Tommy noticed Marcus was walking different. Not faster. Not taller. Just different. The way a man walks when the thing he's been carrying got lighter, and the place he's going got closer, and the reason he's going there got clearer.

820 points. Whatever that meant.

It meant something to Marcus. And for now, that was enough.

They left Buckner behind. The warehouse, the post office, Mama Jean's chicken fried steak, the church with the leaning steeple. Another small town that the world had forgotten.

The road went northwest. The sun went west. The group went with it.

Six hundred and seventy-five miles to Colorado.
