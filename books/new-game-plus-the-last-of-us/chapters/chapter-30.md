# Chapter 30: Teeth

## — Marcus —

Day one hundred and three.

The meadow behind the maintenance building was flat, mostly. A hundred feet of frozen grass between the garden beds and the tree line, with a slope on the east end where the ground dropped toward the creek. Frank had cleared the worst of the rocks and deadfall after Marcus asked him to, and now it was the closest thing they had to an open training ground.

Twelve volunteers stood in a loose group at the center of it, breathing clouds into the December air.

Marcus had expected eight. He'd posted the notice on Sarah's bulletin board two days ago, a handwritten sheet that said COMMUNITY DEFENSE VOLUNTEERS — SEE MARCUS and nothing else. No promises. No requirements. Just a sheet of paper on a board that already held the meal rotation, the work schedule, the patrol roster, and a crayon drawing of the dam that Mia had made and Sarah refused to take down.

Twelve people had signed up.

He knew most of them by now. Helen Park, who'd been asking about patrol assignments since her second day. Carson, who'd swung an axe for a living before the outbreak and handled the weight of a tool like someone who'd been doing it for decades. Mateo, the fifteen-year-old who Joel had refused to put on perimeter watch two weeks ago. Paul Whitfield, the maintenance man who'd arrived with Dr. Reyes. Two of the Denver refugees whose names Marcus had learned last week: a woman named Keiko Tanaka who'd been a high school gym teacher, and a quiet man named Dale Briggs who'd done something with electrical line work and had forearms like bridge cables.

The other six were newer arrivals. People Marcus recognized from meals and work rotations but hadn't spoken to individually. A mix of ages and sizes and backgrounds, standing in the frozen meadow with expressions that ranged from determined to terrified.

Joel was leaning against the maintenance building wall, arms crossed, watching. He hadn't volunteered to help. He hadn't said he wouldn't, either. He was doing the Joel thing: being present without committing, keeping his options open until he decided whether this was worth his time.

Marcus stepped in front of the group.

"I'm not going to make a speech," he said. "Here's what this is. You're volunteering to learn how to defend this settlement. Not how to be soldiers. Not how to fight. How to defend. There's a difference."

Dale Briggs nodded. The rest watched.

"We run patrols. You've seen them. Donna's team walks the perimeter twice a day, checks the observation posts, reports anything unusual. That works with five people. It won't work with a hundred."

"We don't have a hundred," Helen said.

"We will."

Nobody argued with that. The lights on the mountainside were pulling people in at a steady rate. Fifty now. Sixty by January, if the pattern held.

"I'm going to teach you three things," Marcus said. "How to identify infected by type. How to move in a group without getting each other killed. And how to hold a position until help arrives. That's it. You're not going to be clearing nests. You're not going to be running solo ops. You're going to be the people who keep the gate secure and the perimeter watched while the rest of the community sleeps."

He paused.

"Questions."

Mateo raised his hand. "Do we get weapons?"

"You get training first. Weapons after."

"How long?"

"Until I say you're ready."

Mateo's jaw tightened. He wanted to argue. Marcus could see it. Fifteen years old and desperate to prove that he belonged, that the group who'd left him near Independence Pass had been wrong to leave him.

"Start with formation," Marcus said.

He spent the next two hours on basics. Walking in a line. Maintaining spacing. Hand signals for stop, move, danger-left, danger-right. Simple things that felt stupid on a frozen meadow with no threat in sight. Simple things that would keep them alive when the threat was real.

The gap between willing and capable was a canyon.

Keiko Tanaka was the best of them. She moved naturally, kept her spacing without being reminded, and picked up the hand signals after two repetitions. Gym teacher reflexes. She was used to managing groups of bodies in space.

Carson was strong but loud. Every step crunched. Every turn was a production. He moved like a man who'd spent his life building things, not sneaking past them.

Helen was disciplined. Eight years of Army Reserve had given her the instinct to follow instructions precisely, which was useful for formation work but made her rigid. She did exactly what Marcus said and nothing else. No adaptation. No improvisation.

Dale Briggs was quiet and careful and kept looking at the tree line as though something was going to come out of it. Good instinct. Bad for focus.

Paul Whitfield tripped over his own feet twice in the first thirty minutes.

Mateo was fast and eager and kept breaking formation to get ahead of the person in front of him.

Marcus corrected them. Over and over. Spacing. Foot placement. Eyes forward, not down. When Mateo broke formation for the fourth time, Marcus stopped the line.

"What happens when you move ahead of the person in front of you?"

"I get there faster," Mateo said.

"You leave a gap. The person behind you can't see past you. The person in front of you doesn't know you're close. If something comes from the left, you're blocking the person behind you from reacting and the person ahead doesn't know you've moved."

"So I just walk slow?"

"You walk at the speed of the group. The group is only as fast as its slowest person."

"That's Paul," Mateo said.

Paul Whitfield, to his credit, didn't flinch. "He's not wrong."

"Paul will get faster," Marcus said. "You need to get patient."

Joel, from the wall, said nothing. But Marcus saw him nod. Once.

---

They broke for lunch at noon. Sarah had organized a midday meal rotation three weeks ago when the population passed forty, and the kitchen table in the maintenance building was now a serving station where Donna ladled soup into tin cups and Lena distributed bread from the batch she'd baked that morning.

Marcus sat on the dam walkway with his soup. Potato and onion, from the last of the fall stores. It was fine. Not good. The kind of food that existed because calories existed, not because taste did.

Helen found him.

"You're starting too basic," she said. She sat down without being invited. Helen did that. "Half of them know how to walk in a line. The ones who don't won't learn it from repetition. They'll learn it under pressure."

"I know."

"Then why are you doing it this way?"

"Because the other half will get someone killed if I skip the basics."

"So split them. Put Keiko, Dale, Carson, and me in an advanced group. Let the others drill formation until they're ready."

Marcus sipped the soup. It needed salt. Everything needed salt. He'd bought a ten-pound bag from the Mall two weeks ago and it was already half gone. Fifty people used a lot of salt.

"No," he said. "They train together. The whole point is that they learn to work as a unit, not as individuals."

Helen frowned. She didn't agree. Marcus could see her military brain sorting the volunteers into tiers, assigning capability ratings, building a hierarchy. It was good thinking. It was wrong thinking for what he was building.

"This isn't an army, Helen."

"I know what it isn't. What is it?"

"A neighborhood watch with better training."

She looked at him. The expression said she thought that was naive. She might have been right. Marcus didn't care. The moment you started sorting people into first-string and second-string, you created a class system. First-string got respect. Second-string got overlooked. And the overlooked people were the ones who stopped showing up.

"They train together," he repeated. "If Carson can't sneak, he learns from Keiko. If Paul can't keep up, Dale slows down for him. That's the point."

Helen drank her soup and said nothing for a while.

"Joel should be helping," she said.

"Joel will help when he's ready."

"He's the best combat instructor in this settlement. Better than you."

"I know."

"Then why isn't he out there?"

Marcus looked at the meadow, where Mateo was doing push-ups for reasons that were entirely his own. "Because Joel teaches by doing. He doesn't lecture. He puts you in a situation and watches how you handle it and then tells you what you did wrong. That's how he trained in the game." He caught himself. "In... before."

Helen's eyes narrowed slightly. The slip registered. Marcus kept his face still.

"He'll come around," Marcus said. "When the volunteers are ready for pressure, I'll ask Joel to run scenarios. He's better at that than me."

"And until then?"

"Until then, they learn to walk in a line without tripping over Paul."

Helen almost smiled. Almost.

## — Sarah —

The school met in Bunkhouse B from eight to eleven every morning.

Technically, it wasn't a school. It was two former teachers, a retired math tutor named Barbara Conti and a younger guy named Elliot Vance who'd taught middle school English in Glenwood Springs, sitting at a table with seven kids aged four to twelve. They had three textbooks that Marcus had bought from the Mall, a stack of paper, and a box of colored pencils that Mia hoarded like currency.

Sarah checked in at nine.

"How's the reading group?" she asked Barbara.

"Diego is ahead of where he should be. Sophie still won't talk, but she follows instructions and she writes." Barbara adjusted her glasses, which were held together with tape and willpower. "Mia wants to learn multiplication."

"She's six."

"She's six and she told me she needs to count faster because her snowman inventory is getting complicated."

Sarah wrote it down. Mia, multiplication. The list of things people needed was always longer than the list of things she could provide, but she wrote them all down anyway. Sooner or later, the items moved from the list to reality. That was the whole trick. Write it down, keep track, follow through.

Elliot was reading to the younger kids from a battered copy of Charlotte's Web that someone had brought from Grand Junction. Three kids sat cross-legged on the bunkhouse floor, listening. Owen Okafor was asleep in a basket by the wall, bundled in a fleece that Dr. Reyes had declared was too many layers but Jessica refused to reduce.

Outside, Sarah could hear Marcus's voice carrying across the meadow. Formation drills. She'd watched the first twenty minutes from the kitchen window while eating breakfast. Twelve people walking in a line, stopping, turning, walking again. It looked ridiculous. It was probably necessary.

The community was splitting. Not in a bad way. In the way that living things split when they grow. Cells dividing. Branches forking.

The military side: Marcus, Joel, Helen, the militia volunteers. Patrols, clearing runs, defense planning. The part of the settlement that faced outward.

The civilian side: Sarah, Lena, Tommy, Dr. Reyes, the teachers, the work crews. Food, power, health, education, governance. The part of the settlement that faced inward.

Both were necessary. Neither could function without the other. But they were becoming distinct in a way they hadn't been when the community was fourteen people eating dinner at one table.

Sarah walked to the garden beds after the school check-in. Lena was there, kneeling in the frozen dirt with a notebook and a look of concentration that Sarah recognized as planning.

"Spring planting," Lena said without looking up. "If we break ground in March, we can have lettuce by April, potatoes by June, corn by August. But I need more beds. At least eight more, on the south slope where the snow melts first."

"How much lumber?"

"For the frames? Six hundred board feet. Plus soil amendments. Compost, lime, whatever Marcus can get from his cache." The pause on "cache" was there. Everyone had the pause now. It was the community's open secret, the thing everyone knew was a fiction but nobody wanted to replace with a harder conversation.

"I'll put it on the request list," Sarah said. "Marcus is running a clearing run Thursday. He'll have the points."

"The what?"

"The supplies. He'll have the supplies."

Lena looked at her. Sarah looked back. Thirteen years old and already experienced enough at this particular dance to know when she'd said too much and how to walk it back.

"Spring planting," Sarah said. "What else?"

"Chickens."

"Where are we getting chickens?"

"There's a ranch twelve miles south that had a poultry operation. Tommy drove past it last week on a scout run. Said the buildings were still standing. If the birds survived, we could bring back a flock."

Sarah wrote it down. Chickens, scout run, Tommy. The list was getting long. She flipped the page in her notebook. Fifty entries. Things to build, things to find, things to fix, people to talk to, schedules to update. Every day the list grew faster than she could shrink it.

This was what leadership looked like. Not speeches. Not decisions made in dramatic moments. Lists. Schedules. Following up on the thing you said you'd follow up on.

She was thirteen. She ran the civilian half of a fifty-person settlement. She didn't have a title because nobody had thought to give her one, and she didn't want one because titles were for people who needed permission to do what they were already doing.

## — Marcus —

The afternoon session was patrol routes.

Marcus took all twelve volunteers out of the meadow and into the valley. Not far. Half a mile south, along the creek, through the stand of spruce that separated the compound from the access road. The route Donna's team walked twice a day, checking for footprints and tire tracks and anything that didn't belong.

The formation work from the morning fell apart in about thirty seconds.

The trail was narrow. Single file, tight spacing. Branches at head height. Roots across the path. Snow on the ground made every step louder than it should have been. Carson's boots cracked through the frozen crust like gunshots. Paul kept stopping to look at things. Mateo, predictably, surged ahead.

Marcus stopped the group at the creek crossing.

"Listen," he said.

They listened. Wind in the spruce. The creek, low and slow under thin ice. A bird somewhere. Nothing else.

"What did you hear on the walk here?"

Silence.

"Carson's boots," Helen said.

"And?"

"Paul stopping every forty feet."

"And?"

"Mateo's breathing. He breathes through his mouth when he's moving fast."

Mateo looked offended. Helen looked at him with an expression that was not unkind but was not sorry, either.

"An infected hears all of that," Marcus said. "Runners are attracted to sound. Clickers are blind and hunt entirely by echolocation. A Stalker will hear you coming and set up an ambush. On a patrol route, noise discipline is the difference between seeing a threat and being the threat's next meal."

He pointed at the trail behind them. "We're going to walk back. Same route. Half the noise. Carson, shorter steps. Paul, keep moving. Mateo, nose-breathe or stay home."

They walked back. It was better. Not good, but better. Carson found a rhythm that reduced the crunching. Paul stopped stopping. Mateo breathed through his nose and turned red but didn't complain.

At the halfway point, Dale Briggs raised his fist. The stop signal.

Everyone stopped. Not perfectly. Paul bumped into Keiko. Keiko kept her balance and didn't make a sound. Good.

Dale was staring at the base of a spruce tree, ten feet off the trail.

"What is it?" Marcus asked quietly.

"Fungal growth."

Marcus moved forward. Dale was right. A patch of pale yellow growth on the bark, thin, maybe a handprint's width. Not the thick mats of an active nest. A trace. A single infected had passed through recently and left biological residue on the tree. It was the kind of thing Marcus had learned to spot on solo clearing runs, the breadcrumbs that told you where infected had been and which direction they were heading.

"Good eye," Marcus said.

Dale didn't look pleased. He looked worried. "That's fresh. Last couple of days."

"Probably a lone Runner moving through the valley. The map shows two contacts four miles south, headed away from us."

"You can see that?"

"I have tools." Marcus kept his voice even. "The growth pattern tells you direction. The color tells you age. Light yellow is recent. Darker orange is older. If you see brown or red, you're near a nest and you should not be there without backup."

He looked at the group. "This is what patrol is. Not walking a route. Reading what the route tells you. Fungal traces on trees. Footprints in snow that don't match human gait. Sounds that repeat in patterns instead of randomly. If you see something, you stop the group. You report it. You don't investigate alone."

Twelve people looked at the patch of growth on the tree and understood, in a way that the morning's formation drills hadn't achieved, that the things they were training for were real.

They walked back to the compound in near-silence.

## — Joel —

Joel watched them come back.

Twelve people walking through the spruce in something close to a proper file. Spacing was off. The kid, Mateo, was still too close to the person in front of him. But the noise was reasonable. The movement was controlled. Marcus walked at the back, watching the tree line, and the woman from the gym, Keiko, walked point like she'd been doing it for weeks instead of hours.

Joel had trained men before. Not officially. Not in a classroom. But in the QZs, in the smuggling years, he'd shown people how to move and shoot and survive. The ones who listened lived longer. The ones who didn't, didn't.

Marcus was doing it differently. No yelling. No punishment drills. No ranking. He corrected, he demonstrated, he moved on. It was slower than Joel's way. Probably less effective in the short term. But the twelve people coming out of the trees looked like they trusted each other in a way that Joel's method didn't produce.

Joel's way made individuals. People who could survive alone. That had been the point. In the Boston QZ, in the smuggling runs with Tess, survival was individual. You watched your own back because nobody else would.

Marcus was making a group. People who watched each other's backs because they'd learned to walk in a line together before they'd learned to fight.

It was different. Joel wasn't sure it was wrong.

He pushed off the wall and walked toward Marcus. The volunteers were dispersing, heading toward the kitchen for afternoon soup, talking in low voices. Marcus was cleaning mud off his boots on the edge of the maintenance building step.

"The woman," Joel said. "Keiko."

"What about her?"

"She's good. Natural awareness. Doesn't panic."

"I know."

"Put her on point for the next session. Let her run the route. See if she can lead."

Marcus looked at him. "You paying attention out here?"

"I'm always paying attention."

"Want to help next time? Run a scenario?"

Joel was quiet for a few seconds. The silence that meant he was weighing something. Not the decision itself. Joel had already decided. He was weighing how to say it in a way that preserved his distance.

"I'll watch one more session," Joel said. "If they're worth my time, I'll run something."

"Fair enough."

Joel turned and walked toward the gate. His shift started in twenty minutes. The gate was solid now, a proper barrier of reinforced timber with a drop-bar and a peephole, strong enough to slow a vehicle and stop anyone on foot. He'd built it with Carson over eleven days, and it was the best piece of construction work he'd done since Austin.

He didn't look back at the meadow. Didn't need to. He'd seen enough.

## — Marcus —

The stars came out around six. The December sky over the Rockies was absurd when it was clear, more stars than Marcus had ever seen in his previous life, the Milky Way a visible band across the black. The cold was sharp enough to make his lungs ache, but the dam walkway had become his thinking spot and he wasn't giving it up to weather.

Sarah found him there.

She had her notebook. She always had her notebook. It was a spiral-bound thing Marcus had bought from Tier 1 two months ago, and it was nearly full.

"Militia report," she said, sitting on the walkway railing.

"Twelve volunteers. First day. Formation is rough. Patrol awareness is better. Dale Briggs found fungal trace on the south route. Keiko Tanaka is the standout. Joel noticed."

"Joel said something?"

"He volunteered to run scenarios. Eventually."

Sarah wrote it down. "Civilian side. Lena needs eight more garden beds for spring planting. Barbara wants more textbooks. Elliot says Sophie still isn't talking but she's writing complete sentences now. And Lena wants chickens."

"Chickens."

"There's a ranch south of here. Tommy saw it."

Marcus thought about it. Chickens meant eggs. Eggs meant protein that didn't come from the Mall. Protein that renewed itself. That was the kind of resource that reduced his dependency on the system, which was something he'd been thinking about more and more.

"Put it on the Thursday run. I'll clear the route and Tommy can take the truck."

Sarah wrote. The pencil scratched in the cold air. She flipped through earlier pages, cross-referencing something, then looked up.

"We need to name it," she said.

"Name what?"

"This." She gestured at the compound. The lights in the bunkhouses. The smoke from the wood stove. The gate. The dam. The observation post where Gene was doing his evening shift, bundled in a coat and scarf, watching the valley with binoculars Marcus had bought from Tier 2.

"The settlement?"

"It's not a settlement anymore. Settlements are temporary. This is permanent. People are having babies here. Kids are going to school. We have a doctor and a council and a militia."

"We have twelve people who can walk in a line. That's not a militia."

"It will be." She closed the notebook. "We need a name. Something the community chooses. Not us. Put it to a vote or something."

"A town name."

"Yeah."

Marcus looked at the compound. The amber light from the buildings colored the snow. From up here, on the walkway, you could see the shape of it. The residence. The maintenance building. The bunkhouses radiating outward. The garden beds on the south slope. The gate at the valley entrance. The creek running through the middle like a main street made of water.

A town.

"I'll bring it up at Monday's council meeting," he said.

"Good." Sarah stood. She put the notebook in her jacket pocket and looked at the stars. "Lena's garlic survived the first frost, by the way. She's unreasonably happy about it."

"Garlic is worth being happy about."

"It's garlic, Marcus."

"Garlic means flavor. Flavor means morale. Morale means people stop complaining about the soup."

"The soup isn't that bad."

"The soup is terrible and you know it."

Sarah almost smiled. In the cold, with the stars out and the compound lit up below them, she looked older than thirteen. Not in a bad way. In the way that people who carry responsibility always look older than they are. Joel had it. Tommy had it. Helen had it.

Sarah had it more than any of them.

"Fifty people," she said. "A hundred days."

"Approximately."

"Where does it go from here?"

Marcus leaned on the railing. He thought about the plan. Not the plan he'd written on paper or discussed with the council or posted on the bulletin board. The one in his head. The canon timeline. Joel in Boston, smuggling, surviving, waiting for a girl named Ellie to cross his path. The Fireflies. The twenty-year clock ticking toward a choice in a hospital in Salt Lake City.

But that was far away. Twenty years away. Right now, the thing in front of him was simpler and harder. More beds. More food. More training. A name for the town. Chickens.

"It gets bigger," Marcus said. "And we try to keep it good while it does."

Sarah nodded. She didn't need more than that. She never did.

She went inside.

Marcus stayed on the walkway. The dam hummed under his feet, 168 kilowatts of power pushing through wires into lightbulbs and heaters and the little Christmas lights that Mia had asked for and Marcus had bought from the Mall without telling anyone, stringing them along the bunkhouse eaves when nobody was looking.

Owen Okafor was three days old. The militia was twelve people who could almost walk in a line. Spring was three months away. The chickens were a scouting mission.

Small things. Real things.

Marcus finished his coffee. Cold again. He made a mental note to buy a thermos.
