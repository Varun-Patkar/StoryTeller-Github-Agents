# Chapter 5: Awake

They found the gas station around noon.

It was a two-pump Chevron at the junction of a farm-to-market road and a two-lane highway that went nowhere important. The pumps were dead. The windows were intact. The door was locked, which Marcus took as a good sign because locked doors meant nobody had broken in yet, which meant nobody was inside waiting to do something unpleasant.

Tommy tried the handle. "Locked."

"I got it." Marcus pulled the hunting knife and wedged it into the doorframe near the latch. The cheap aluminum bent. The lock popped. Not elegant, but functional.

The inside was dim and hot and smelled like beef jerky and floor cleaner. Shelves half-stocked with chips and candy bars and motor oil. A cooler along the back wall with warm sodas and water bottles that hadn't been touched. A bathroom in the corner. No bodies. No blood. No fungal growth creeping along the ceiling tiles.

"Clear," Tommy said, finishing his sweep. He checked the bathroom, the storage closet behind the counter, the back exit. Military habits. "We can rest here."

Joel was already moving toward the counter area, where a stretch of floor tile was wide enough to lay Sarah down. He'd been carrying her for four hours. Four hours without stopping, without shifting her to his other arm, without asking anyone else to take a turn. His face was red and the veins in his neck stood out like cords, but his arms were steady. They hadn't shaken once.

He knelt and set Sarah down on the floor, carefully, the way you'd set down something made of glass. Her head on his balled-up flannel shirt. The emergency blanket spread beneath her. His hand went to her forehead, checking for fever the old-fashioned way, and lingered there.

"She's warm," Joel said.

"Could be the heat." Marcus dropped the backpack next to the counter. "Could be infection. Let me check the wound."

Joel didn't move. His hand stayed on Sarah's forehead. He was looking at her the way a man looks at something he'd already grieved for and gotten back, like he was afraid that looking away for even a second would make her disappear again.

"Joel. I need to check her bandages."

Joel moved. Slowly. He shifted to Sarah's left side and made room. His hand found hers and held on.

Marcus knelt and lifted the hem of Sarah's t-shirt. The gauze packing was holding, but the outer bandage was stiff and dark with dried blood. He peeled the medical tape back carefully. Sarah flinched in her sleep. The wound beneath was ugly but not terrible. Swollen, red around the edges, the hemostatic gauze doing its job. No streaking. No pus. The antibiotics were working.

"It looks okay," Marcus said. "Swelling's normal. No sign of serious infection."

Joel exhaled. A long, slow breath through his nose. The kind of exhale that carries about twelve hours of held tension with it.

Marcus redressed the wound with fresh gauze from the first aid kit, taped it clean, and sat back. He pulled the bottle of ibuprofen from his pocket and shook out three tablets.

"These are for when she wakes up. For the pain."

Joel took them and put them in his shirt pocket without a word.

...

Sarah woke up at 2:17 PM. Marcus knew the exact time because he'd been staring at the cracked clock on the gas station wall, the kind with a Coca-Cola logo at the top and a second hand that ticked in uneven jumps. He'd been counting the seconds between the jumps, which was the kind of thing a person did when they were too tired to sleep and too wired to relax.

The first sound she made was small. Not a word. A breath that caught on something, a half-swallowed noise that might have been pain or confusion or both. Then movement. Her hand reaching for her hip, finding the bandage, fingers pressing against it and flinching away.

Then her eyes opened.

Not the half-open, drugged-looking drift of the night before. Not the glassy, unfocused stare from the house that morning. Open. Clear. Aware.

She looked at the ceiling. Stained acoustic tiles, the kind you'd find in any cheap commercial building in America. Her brow furrowed.

"Dad?"

Her voice was rough. Dry. But there was a person behind it this time. Not a body running on autopilot.

Joel was beside her in a heartbeat. He'd been sitting three feet away, leaning against the counter with Tommy's handgun on his lap and his eyes on the front door, and the second she spoke he was there, on his knees, both hands on her face, thumbing tears off her cheeks before she'd even started crying.

"I'm here, baby girl. I'm right here."

"Dad." She said it again, different this time. Not a question. A confirmation. She reached up and grabbed the front of his undershirt with both hands and pulled herself into him, and Joel folded around her like he was trying to put himself between her and everything else in the world.

She cried.

Not loud. Not dramatic. Small, hitching sobs that she tried to muffle against his chest. The crying of a kid who was old enough to be embarrassed about crying and too hurt and too scared to stop.

Joel held her. He didn't say anything. Didn't tell her it was okay. Didn't tell her to be strong or brave or any of the things adults say to kids when they don't know what else to say. He just held her and put his chin on top of her head and closed his eyes and breathed.

Marcus got up quietly and walked to the other side of the gas station. He pretended to look at the warm sodas in the cooler. Tommy was already there, examining a shelf of motor oil with an intensity that suggested he'd also decided to be somewhere else for a few minutes.

They stood in silence and listened to Sarah cry and Joel not cry and the Coca-Cola clock tick in its uneven rhythm.

"She's tough," Tommy said, very quietly.

"Yeah."

"Gets it from Joel."

Marcus looked at Tommy. Tommy was watching the motor oil shelf like it contained the answers to everything. His jaw was tight.

"You okay?" Marcus asked.

"She's my niece," Tommy said. "I shot a man last night. And she's alive. So yeah. I'm okay."

It wasn't an answer, exactly. But it was all Tommy was offering, and Marcus respected that.

---

When the crying stopped, Marcus went back.

Sarah was sitting up. Leaning against Joel, his arm around her shoulders, but upright. Her face was puffy and red and her hair was a tangled mess and she looked about ten years older than twelve. Pain and fear did that. Aged people in ways that had nothing to do with time.

She was looking at him.

"You're Marcus," she said.

"Yeah."

"Dad says you helped me." Her voice was steadier now. Post-cry steady, the brittle kind that could crack again without warning. "He says you fixed the... the hole."

"I patched you up. A doctor would have done a better job."

"There's no doctors." She said it flat. Matter-of-fact. A twelve-year-old who had already figured out that the world was different now. "Are there."

It wasn't a question. Marcus shook his head anyway.

"Where are we?" Sarah asked. She winced as she shifted, her hand going to her hip and stopping just short of touching the bandage.

"Gas station," Joel said. "About fifteen miles north of Austin."

"Austin's..." She trailed off. Her eyes went to the window, where the sky was still hazy and grey-brown, the smoke from the bombings spreading out into a flat ceiling of haze that turned the September sun into a pale smudge.

"Yeah," Joel said. He didn't elaborate. Didn't need to. She could see the sky.

Sarah was quiet for a while. Marcus could see her working through it. The way her eyes moved, scanning the gas station, taking inventory of the situation the way a smart kid does when the adults aren't giving her enough information. The shelves. The backpack. Tommy by the door. The stranger.

"The soldier shot me," she said.

Joel's arm tightened around her.

"I remember the car. Uncle Tommy's truck. And the running. And the soldier. He pointed his gun at us and..." She stopped. Swallowed. "He shot me."

"Tommy got him," Joel said quietly. "He's gone."

"But he shot me." She touched her hip this time, lightly, fingers tracing the edge of the bandage through her shirt. "I thought I was going to die."

"You didn't."

"I know I didn't, Dad." A flicker of something. Not quite annoyance. Just the faintest edge of the Sarah who argued about chores and made Joel birthday presents and cracked bad jokes. "I'm sitting here. I can tell I didn't die."

Joel looked at her. Something shifted in his face, a crack in the tension that might have been the beginning of a smile if the circumstances had been even slightly less terrible.

"Smart mouth," he said. "You definitely didn't die."

Sarah's lips twitched. Not a smile either. But close.

Marcus pulled the ibuprofen from his pocket. "You should take these. For the swelling."

Sarah looked at the pills in his palm. Looked at Marcus. Looked at Joel.

"It's okay," Joel said. "He's... he's with us."

The hesitation was slight. Barely there. But Marcus caught it, and from the way Sarah's eyes narrowed just a fraction, she caught it too. Joel didn't trust him. Joel was allowing him. There was a difference, and Sarah Miller, even at twelve, even shot and scared and running on fumes, was sharp enough to notice.

She took the pills and swallowed them dry. Didn't even ask for water. Tough kid.

"Thank you," she said to Marcus. Polite. Careful. The voice of a girl who'd been raised right and wasn't going to forget her manners even when the world was actively ending around her.

"You're welcome."

...

The next problem was transportation.

Sarah couldn't walk. The hip wound made it impossible. She could sit up, she could shift her weight, but the second she tried to put pressure on her right leg, her face went white and her breath caught and Joel's hand was on her shoulder telling her to stop.

Joel could carry her. Joel had been carrying her. But four hours of carrying a twelve-year-old while walking over uneven ground on no sleep had left him grey-faced and breathing hard, even if he'd die before admitting it.

"She needs something to ride in," Tommy said. They were standing near the gas station's back door, out of earshot. "A wagon. A wheelbarrow. Something."

Marcus thought about the Mall. A wheelchair was probably in there. Medical equipment. But how much would it cost? And how would he explain it appearing from nowhere?

"Let me look around," he said. "There's a house across the road. Might have a garden cart or something."

He didn't find a garden cart in the house across the road. He found a dusty garage with a lawnmower, a broken weed whacker, and a red Radio Flyer wagon meant for a toddler. Too small. In the second house down, the one with the sagging fence and the overgrown yard, he found what he was looking for: a fold-up sports wagon, the kind parents use at soccer games and flea markets. Blue canvas, rubber wheels, a pull handle. Big enough for a small twelve-year-old to sit in if she tucked her legs.

He brought it back. Joel looked at it. Sarah looked at it.

"I'm not riding in a wagon," Sarah said.

"You're not walking on that hip," Joel said.

"I can use a stick. Like a crutch."

"You're riding in the wagon."

Sarah opened her mouth to argue. Then she shifted her weight and the pain hit and she closed her mouth and sat down in the wagon without another word.

"Smart kid," Tommy said.

"Shut up, Uncle Tommy."

---

They moved north through the afternoon. The farm-to-market road was empty. No cars. No people. Just flat Texas countryside and fence posts and fields of brown grass that stretched out to the horizon in every direction. The sky was still hazy. The smoke from Austin followed them like a stain on the ceiling of the world.

Tommy pulled the wagon. Joel walked beside it, close enough to touch Sarah at all times. Marcus walked point, which was a generous term for "the guy with the knife who checks around every bend in the road before the others catch up."

He needed supplies. The MREs from this morning were gone. The water was running low. Sarah needed more pain medication in four hours. And the system was nudging him. Not with a notification, not with a quest popup, just a feeling, a faint pressure at the back of his thoughts, like the Mall was sitting there with its catalog open waiting for him to browse.

He fell back to walk beside Tommy.

"I'm going to get some things," Marcus said. Quietly. "Food. Water. More medicine."

Tommy didn't look at him. "The way you usually get things."

"Yeah."

"Joel's watching."

Marcus glanced back. Joel was walking beside the wagon, one hand on the canvas rim, talking to Sarah in a low voice. But his eyes flicked to Marcus. Brief, measuring, then back to Sarah.

"I know."

He slowed his pace until he was behind the group. Ten feet back. Enough distance that the items wouldn't materialize directly in Joel's line of sight. He angled his body away from them and opened the Mall.

Food. Six MREs, 18 points. A pack of saltine crackers, 1 point. A jar of peanut butter, 1 point. Three gallons of water, 3 points. Acetaminophen, children's liquid, 2 points. That last one was for Sarah. The adult ibuprofen worked but the liquid would be easier for her to take.

The items appeared in his backpack. He felt the weight settle onto his shoulders, the pack suddenly heavier, straps digging into new places. He adjusted them and kept walking.

A notification pulsed in his peripheral vision.

**[QUEST: Find Safe Shelter]**
**[Locate defensible shelter for your group within 48 hours.]**
**[Reward: 200 Points + Perk Unlock]**
**[Time Remaining: 47:41:22]**

"Thanks for the countdown," he muttered. "Very calming."

He dismissed the quest and jogged to catch up with the group. He pulled a water bottle from the backpack and held it out to Joel without comment. Joel took it. Looked at it. Looked at the backpack that had been noticeably lighter ten minutes ago.

"Where'd this come from?"

"The backpack."

"The backpack that I watched you pack this mornin' with two bottles of water. Not six."

Marcus didn't answer. He handed a second bottle to Tommy and the crackers to Sarah, who took them with a small "thanks" and tore the sleeve open with her teeth.

Joel stopped walking. Tommy stopped. The wagon stopped. Sarah looked up from her crackers.

"I asked you a question," Joel said.

His voice was level. Not angry. That was worse. Angry Joel was a Joel who'd already decided how he felt. Level Joel was a Joel who was still deciding, and the options he was considering weren't all friendly.

"You did," Marcus said.

"And?"

"And the answer hasn't changed since this morning. I have resources. I can't explain how. I know that's not good enough."

"You're right. It's not."

"But your daughter's eating crackers that didn't exist five minutes ago, and she's going to take medicine tonight that I'll have in my pack by sundown, and tomorrow I'll have more food and more water and whatever else we need. And all of that is free. I'm not asking for anything."

Joel stared at him. The two men stood on a empty farm road in the middle of Texas, the smoke of a dead city behind them, and had a conversation entirely with their eyes. Marcus kept his face open and his hands visible and tried to look like a person who was telling the truth about the only thing that mattered, which was that he was not a threat.

"If you're dangerous," Joel said, "I will find out. And it won't go well for you."

"I know."

Joel turned and started walking again. He didn't look back.

Tommy pulled the wagon forward. As he passed Marcus, he shook his head. Not disapproval. More like the head-shake of a man watching someone juggle knives and wondering how many passes they'd get before they lost a finger.

Sarah was watching Marcus over the rim of the wagon. Chewing a cracker. Thinking.

---

Joel and Tommy went ahead to scout a farmhouse they'd spotted from the road. White house, red barn, windmill. Set back a quarter mile from the highway behind a cattle gate and a gravel drive. It looked abandoned. No cars. No movement. The windmill was turning slowly in the late afternoon breeze.

"Stay with her," Joel said to Marcus. Not a request. He and Tommy crossed the fence and headed up the drive, Tommy with the handgun, Joel with Marcus's hunting knife because Joel had refused the handgun Marcus offered him, which was a whole separate issue that Marcus was choosing not to think about.

Marcus sat on the gravel shoulder of the road next to the wagon. Sarah was lying back, knees up, staring at the sky. The haze had thinned a little as they moved north. Actual blue was visible between the smoke layers, bright and ordinary, the kind of sky that didn't know the world was ending.

"You're not from here," Sarah said.

Marcus looked at her. "What do you mean?"

"Texas. You don't talk like you're from Texas."

"I'm not." True enough.

"Where are you from?"

"Up north."

"That's not a real answer."

"It's the best I've got right now."

Sarah turned her head on the bundled flannel shirt. Her eyes were brown, same as Joel's. Same intensity, too. At twelve years old she already had that look, the Joel Miller look, the one that said "I'm not buying what you're selling but I'll listen to the pitch."

"My dad doesn't trust you," she said.

"I noticed."

"But you saved my life."

"I helped."

"No. Dad said you stopped the bleeding. He said when he got to me the soldier had already..." She paused. "He thought I was dead. He was holding me and he thought I was dead and then you showed up with bandages and you fixed it."

Marcus didn't know what to say to that. The simplicity of it, the way she laid it out like a math problem, X happened then Y happened then Z, made it harder to deflect.

"I was in the right place," he said.

"Nobody's in the right place during something like that. Not by accident." She crunched another cracker. "You came looking for us."

It wasn't a question. Marcus felt something cold move through his chest. Not fear. Surprise. At twelve, with a bullet wound and a ruined world, Sarah Miller was sitting in a wagon on the side of the road and taking him apart with observations that most adults would have missed.

"You're pretty sharp," he said.

"I know." No false modesty. Joel's daughter. "So did you? Come looking for us?"

He thought about lying. He thought about deflecting. He thought about changing the subject and asking about her hip or offering more crackers or pointing at something in the distance.

"Yeah," he said. "I did."

Sarah was quiet for a while. She ate another cracker. A grasshopper landed on the rim of the wagon and she watched it without moving, letting it sit there on the blue canvas, rubbing its legs together.

"Why?"

"Because I heard the gunshot. And I knew someone was hurt."

"But how did you know where to go? Dad said you showed up out of nowhere. At the highway."

"I followed the sounds."

"From where?"

"From a few blocks away."

"A few blocks, and you ran toward gunshots." She said it the way someone would say "you jumped into a burning building." With a mix of disbelief and something that might have been reluctant respect. "That's either really brave or really stupid."

"Probably both."

The grasshopper jumped away. Sarah watched it go.

"My dad's name is Joel," she said. "In case you didn't know."

"I know."

"And my uncle Tommy."

"I know."

"And I'm Sarah."

"I know that too."

She looked at him sideways. "You know a lot for someone who just followed sounds."

Marcus almost laughed. Almost. He managed to turn it into something that was more of a breath than a sound. This kid was going to be a problem. The good kind. The kind that kept you honest because lying to her felt like trying to cheat at a game she'd already figured out.

"Yeah," he said. "I do."

Sarah seemed to accept that. Not as an answer. More like a bookmark. She was putting a pin in it, filing it away, and she'd come back to it later when she had more pieces of the puzzle.

"Can I have another cracker?" she asked.

"Take the whole sleeve."

She did. They sat in silence and watched the windmill turn and waited for Joel and Tommy to come back.

---

The farmhouse was clear.

Three bedrooms, a kitchen, a living room with a wood stove. Running water from the well, powered by the windmill. No electricity, but the stove had a propane tank that was two-thirds full. There was canned food in the pantry. Not a lot, but enough to supplement what Marcus carried.

Someone had lived here recently. Dishes in the drying rack. A magazine on the coffee table, open to an article about cattle feed prices. A pair of reading glasses on the kitchen counter. Whoever they were, they'd left in a hurry. The back door was unlocked and a suitcase lay open on the bedroom floor, half-packed. They'd started to run and then ran faster than packing allowed.

Joel carried Sarah inside and set her on the couch. She grabbed a throw pillow and hugged it against her chest, wincing at the movement, and looked around the room with an expression that Marcus recognized. The expression of someone standing in a dead stranger's home and knowing that the strangest part was how normal it all looked.

Tommy secured the perimeter. Checked every window lock. Tested the back door. Walked the barn and came back with a report: one tractor, no fuel, a pile of hay bales, and a calico cat that hissed at him from the rafters.

"No infected in any direction," Tommy said. "Nearest neighbors are a mile south, and that place looks empty too. We're good here."

Marcus dismissed the notification before anyone noticed him staring at nothing. Four hundred and eighty-eight points. The quest reward had nearly topped him back up. And the perk would stretch his supplies further. The system was rewarding him for doing what he'd have done anyway, which was either good design or manipulation, and he wasn't in a position to be picky about the difference.

...

Evening came slow and gold, the way Texas evenings did when there was still enough atmosphere to scatter the light properly. The smoke haze had thinned to a gauze that softened everything, made the fields outside the windows look like a photograph with a filter on it. Pretty, if you didn't think about why the air was hazy.

Tommy heated canned soup on the propane stove. Chicken noodle. The smell filled the kitchen and made the farmhouse feel like a place where people lived instead of a place where people had fled. Sarah ate an entire bowl and half of a second one, which Joel watched with an expression of naked relief that he clearly thought he was hiding.

"Easy," Joel said, when she reached for the pot again.

"I'm hungry."

"You'll make yourself sick."

"I'll make myself less hungry."

Joel looked at Tommy. Tommy shrugged. "She's got you there."

Sarah got her third helping.

After dinner, Marcus changed Sarah's bandages again. The wound looked better. The swelling had gone down. The edges were clean. She gritted her teeth through the process and didn't make a sound, which was either bravery or stubbornness or both. Joel held her hand and looked at the ceiling.

"A few weeks," Marcus said. "With rest and antibiotics, she'll be on her feet."

"Few weeks," Joel repeated. He said it the way you'd say "a million miles." A distance too large to comprehend from where he was standing.

"We've got time. This place is solid. Water, walls, a stove. We can stay."

Joel nodded. Not agreement, exactly. Acceptance. The nod of a man who was too tired to argue and too smart not to recognize that the stranger with the supplies was right about the practical stuff, even if everything else about him was a question mark the size of Texas.

Tommy took first watch. He sat in an armchair by the front window with the handgun on his knee and a can of Sprite from the gas station on the side table. Joel settled on the floor next to the couch where Sarah was already falling asleep, her hand dangling off the edge, her fingers brushing Joel's shoulder.

Marcus sat on the back porch.

The night was quiet. Actually quiet, not the fake quiet of Austin where the silence was just a gap between sirens. Crickets. The creak of the windmill. A breeze through the dry grass that sounded like paper shuffling. Stars were visible in the patches between the smoke haze, faint and far away and completely indifferent to what was happening on the ground.

"Status," he thought.

**[Perks: Efficient Provider — 10% discount on consumables]**
**[Evaluation: Survived Day 2. Found shelter. Protected the group. Adequate.]**

"Adequate," he muttered. "Thanks, system. Really feeling the love."

The back door opened. Joel stepped out onto the porch. He didn't sit down. He stood at the railing and looked out at the dark fields and the stars and the faint orange smudge on the southern horizon that was all that remained of Austin's glow.

Neither of them spoke for a while.

"She asked about the soldier," Joel said finally. Not looking at Marcus. Looking at the dark. "Asked me why he shot her. I didn't have an answer."

Marcus didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say. A soldier had shot a twelve-year-old because someone on a radio told him to shoot everyone, and no answer in the world was going to make that make sense to the twelve-year-old it happened to.

"She trusts you," Joel said. "I can tell. She's careful about people, gets that from me, but she's already decided about you."

"Is that a problem?"

Joel turned his head. In the dark, his face was all angles and shadow. The face of a man who was thirty-six and looked fifty and had watched his daughter almost die eighteen hours ago.

"You know what happened last night," Joel said. "I was holdin' her and she wasn't movin'. I could feel the blood on my hands and it was warm and there was too much of it and I thought..." He stopped. Swallowed. "I thought that was it. I thought I was holdin' my little girl while she died."

A pause. The windmill creaked.

"And then you showed up. Out of nowhere. With bandages that came out of thin air. And you saved her." Joel's voice was low and rough, like the words were being dragged out of him against their will. "I don't understand you. I don't trust you. But my daughter is alive because of you, and I don't know what to do with that."

Marcus looked at him. At Joel Miller. Not the Joel from the game, the grizzled survivor with the grey beard and the thousand-yard stare and the willingness to massacre an entire hospital to save one girl. This was a different man. A younger man. A man who still knew how to cry and who talked to his sleeping daughter about runny eggs and whose biggest concern, until eighteen hours ago, had been whether his truck would pass inspection.

Twenty years from now, this man would be beaten to death with a golf club in a ski lodge in Jackson, Wyoming. Marcus knew that. Knew it like he knew the plot of a movie he'd watched too many times. And looking at Joel now, at the father on the porch who couldn't say thank you because thank you was too small for what he felt, Marcus understood something that he hadn't understood while playing the game.

Joel Miller wasn't a character. He was a dad. Just a dad who'd been lucky enough to get his kid back.

"You don't have to trust me," Marcus said. "You don't have to understand me. Just keep doing what you're doing."

"What's that?"

"Being her dad."

Joel was quiet for a long time. The crickets filled the silence. The windmill turned. The orange glow on the horizon pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat that was slowing down.

"Get some sleep," Joel said. "I'll take second watch."

He went back inside. The screen door swung shut behind him with a soft clap.

Marcus sat on the porch and listened to the crickets and looked at the stars and thought about the twenty years ahead of him. The things he knew. The things he could change. The man sleeping on the floor next to his daughter who had no idea how much he was going to lose, unless Marcus could stop it.

"One chapter at a time," he said to nobody.

The system didn't respond. The crickets didn't care. But somewhere inside the dark farmhouse, Sarah Miller was breathing steadily on a stranger's couch, and Joel Miller was sitting on the floor beside her with his hand on her arm, and Tommy Miller was keeping watch by a window with a warm Sprite and a handgun, and for now, for one night in the middle of everything falling apart, that was enough.
