# Chapter 20: The Long Game

## — Marcus —

Day forty-four. Clear sky. Temperature in the low thirties.

Marcus left the dam compound at first light with the Glock, the hunting knife, a canteen of water, and forty rounds of ammunition he'd bought from the Mall at twelve points. Joel had wanted to come. Marcus had talked him out of it.

"Somebody needs to help Tommy with the generator windings. You're the only one who can hold the stator housing while he tests the insulation."

Joel had looked at him for a long moment. The look that said he knew Marcus was making excuses and was deciding whether to care.

"Stay on the road where the map works," Joel said. "And if you see more than six in a group, you come back."

"Yes sir."

"Don't call me sir."

Marcus headed north along the Crystal River, past the town of Marble they'd cleared yesterday. The general store's door was still open from where they'd come through. A bloodstain on the front step was already turning brown in the cold air. He kept walking.

The map showed contacts scattered through the valley and up into the surrounding hills. Mostly red. Mostly Level 1 Runners. A few orange dots, which meant Stalkers or something the system wasn't sure about. The nearest cluster was two miles north, a group of ranch properties set back from the old county road where the valley widened before climbing into the national forest.

Two miles was nothing now. Six weeks ago, two miles through infected territory would have had his hands shaking. Now it was a commute.

He walked along the road. Frost on the asphalt. His boots crunched on it. The river kept him company on the left, running fast and clear over rocks smoothed by centuries of snowmelt. The aspens along the bank had dropped their leaves weeks ago and stood bare and pale, like bones sticking out of the hillside.

A hawk circled above the ridgeline. No sound except the river and his breathing and the crunch of frost under his feet.

This was the part of the apocalypse that nobody talked about. The quiet. The sheer, overwhelming quiet of a world without traffic or construction or music from passing cars or the low hum of civilization doing its thing. Just wind and water and birds and the occasional pop of a tree branch dropping in the cold.

He could have stayed on this road forever.

But red dots don't clear themselves.

---

The first ranch was a low-slung log house with a detached barn and a fenced pasture gone to scrub. Three Runners inside the house. Marcus could hear them through the walls, the wet shuffling and the occasional click of teeth. Not Clickers. Runners who'd been here long enough to start losing coordination but hadn't developed the fungal plating yet.

He went in through the kitchen door. It was unlocked. Country people out here probably never locked their doors. The kitchen was small and dated. Green linoleum floor. A rooster-shaped clock on the wall, stopped at 3:17. A coffee mug on the counter with a faded logo that read MARBLE MOUNTAIN OUTFITTERS.

The first Runner was in the hallway. A man in work boots and jeans, face half-covered in fungal threads. Marcus took him from behind, knife through the base of the skull. The body dropped and he caught it by the collar, lowering it to the floor instead of letting it crash.

**[Runner killed. +7 points]**

The second was in the master bedroom. A woman in a nightgown. She'd been standing in the corner, facing the wall, doing whatever Runners did when there was nothing to chase. She turned when she heard Marcus's boot on the hardwood. Came at him fast. He sidestepped and put the knife in her temple. Clean. The angle Joel had corrected him on yesterday.

**[Runner killed. +8 points]**

The third was in a bedroom down the hall. A teenager. Maybe sixteen. Posters on the wall. A guitar in the corner. A desk with homework still on it, the paper curling at the edges.

Marcus stood in the doorway and looked at the kid. The fungal growth had consumed most of the left side of his face. One eye was still visible, filmed over and white. His mouth worked silently, opening and closing.

He was wearing a Broncos jersey. Number 18. Peyton Manning.

Marcus killed him quickly and didn't look at the homework.

**[Runner killed. +7 points]**

He searched the house. Found a box of .22 rounds in the closet. A can of propane for the grill. A wool blanket that smelled like dust but was intact. He took all of it. Real scavenging, not Mall purchases.

The barn had two more Runners. Farm hands, maybe. Or neighbors who'd come looking for help. They were faster than the ones in the house, more alert. Marcus used the Glock for the first one when it charged out of a horse stall, and the knife for the second when it came around the corner of a hay bale and he didn't have time to aim.

**[Runners killed (x2). +15 points]**
**[Streak 5: +10 points]**

He sat on a hay bale and drank from the canteen. The barn smelled like old straw and motor oil and death. A tractor sat in the back corner with flat tires and a layer of dust on the seat. A saddle hung on the wall next to a row of bridles.

Normal things. Things from a world where people rode horses and fixed tractors and came home to kitchens with rooster clocks.

He checked the map. More red dots further north. A cluster of four at what looked like another ranch. Two singles scattered in the forest. And an orange dot at the edge of the map's range, up near the trailhead for a hiking path.

Keep going.

---

The second ranch was easier. Four Runners in a horse barn. They were sluggish, cold-slowed. Marcus worked through them methodically, one at a time, using the narrow aisle between stalls to funnel them. No surprises. No fumbles.

**[Runners killed (x4). +28 points]**

The singles in the forest took longer. He had to leave the road and push through dense spruce on a deer trail that wound up the hillside. The first was a hiker, still wearing a daypack with a water bottle and trail mix inside. The trail mix was expired but the water bottle was fine. The second was sitting against a tree, head down, not moving until Marcus was three feet away.

That one startled him. He jumped back, tripped on a root, and ended up on his back with the Runner stumbling toward him. He shot it with the Glock from the ground. Messy. Loud. A reminder that six weeks of practice didn't make him special.

**[Runners killed (x2). +14 points]**

He lay on the forest floor for a minute after that, looking up at the spruce canopy. His heart was going fast. Cold sweat on his neck.

"Getting cocky," he said to nobody. "Don't get cocky."

The orange dot was a mile further up the trail. Marcus debated. Joel's rule was six or fewer. This was one contact, but orange meant the system wasn't sure what it was. Could be a Stalker. Could be a Clicker.

He went.

The trailhead parking lot had three cars in it, all with flat tires and frost on the windshields. A wooden sign read CRYSTAL RIVER TRAIL — 4.2 MI TO LEAD KING BASIN. A map under glass showed the trail winding up through alpine meadows and old mining roads.

The orange dot was inside the restroom building. A squat concrete structure with steel doors, the kind built to survive anything up to and including a direct hit from a toddler.

Marcus stood outside and listened.

Nothing. No clicking. No shuffling. Just the faint sound of dripping water inside.

He opened the door slowly. Dark inside. The smell hit him first, the sour-sweet rot of Cordyceps fungal growth, stronger than Runners, thicker. He pulled the flashlight from his jacket and clicked it on.

A Stalker.

It was pressed into the corner between the wall and the sink, crouched low, fungal growth spreading across its shoulders and up the left side of its head. Its eyes were open and tracking. Watching him. Not charging. Watching.

That was the difference between Runners and Stalkers. Runners came at you. Stalkers decided when.

Marcus kept the flashlight on it and drew the Glock with his other hand. The Stalker shifted its weight. Muscles in its legs tensed.

He fired twice. The first round caught it in the chest and knocked it back against the wall. The second hit the head. It slid down the tiles and stopped moving.

**[Stalker killed. +22 points]**
**[New area cleared: Crystal River Trailhead — Bonus: +15 points]**

He stood there for a moment, breathing. Stalkers were different. The way they watched. The intelligence behind the fungal growth, or whatever passed for intelligence in a brain that was ninety percent mushroom. Runners were tragic. Stalkers were unsettling.

**[Points: 696]**

Not bad for a morning. A hundred and twenty-six points from one clearing run. He could do this every day, push the perimeter further, clear the valley and the surrounding forest mile by mile. Build a buffer zone around the dam where nothing infected moved.

The system's economy made sense now. Early game, you scraped for every point, buying bandages and canned soup. Mid game, you farmed efficiently and saved for infrastructure. The transition happened somewhere around the time you stopped being afraid of Runners and started seeing them as resources.

That was a dark thought, and he let himself sit with it for a minute.

Then he holstered the Glock and started walking back.

---

## — Sarah —

The valley entrance was a natural chokepoint. One road in, river on one side, steep rock face on the other. Sarah sat on a boulder above the road with the rifle across her knees and her journal open beside her.

She could see half a mile down the road from up here. Further on clear days. Today was clear.

Nothing moved on the road. Nothing had moved on the road all morning. A deer had crossed about an hour ago, stepping carefully over the frost, and a hawk had been circling since sunrise. That was it. The sum total of traffic in post-apocalypse Colorado.

She wrote in her journal:

_Day 44. Valley entrance watch. Nobody coming. Deer and a hawk. Tommy says the turbine might spin tomorrow. Marcus is out clearing north. Joel is helping Tommy with the generator. I have the rifle and nothing to shoot at._

_The room is good. Cold at night but the sleeping bag handles it. Need glass for the window. Or something better than cardboard._

_I can hear the river from here. It sounds different than the rivers in Texas. Faster. Colder. Like it's in a hurry._

She closed the journal and watched the road.

The rifle was a .30-06 bolt action that Marcus had bought from the Mall three weeks ago. She could load it, chamber a round, sight, and fire in under four seconds. Joel had timed her. He'd said "not bad" which, in Joel Miller language, meant she was doing well.

She scanned the tree line again. Nothing.

Being bored on watch was good. Bored meant nothing was trying to kill them. She'd take bored over interesting every day of the week.

---

## — Marcus —

He got back to the dam at midday. Sarah was at the valley entrance, right where she was supposed to be. She waved from the boulder. He waved back.

Joel and Tommy were in the powerhouse. Tommy was elbow-deep in the generator, testing winding insulation with a multimeter Marcus had bought him that morning for nineteen points. Joel held the stator housing at a specific angle with both hands while Tommy probed connections.

"How's it look?" Marcus asked from the doorway.

"Windings are good on phases one and two," Tommy said without looking up. "Phase three has some degradation. I can work around it. We'll lose about fifteen percent capacity, but that still gives us a hundred and seventy kilowatts. Plenty."

"When?"

"Tomorrow afternoon. Maybe tomorrow night. I want to check the intake gate mechanism before we open the penstock. If the gate's rusted in place, we'll need to free it before we can run water."

Joel lowered the housing when Tommy waved him off. He wiped his hands on his jeans and looked at Marcus.

"How many?"

"Thirteen. Eleven Runners and a Stalker at the trailhead."

Joel nodded. Didn't ask about the Stalker. Joel understood that some fights needed talking about and some didn't, and he could tell the difference by looking at Marcus's face.

"We need to talk about the supply run," Marcus said.

They gathered around Tommy's hand-drawn site map on the workbench. Marcus laid the atlas beside it, open to western Colorado and northern New Mexico. He traced the route with his finger.

"Sweetwater, New Mexico. Four hundred eighty-seven miles. The truck gets maybe twelve miles per gallon, so we need about eighty gallons round trip. I can buy fuel from the Mall in jerry cans."

"How long?" Joel asked.

"Two days down, one day to load, two days back. Five days total if nothing goes wrong."

"Things go wrong."

"Things go wrong," Marcus agreed. "Call it a week."

"Roads?"

"Highway 133 north to Carbondale, then I-70 west to Grand Junction, south on I-25 through Albuquerque, then east to Sweetwater. Interstates are probably the safest bet. Wide lanes, good sightlines, less chance of an ambush."

"Interstates are also where every group of armed idiots with a truck sets up a roadblock."

"Yeah."

Joel studied the map. His finger traced an alternate route along state highways. "Back roads are slower but quieter. Stay off the interstates until you're past the populated areas."

"You and me," Marcus said. "Tommy stays on the turbine. Sarah stays on watch."

"I know the plan." Joel folded his arms. "When do we leave?"

"Day after tomorrow. Let Tommy finish the turbine first. If something goes wrong while we're gone, Sarah needs power, not promises."

Joel looked at the map for another few seconds. Then he turned and went back to the powerhouse to hold things for Tommy.

Conversation over. Joel Miller, master of the efficient exit.

...

The afternoon was quiet. Marcus spent it doing things that felt absurdly normal. He patched a section of fence near the residence with wire and posts. He stacked the canned goods from yesterday's Marble run into the kitchen shelves Sarah had cleaned. He bought glass panes from the Mall for sixty-two points and replaced the broken window in Sarah's bedroom, which cost him an hour of careful work with a putty knife and glazing compound.

**[Points: 615]**

When he finished, the east-facing window was clear and clean, letting in a bar of afternoon light that crossed the floor just like the morning light had. Better. She'd asked for a window and now she had one that worked.

He left before she saw it. Let it be a surprise.

---

## — Marcus —

He couldn't sleep.

The generator hummed outside. Tommy's breathing was slow and steady from the corner where he'd claimed a camp cot. Joel was on watch, somewhere outside. Sarah was in the residence, in her room with the new window she'd found and hadn't said anything about except to punch Marcus in the shoulder and call him an idiot, which was Sarah's way of saying thank you.

Marcus lay on his sleeping bag and stared at the ceiling.

The fluorescent lights were off. Just the faint glow of the generator's indicator panel through the window, casting a green square on the concrete wall. He watched it pulse.

Twenty years.

That's what was ahead of them. Twenty years before Joel walked into that hospital in Salt Lake City and killed everyone between him and Ellie. Twenty years before Abby Anderson picked up a golf club and caved in Joel's skull on the floor of a ski lodge while Ellie screamed and begged.

Twenty years was a long time. Long enough to build a city. Long enough to raise a family. Long enough to forget that the man sleeping thirty feet away, the man who'd held a stator housing for four hours without complaining because his brother asked him to, was supposed to become something else entirely.

In the game, Joel spent those twenty years becoming a monster. Not the kind of monster that enjoyed what he did. The kind that stopped feeling anything about it. He and Tommy had been through things that the game only hinted at. Ambushes. Torture. Killing innocent people to take their supplies. Tommy had left because he couldn't stomach it anymore. Joel had kept going because stopping meant dying, and dying meant Sarah had died for nothing.

Except Sarah hadn't died. She was sleeping in a ranch house fifty yards away with a journal on the windowsill and a rifle by the door and a scar on her hip that she'd carry forever.

Did that change everything? Or just some things?

Marcus rolled onto his side.

Joel right now was a man who'd lost his daughter eleven days ago and gotten her back through a stranger's impossible intervention. He was still the Joel who'd been a contractor in Austin. Still the Joel who built things and fixed things and made terrible coffee and loved his brother even when his brother drove him crazy. He hadn't become the Boston smuggler yet. Hadn't done the things with Tommy that would haunt them both. Hadn't met Tess. Hadn't spent twenty years building walls so high and so thick that a fourteen-year-old girl would need months to find a crack in them.

This Joel still smiled. Barely, and only at Sarah, and only when he thought nobody was looking. But he smiled.

In the game, Marcus had watched Joel die on a screen and felt sick for a week. He'd played Part II twice and both times he'd set the controller down during the golf club scene and walked away. Not because it was poorly done. Because it was too well done. Because the game made you understand why Abby did it and made you hate that you understood.

Now Joel was real. He was a real person who snored slightly and drank bad coffee and corrected Marcus's knife angle during combat and said things like "good store" as if that was an adequate review of a scavenging run. He was a real person with a real future that Marcus had memorized from a wiki and a YouTube lore video and three playthroughs.

And somewhere out there, right now, a man named Jerry Anderson was alive. A surgeon. A Firefly. A father to a girl named Abby who was probably nine or ten years old right now, playing with friends, having no idea that her father would one day choose to cut open a child's brain for a vaccine that might not even work. No idea that a man from Texas would kill her father to stop it. No idea that she'd spend years training to murder that man in return.

The cycle hadn't started yet. The dominoes were still standing.

Marcus could knock them down differently. That was the whole point of being here. Save Sarah. Build a home. Find Joel before Boston turned him into someone who'd need saving. Find Ellie before Marlene could sacrifice her. Find Abby before grief could consume her.

Fix everything.

Easy. Just fix everything in a world full of fungal zombies, collapsing governments, armed psychopaths, and a system that gave him shopping powers and a score counter.

He almost laughed. Didn't. Tommy was sleeping.

The green light pulsed on the wall.

He ran through the timeline. The one he knew from memory.

2013: Outbreak. They were here.
2014 through 2033: Twenty years of collapse. FEDRA quarantine zones rising and falling. The Fireflies forming, growing, fracturing. Joel and Tommy separating. Tommy finding Maria, finding Jackson. Joel finding Tess, finding Boston, finding a way to exist without living.

2033: Marlene finds Ellie. Joel gets the job. The cross-country trip that changes everything. Bill. Pittsburgh. Henry and Sam. Sam gets bitten. Henry shoots himself. The University. David and the cannibals. Salt Lake City. Joel's choice.

2034 to 2038: The years between games. Ellie and Joel in Jackson. The lie festering. Ellie finding out. Their relationship fracturing and slowly, painfully healing.

2038 or 2039: Abby. The lodge. The golf club.

He could stop all of it. He was already stopping it. Sarah was alive. Joel wouldn't spend twenty years as a husk. If Marcus could get Joel to Colorado, to the community, within the next year or two, Joel would never become the man who needed to become a monster to survive.

But that meant Joel would never go to Boston. Never meet Tess. Never get Marlene's job. Never meet Ellie.

And if Joel never met Ellie, Ellie died on a surgeon's table.

The math wasn't simple. It never was, in stories like this. The tragedy and the beauty were woven into the same thread. Pull one and the other unraveled.

He needed to let the timeline run. Not all of it. Not the worst of it. But enough of it that the pieces landed where they needed to. Joel had to meet Ellie. Joel had to love Ellie enough to burn the world for her. That part was non-negotiable.

But Joel didn't have to do it alone. And Joel didn't have to spend twenty years in hell to get there.

Marcus closed his eyes. The plan was long. Twenty years long. And he couldn't tell anyone because "I played a video game about your life" was not a conversation that ended well for anyone.

So he'd build. Farm points. Grow the community. Keep Sarah safe. Keep Joel close for as long as he could. And when the time came, when Marlene found a fourteen-year-old girl in a Boston hospital, Marcus would be ready.

He'd be ready.

---

Footsteps outside. The maintenance building door opened and Joel's silhouette filled the frame, backlit by moonlight.

"You up?" Joel said quietly.

"Yeah."

Joel came inside and sat on the workbench across from Marcus. He had the SIG in his lap and a cup of something in his hand. Probably cold water. Joel didn't sleep much either.

They sat in the dark for a while. The generator hummed.

"I used to build houses," Joel said.

Marcus waited.

"Spec houses, mostly. Three-bed, two-bath, two-car garage. Cookie-cutter stuff, but the framing was solid. I'd walk through a house I built and know every stud was straight, every joint was tight. Used to drive Tommy crazy how long I'd spend on things nobody would ever see. Backing boards behind drywall. Double-nailed joists. Stuff the homeowner would never know about."

He took a drink. Water. Marcus could hear it.

"This place," Joel said. "This dam. It's got good bones. Whoever built it did the same thing I did. Put in the work where it don't show." He paused. "I think we can make something here."

Marcus looked at him in the dark. Joel's face was just shapes. The line of his jaw. The slope of his nose. The faint movement of his mouth when he was thinking and didn't know anyone was watching.

"Yeah," Marcus said. "I think so too."

Joel stood up. Put the cup down. Picked up the SIG.

"Get some sleep. Long couple of days ahead."

He went back outside. The door closed. His footsteps faded across the yard.

Marcus lay back down. The green light pulsed.

Long couple of days. Long couple of decades, really.

But Joel was right. The bones were good. And good bones were a place to start.

He closed his eyes and this time, eventually, he slept.
