# Chapter 33: New Breeds

## — Marcus —

April 21, 2015. Aspen Corridor, Colorado.

The ski lodge had been dead for eighteen months, but nobody had told the Runners.

Marcus counted four red contacts on his map, clustered in the restaurant on the first floor. Two more in the kitchen behind it. Standard placement. Runners liked enclosed spaces with doors and furniture. Something about the geometry appealed to whatever was left of the human brain under the fungus. They didn't sit at the tables or open the fridges, but they gathered in rooms that used to have purpose, like ghosts who'd forgotten why they were haunting.

"Four in the dining room, two in the kitchen," Marcus said. "Keiko, you take the kitchen entrance. Dale, you're with me on the front."

Keiko Tanaka adjusted the strap on her rifle and moved without answering. She'd been doing that for two days now. Communicating through action instead of words. It was a Joel habit she'd picked up from training, and Marcus found it both efficient and slightly unnerving.

Dale Briggs nodded. He was breathing through his mouth. Not from fear. The altitude. They were at eight thousand feet and the air was thin and cold and smelled like wet pine. Dale had been breathing through his mouth since they'd left the valley floor yesterday morning, and Marcus had stopped mentioning it after the third time.

They'd been on the road for two days. Forty-seven miles north of Crystal Falls, following Highway 82 through the Crystal River valley toward Aspen. The route was littered with abandoned vehicles, collapsed roofs, and the particular silence that meant nothing human had passed through in a very long time.

The silence was productive. Marcus had cleared thirty-one infected in the first day and a half. Mostly Runners, a few Stalkers in a gas station outside Carbondale, and one Clicker in a church that Dale had spotted by the sound before Marcus's map registered it. The Clicker kill alone had been worth 62 points.

**[Points: 923]**

Almost double what he'd started with. Another good day and he'd have enough for the emergency food supplies, water filtration expansion, and the four new cabin kits Sarah needed for the Denver refugees. He just needed to keep moving north where the density was higher.

The lodge's front doors were glass, one intact and one shattered. Marcus went through the shattered one. Dale followed three steps behind, rifle up, covering the left side of the lobby while Marcus took the right.

The lobby was trashed. Luggage carts on their sides. A reception desk with papers scattered across the floor. A brass luggage rack had toppled into a decorative fountain that hadn't held water in over a year. The carpet squished underfoot. Snowmelt had been seeping through the broken door all winter, and the whole first floor had the damp, organic smell of a building slowly returning to the earth.

The restaurant was through a set of double doors on the right. Marcus pushed one open with his left hand, knife in his right.

Four Runners. Two standing near the windows. One crouched behind an overturned table. One near the back, by the door to the kitchen.

The two by the windows turned first. Fast, twitchy, the herky-jerky movement of Stage 1 infected whose motor control had been hijacked by fungal threads wired into their nervous system. They screamed. The sound bounced off the restaurant's high ceiling and came back louder.

Marcus was already moving.

He hit the first one at full speed, knife into the throat, twist, pull. It dropped. The second swung at him and he ducked under it, drove the knife upward into the jaw, felt the blade scrape bone. It went down gurgling.

The one behind the table charged. Marcus sidestepped and let Dale take it. Three shots from the rifle. Two hit center mass, one went high and punched through the ceiling tile. The Runner folded.

The fourth was running for the kitchen.

"Keiko," Marcus said. Not shouting. Just his normal voice, loud enough.

A single gunshot from the kitchen. Then silence.

"Clear," Keiko called.

Marcus wiped the knife on a tablecloth. The cloth had been white once. Now it was the color of old dishwater.

**[Runner eliminated: +7 points]**

"Kitchen clear?" he asked.

"Two down." Keiko appeared in the doorway. Not a scratch. She had a smear of something dark on her jacket sleeve that she hadn't noticed. "The second one was in the walk-in freezer. Door was closed. It came out when it heard the shots."

Dale lowered his rifle. His hands were steady. Joel's training. Three months ago, Dale would have frozen after the first shot. Now he was checking corners and scanning doorways without being told. Joel would have approved.

"Good shooting," Marcus said.

"I missed one."

"You hit two out of three on a moving target in a dark room. That's a passing grade."

Dale didn't look convinced, but he reloaded.

They cleared the rest of the lodge floor by floor. Six more Runners on the second level, scattered through guest rooms. Two Stalkers on the third floor that gave them trouble. One hid behind a housekeeping cart and lunged at Dale from behind. Keiko shot it off his back before it could bite.

The Stalker kill was worth 22 points. Not bad. Marcus's total climbed steadily.

**[Points: 1,031]**

Over a thousand. In two days. The Aspen corridor was a goldmine. Dense buildings, lots of enclosed spaces, high concentration of infected who'd wandered in from the surrounding towns and never wandered out. If he could find a Clicker nest or something bigger, he'd hit the target by tomorrow.

They set up camp on the lodge's fourth floor. A corner suite with a balcony facing east, where the morning sun would wake them without an alarm. Dale found a stack of minibar chocolates in a hallway closet. Expired by fourteen months. They ate them anyway.

"How many tomorrow?" Keiko asked. She was cleaning her rifle by flashlight, the components laid out on a hotel pillow with military precision.

"As many as we can find. The map shows a cluster of contacts two miles northwest. Old resort area. Hotels, restaurants, shops. Could be thirty or forty."

"What kind?"

"Mostly red. Some orange."

"No Clickers?"

"Not showing on the map. But Clickers don't always register until you're close. The echolocation interferes with the scan somehow."

Keiko absorbed this. She'd fought Clickers before, in controlled scenarios with Joel directing traffic. This would be her first encounter outside the settlement's perimeter, where the backup was a 23-year-old supply runner and a guy who'd been a data analyst before the world ended.

"Get some sleep," Marcus said. "We move at first light."

Dale was already asleep on a king-size bed with his boots on. The chocolate wrappers were on the nightstand next to his rifle.

Marcus sat on the balcony with his back against the railing and opened his map. The blue glow reflected off the glass door. Below, the town of Aspen spread out in the moonlight. Dark buildings, dark streets, snow on the higher peaks. Beautiful. Empty. The kind of place where people used to pay thousands of dollars a night to feel special.

Now it was a hunting ground.

He zoomed in on the contact cluster to the northwest. Dense. Thirty-plus contacts in a two-block area centered on what the map labeled as the Limelight Hotel. Most were red. A handful of orange. And at the bottom of the map, below the building's footprint, a cluster of contacts in what had to be a basement or underground parking level.

Those contacts were a color he hadn't seen before.

Not red. Not orange. Not the deep red of a Clicker.

They were yellow.

"What the hell are you?" Marcus murmured.

The system offered no tooltip. No classification. Just yellow dots, three of them, in a flooded basement two miles from where he was sitting.

He closed the map and stared at the dark mountains. In the games, yellow wasn't a standard enemy color. He'd played both titles enough times to know every infected type, every variant, every mechanic. Runners were the baseline. Stalkers were the ambush class. Clickers were the stealth-check. Bloaters were the boss fight.

And Shamblers were the environmental hazard.

Shamblers. Wet environments. Corrosive acid gas. Area denial. They showed up in Part II, set in 2038. Twenty-three years after this moment.

"That's not possible," he said to nobody.

But the yellow dots didn't care what was possible. They sat in the flooded basement of a hotel in Aspen, Colorado, two years into the apocalypse, existing ahead of schedule.

Marcus didn't sleep well.

## — Marcus —

April 22, 2015. Aspen, Colorado.

The Limelight Hotel was six stories of glass and stone on South Monarch Street, two blocks from the gondola station. Pre-outbreak, it had been the kind of place that served $40 cocktails and offered valet parking for Range Rovers. Now the valet station was buried under eighteen months of debris, and the Range Rovers in the parking structure were missing their batteries.

They cleared the upper floors first. Twelve Runners and three Stalkers across six floors of guest rooms, conference spaces, and a spa that still smelled faintly of eucalyptus under the mold. Efficient work. Keiko took point on each floor, Dale covered the rear, Marcus moved through the center. They'd developed a rhythm over two days that wasn't quite military precision but was close enough.

**[Points: 1,214]**

The ground floor was waterlogged. Snowmelt had been pouring through broken windows all spring. The lobby carpet was submerged under two inches of brown water that sloshed around their boots. A grand piano in the corner was warped and splitting, its keys buckled upward like bad teeth.

Marcus checked the map. The red and orange contacts on the upper floors were gone. What remained were the three yellow dots below them.

"There's something in the basement," he said.

"How many?" Keiko asked.

"Three. Different color on my scan. I haven't seen it before."

Keiko looked at him. She knew about the map. She didn't know how it worked and she'd stopped asking after Marcus gave her the same non-answer he gave everyone. What she did know was that it had never been wrong.

"Different how?"

"I don't know. That's the problem."

The basement stairs were behind the front desk, through a door marked EMPLOYEES ONLY. The door was swollen with water damage and Marcus had to kick it open. Behind it, a concrete stairwell descended into darkness.

And water.

The stairs went down twelve steps before they hit the waterline. Dark, still water that reflected the beam of Marcus's flashlight back at him. The basement was flooded. Not two inches. Not ankle deep. The water was at least two feet up the walls, judging by the stain line. Spring snowmelt, maybe a broken pipe, maybe both. The basement had been sitting in it for months.

The smell hit them. Not the clean rot of mold or the sharp ammonia of decomposition. Something different. Acidic. Chemical. It burned the back of Marcus's throat like breathing near a swimming pool, but worse. Heavier.

Dale coughed. "What is that?"

"Stay here," Marcus said.

"Both of us?" Keiko asked.

Marcus hesitated. He didn't want to go down alone. But the yellow contacts were unknown, the environment was terrible, and Keiko and Dale didn't have the physical resilience to survive something he couldn't predict.

"Both of you. Stay at the top of the stairs. If I call, you come. If you hear me shooting, you come. If you hear something you've never heard before..." He paused. "You run."

"I don't run," Keiko said.

"Then you cover the stairwell and don't come past the waterline."

He didn't wait for her to agree. He went down.

The water was cold. Mountain snowmelt, maybe forty degrees. It hit his boots at step eight, his calves at step ten, his knees at the bottom. The basement opened into a large space. Storage, probably. Racks of shelving lined the walls, half of them toppled into the water. Boxes of hotel supplies floated in the murk. A crate of minibar bottles bumped against his shin.

The flashlight beam cut through the dark. Concrete pillars every fifteen feet. Low ceiling, maybe seven feet. The water was opaque. Brown. He couldn't see his own boots.

The map showed the three yellow contacts ahead and to the left. Sixty feet. Forty. Thirty.

He smelled them before he saw them.

The acidic chemical burn in his throat intensified. His eyes watered. A thin haze hung in the air above the water, visible in the flashlight beam. Not fog. Not steam. Something organic. Something the fungus was producing.

The first one stepped out from behind a pillar.

Marcus had killed Runners, Stalkers, Clickers, and a Bloater. He thought he understood the progression. He was wrong.

The Shambler was roughly human-shaped, but only roughly. The fungal growth covered its body in swollen, pustule-like masses. Bulging sacs of tissue that looked like they were filled with fluid. The head was partially visible, one eye socket still open, but the rest of the face was buried under growths that pulsed with each labored breath the thing took.

It was wet. Not from the water. The fungal growths wept a clear, viscous fluid that dripped from its arms and torso. Where the fluid hit the water, it hissed. Not steam. Chemical reaction. Acid.

"Oh no," Marcus whispered.

The Shambler lurched toward him.

It was slower than a Runner. Much slower. It moved with the plodding deliberation of something that didn't need speed because it had other tools. It opened its mouth and a cloud of gas erupted from the growths on its chest and shoulders. Yellow-green. Dense. It rolled across the water's surface like a living thing, spreading outward in every direction.

Marcus backpedaled. Fast. He got ten feet before the gas reached him and he felt it. Not pain. Worse. A burning sensation on every exposed inch of skin. His hands. His face. His neck. Like someone had rubbed sandpaper dipped in bleach across his body.

He fired. Three shots into the Shambler's center mass. The bullets hit the fungal growths and burst them. More gas. A secondary cloud erupted from the impact points, thicker than the first, and Marcus choked on it. His lungs burned.

"Marcus!" Keiko's voice from the top of the stairs.

"Stay there!" He barely got the words out. His throat was closing. Not completely, but enough that each breath felt like swallowing glass.

The second and third Shamblers emerged from behind a collapsed shelving unit. Same anatomy. Same weeping growths. Same slow, inevitable approach. The gas from all three was merging into a single cloud that filled the basement from wall to wall. The air above the water was unbreathable.

Marcus dropped below the waterline.

The cold hit him like a slap. His head went under and for a moment there was silence. Just the muffled sound of his own heartbeat and the distant slosh of something heavy moving through water. He opened his eyes. Brown murk. Nothing visible past three inches.

He came up gasping. The gas was thinner at water level. Not gone, but thinner. He could breathe in short, shallow pulls if he kept his head low.

The first Shambler was fifteen feet away now. Close enough that Marcus could see the individual pustules on its arms, each one the size of a golf ball, each one containing the acidic compound that was stripping the skin off his hands.

He holstered the gun. Bullets made it worse. Every round that hit a growth released more gas. He needed the knife.

Marcus surged forward through the water. The Shambler swung at him. Slow. He ducked under the arm, came up on its left side, and drove the knife into the base of its skull where the fungal growth was thinnest. The blade went in three inches before hitting something hard. Not bone. Calcified fungal plate. He twisted. Pushed harder. The plate cracked and the knife sank to the hilt.

The Shambler dropped.

But it didn't just drop.

It exploded.

The pustules on its body burst simultaneously in a chain reaction, releasing a concentrated wave of acidic gas and fluid that sprayed outward in every direction. Marcus threw himself backward. The fluid hit his jacket, his arms, his chest. It burned through the outer layer of his jacket in seconds. Where it touched skin, it felt like boiling water.

He was screaming. Not from fear. From the acid eating into his forearms where his sleeves had ridden up. He plunged his arms into the water. The cold killed the burn but the damage was done. Red, raw patches of skin where the outer layer had been dissolved.

The other two Shamblers were still coming.

Keiko heard the explosion.

Not a gunshot. Something wetter. Something that sounded like a pressurized container rupturing. Then Marcus screaming. Then splashing.

She was down three steps before she remembered his order.

"Dale."

"I heard it."

"Cover the stairwell. If anything that isn't Marcus comes up, shoot it."

She went down. Into the water. Into the smell. Into the gas.

It hit her at the seventh step. Her skin prickled and then burned. Her eyes watered so badly she could barely see. The flashlight beam caught a yellow-green haze hanging over the surface of the water that looked like something from a chemistry accident.

"Marcus!"

"Get back!" His voice was raw. Damaged. "Keiko, get back up the stairs!"

She didn't.

She saw him through the haze. Waist-deep in water, knife in hand, blood on his arms, facing two shapes that were moving toward him through the murk. The shapes were wrong. Too bulky. Covered in growths that were leaking fluid into the water.

One of them opened its mouth and more gas poured out.

The cloud rolled toward Keiko. She felt it hit her face and hands and she understood, in a single instant, why Marcus had told her to stay upstairs. This wasn't combat. This was chemistry. The things were releasing acid and you couldn't shoot them without making it worse.

The gas burned her right hand where she was gripping the rifle. She looked down. The skin on her knuckles was blistering. Actually blistering, in real time, like a sunburn compressed into seconds.

"Keiko!" Marcus was moving. Toward her, not toward the Shamblers. He grabbed her arm and shoved her toward the stairs. "Go! Now! Take Dale and get outside!"

"I can fight these."

"You can't. Your skin is dissolving. Go."

She went. Not because she was obedient. Because her right hand had stopped working properly. The blisters were spreading up her wrist and the pain was extraordinary. Chemical burns. She'd read about them in the first aid manual Helen kept in the clinic. First degree was redness. Second degree was blisters. Third degree was tissue death.

She was between first and second and climbing.

Dale caught her at the top of the stairs. His face was pale. He'd heard everything.

"What's down there?" he asked.

"Something new."

## — Marcus —

Marcus fought the last two Shamblers alone in a flooded basement, half-blind from acid gas, with chemical burns up both forearms and across the left side of his neck.

He didn't try to be clever about it.

The second Shambler died the same way as the first. Marcus waded in close, took the hit from the gas cloud, and put the knife through the weak point at the skull base. When it exploded, he was already underwater, holding his breath, letting the water absorb the worst of the acid spray. He came up choking and burned but alive.

The third one was smarter. Or the fungus was smarter. It had positioned itself behind a concrete pillar, using the structure as a shield. Marcus had to circle around, sloshing through chest-deep water in the far corner of the basement where a collapsed shelving unit created a maze of submerged metal.

His foot caught on something under the water. A chair leg, maybe. He stumbled. The Shambler lunged.

It was slow. But it didn't need to be fast when it was three feet away.

The gas hit Marcus full in the face. He couldn't breathe. Couldn't see. He stabbed blindly. The knife hit a fungal growth on the thing's shoulder and a spray of acid caught him across the jaw and left ear. The pain was white and total.

He grabbed the Shambler by its throat. The growths burst under his fingers, leaking acid onto his hand. He didn't let go. He dragged it toward him, found the skull base by feel, and drove the knife home.

It exploded. Marcus went under. This time he stayed under for a ten-count, letting the current from his own movement carry the acid away. When he came up, the basement was empty except for the gas and the floating remains of three things that should not have existed for another twenty years.

He dragged himself up the stairs on hands and knees. The chemical burns covered both forearms, his left hand, the left side of his neck, and a strip across his jaw. He bought both. The kit appeared in his pack. Dale, who had stopped asking questions about the supply situation on the second day, just unzipped it and started working.

The burn cream helped. Not enough. But enough to stop the spreading and take the edge off the pain. Dale dressed Keiko's hand first, then Marcus's forearms, then his neck and jaw. His touch was careful. Precise. The data analyst had become a competent field medic through months of Dr. Reyes's training and a willingness to pay attention.

"What were those?" Keiko asked. She was sitting against the hotel wall, her bandaged hand held against her chest. Her voice was flat. Pain control. The voice people used when they were hurting too much to let any emotion into the sound.

"Shamblers," Marcus said.

"That's not a thing."

"It is now."

"They're not in any of the briefing materials. Joel's threat classification doesn't include anything that produces gas."

"Joel's threat classification is based on what we've encountered so far. Runners. Stalkers. Clickers. Bloaters. This is new."

"New," Keiko repeated. "The fungus is making new things."

Marcus didn't answer right away. He was thinking about timelines. About the game he'd played in another life, in another world. Shamblers appeared in The Last of Us Part II. Set in 2038. Twenty-three years after Outbreak Day. The developers had introduced them as a wet-environment variant of the Bloater, evolved over decades of exposure to moisture.

This was 2015. Year two. The fungus had been loose for eighteen months.

Either the games were wrong about the timeline, or the real version of Cordyceps was evolving faster than the fictional one. Neither option was comforting.

"The infected go through stages," Marcus said. "Runners first. Then Stalkers. Clickers. Bloaters. Those things in the basement are a variant. Stage 4, like Bloaters, but adapted for wet environments. The moisture accelerates something in the fungal growth. The pustules fill with acid. When they rupture, the gas is corrosive. When they die, they explode."

"How do you know all that?" Dale asked.

The question hung in the air. Marcus could hear the creek. A bird. The wind through the pine trees on the ridge above town.

"Same way I know everything else," he said. "And I was wrong about the timeline."

Dale looked confused. Keiko looked like she was filing the information away for later, the way she filed away everything Marcus said that didn't make sense. Her file was getting thick.

"Can we kill them?" Keiko asked. The practical question. The Keiko question.

"Yes. But not with guns. Every bullet that hits a growth releases more gas. You have to get close. Knife to the skull base, where the fungal plate is thinnest. Then get underwater before the death burst."

"Underwater."

"The acid disperses in water. Doesn't neutralize, but disperses enough that it won't kill you."

Keiko looked at her bandaged hand. Then at Marcus's bandaged arms. Then at the hotel behind them.

"We need to tell Joel," she said.

"We need to tell everyone."

## — Marcus —

They headed south at noon.

Marcus had planned three days. They were cutting it to two and a half. Keiko could walk and shoot left-handed. Dale was uninjured. Marcus's burns were ugly but functional. The system's passive healing was already working on the tissue damage, the edges of the burns turning from raw red to the angry pink of accelerated recovery.

But pushing further north with an injured team member and an unknown number of Shamblers potentially in every flooded basement between here and Independence Pass was stupid. Marcus was many things. Stupid wasn't one of them most days.

They walked in silence for the first mile. The highway south was empty. April sunshine on wet asphalt. The mountains were green at the base and white at the peaks. Pretty. Indifferent.

**[Points: 1,859]**

After the burn kit purchases. Not the two thousand he'd promised Sarah, but close. Enough for the food supplies and the water filtration parts. The cabin kits would have to wait for another run, or he'd have to find something worth killing between here and Crystal Falls.

"Marcus," Dale said. They were passing a gas station south of Basalt. The roof had collapsed inward but the pumps were still standing. "How many types of infected are there?"

"I thought I knew. I'm less sure now."

"What's worse than what we just fought?"

Marcus considered lying. Considered saying the Shamblers were the top of the evolutionary chain and everything from here was just more of the same in different packaging.

He didn't.

"There's one more. Something called a Rat King. Multiple infected bodies fused together over decades. I've never seen one. I hope I never do."

Dale walked in silence for a while.

"Decades," he repeated.

"Yeah."

"So this isn't going away."

It wasn't a question. Marcus didn't treat it like one.

"No. The fungus isn't going away. It's getting more complex. The Shamblers in that basement were only possible because of the water and the enclosed space. Every flooded building, every waterlogged tunnel, every basement with standing water is a potential breeding ground for that variant. And who knows what other environments produce other variants we haven't seen yet."

"Do we tell the settlement?"

"We tell the council. Joel needs to update the threat classifications. Dr. Reyes needs to know about acid burns. The militia needs new protocols for flooded spaces."

"What protocol? 'Don't go in flooded basements'?"

Marcus almost smiled. The left side of his face hurt too much for a real one.

"That's not bad for a first draft."

They made it back to the outskirts of Crystal Falls at dusk on April 22nd. Marcus could see the lights from three miles out. The dam. The cabins. The greenhouse. The dining hall where a hundred and forty-eight people were eating dinner and arguing about portions and complaining about the work schedule and doing all the small, tedious, beautiful things that living people do.

He stopped on the ridge. Keiko and Dale stopped behind him.

The lights below were the reason he'd killed thirty-nine Runners, five Stalkers, one Clicker, and three things that shouldn't exist yet. Two thousand points on legs that he'd converted into food and medicine and building materials for people he hadn't known a year ago.

The burns on his arms would heal. The knowledge wouldn't go away.

The infected were evolving. Faster than the games had predicted. Faster than he'd planned for. Every model he'd built in his head, every timeline he'd mapped from two playthroughs of a video game in another life, was based on assumptions that were now provably wrong.

The Shamblers were twenty years early. What else was early? What else was the fungus cooking up in flooded basements and dark tunnels and places his map couldn't reach?

He didn't know.

That was the part that kept him standing on the ridge long after Keiko and Dale had started walking down toward the lights.
