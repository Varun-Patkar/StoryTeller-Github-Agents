# Chapter 40: Fireflies

## — Marcus —

December 2020. Crystal Falls, Colorado.

Noah drew a dog every morning.

Not the same dog. Different dogs. Fat dogs, thin dogs, dogs with spots, dogs with three legs. Captain had become the center of a five-year-old's universe, and that universe expressed itself in pencil on the backs of supply requisition forms, one drawing per day, presented to whoever was closest when it was finished.

Today's dog had wings.

"That's Captain?" Marcus asked.

Noah nodded. He was sitting at the kitchen table in a flannel shirt Sarah had cut down from one of Marcus's old ones, sleeves rolled three times, collar loose around his neck. His cheeks had filled out over the past three months. His arms weren't sticks anymore. He still didn't talk much, but the silence had changed from hollow to comfortable. The difference between a kid who couldn't speak and a kid who just didn't feel like it.

"Dogs don't have wings," Marcus said.

"This one does."

Four words. Three months ago, the only word had been "Mama." Now there were sentences. Short ones, offered carefully, like coins he wasn't sure he wanted to spend. But they were there.

Sarah came downstairs with her clipboard and a pencil behind her ear. She looked at the drawing. Looked at Marcus. Looked at Noah.

"Captain can't fly," she said.

"He could if he wanted to," Noah said.

Sarah kissed the top of his head. Noah didn't flinch. That had taken six weeks.

"I'm doing south greenhouse rounds, then the inventory audit with Helen," Sarah said. "Joel's got him until lunch?"

"Joel's got him."

Joel and Noah had found each other the way quiet people sometimes did. Joel didn't push conversation. Noah didn't want it pushed. They'd sit together in the workshop, Joel building or sharpening or fixing something, Noah drawing at the corner of the workbench, Captain asleep between them. An hour could pass without a word. Joel said it reminded him of early mornings with Sarah before the outbreak, when she'd sit at the kitchen counter doing homework while he made coffee. The presence was the point, not the talking.

Marcus finished his chicory. Still terrible. He'd been drinking it for seven years and it had never gotten better. Some things in the apocalypse you just accepted.

The system map showed green across the valley. No contacts within twenty miles. A quiet December morning, frost on the greenhouse glass, woodsmoke from the bunkhouses drifting straight up in windless air. The kind of morning that made you forget the world had ended, which was exactly the kind of morning Marcus didn't trust.

He was halfway to the north gate for his morning perimeter walk when Donna's whistle cut the air.

Two short, one long. Not the alarm pattern. The "unknowns approaching" pattern.

...

Marcus changed direction and headed for the main gate. Joel was already there, rifle slung across his back, standing with Donna on the observation platform. Helen was three steps behind with her clipboard, because Helen was always three steps behind with her clipboard.

"Two contacts," Donna said. "South approach, on foot. They triggered the creek crossing tripwire eight minutes ago and walked straight up the road. Not hiding."

Marcus looked south through the binoculars Donna handed him. Two people, maybe a quarter mile out. A man and a woman. Mid-thirties, rough clothing, packs. Armed but weapons holstered. Walking at a steady pace, hands visible.

Not bandits. Bandits didn't walk up the main road with their hands out. Not refugees either. Refugees moved in groups, looked scared, and carried everything they owned. These two moved like people on a mission. Purposeful. Confident.

"They see the gate," Joel said.

"They saw it before the creek," Donna said. "They've been watching the valley."

Joel unslung his rifle. Not pointing it. Just holding it where it could be pointed. "I'll go down."

"I'll come with you," Marcus said.

"I got it."

"I know. I'm coming anyway."

Joel gave him the look. The one that said "I don't need babysitting" and also "Fine, do what you want." Seven years of that look. Marcus knew all its variations.

They walked down to the road gate together. Tommy appeared from the powerhouse, wiping grease from his hands on a rag. He fell in beside them without being asked. The three of them stood at the gate and waited.

The two strangers stopped twenty feet out. Close enough to talk, far enough to not seem threatening. Professional distance. The man was tall, lean, dark hair going grey at the temples. He wore a canvas jacket patched at the elbows and carried a hunting rifle on a shoulder strap. The woman was shorter, compact, with close-cropped black hair and a medic's satchel across her chest. Both of them wore something on chains around their necks.

Pendants. Metal, slightly tarnished. Shaped like insects.

Fireflies.

Marcus felt the recognition land in his chest like a cold stone. He'd been expecting this. Not today, not specifically, but eventually. Crystal Falls was too big, too bright, too successful to stay invisible forever. FEDRA had found them three years ago. The Fireflies were slower, but they were thorough.

"Morning," the man said. He had a calm voice, the kind that came from practice rather than nature. A teacher's voice, maybe, or someone who'd spent years learning to sound reasonable. "My name is Lieutenant Navarro. This is Corporal Kim. We're with the Fireflies."

Joel said nothing. He stood with his rifle across his body, eyes flat, reading the two of them the way he read everyone. Looking for the lie. Looking for the angle.

"What do you want?" Tommy asked.

"Honestly?" Navarro said. "We want to talk. We've been hearing about Crystal Falls for a year. A self-sustaining settlement with power, agriculture, and a governing council. In a world where most communities don't last six months, that's worth a conversation."

"We're not recruitin'," Joel said.

"We're not asking you to recruit. We're asking for thirty minutes of someone's time."

Marcus studied them. Navarro's heartbeat was steady at seventy-two. Not lying, or at least not nervous about lying. Kim's was slightly elevated, eighty-eight, which could mean anything from anxiety to a faster baseline. Her medic's satchel was genuine. Worn at the seams, stained with iodine, the kind of bag that had been opened in actual emergencies. A real medic, not someone playing dress-up.

"Come inside," Marcus said. "We'll talk."

Joel looked at him. The look that said "I don't like this." Marcus gave him the look that said "I know."

They opened the gate.

____

They met in the council room. Frank's pine table, six chairs, the patrol schedule pinned to the wall beside Mia's faded crayon drawing. Marcus sat across from Navarro and Kim. Joel stood by the door, leaning against the frame, arms folded. Tommy sat at the end of the table, tilted back in his chair, doing the relaxed cowboy thing he did when he wanted someone to underestimate him.

Navarro placed his Firefly pendant on the table. The metal caught the overhead light. A small silver firefly, tarnished green at the edges, with a serial number etched on the back.

"Marlene sends her regards," Navarro said.

The name hit Marcus differently than it should have. Marlene. He'd never met her. He'd never been to Boston. But he knew her face, her voice, her choices. He knew the operating room in Salt Lake City. He knew the surgeon with the scalpel. He knew the fourteen-year-old girl on the table, unconscious, about to die for a vaccine that might not even work.

Thirteen years from now. That was the math. Ellie was about a year old somewhere in Boston right now, if the timeline held. A baby with no idea what she was. And Marlene was already building the machine that would try to kill her.

Marcus kept his face neutral. He'd had seven years of practice.

"How is Marlene?" he asked.

"Leading. As always." Navarro leaned forward slightly. "She's been consolidating Firefly operations in the northeast. The QZs are failing. Boston is holding, barely. Philadelphia fell last spring. Pittsburgh is a warzone run by hunters. FEDRA can't control what it has, let alone take back what it's lost."

"And the Fireflies can?"

"We're trying. We're building networks between settlements. Supply lines, communication, shared defense. The goal is the same as it's always been. Restore some version of democratic governance. Find a cure."

"A cure," Marcus said.

"The Cordyceps fungus is evolving. You've seen it, haven't you? The Shamblers. The Enhanced Runners. The infection is accelerating. Five years ago, a Clicker took a year to develop. Now we're seeing them in six months. If we don't find a way to stop it, there won't be enough people left to save."

Marcus nodded. All accurate. All true. He'd seen the acceleration firsthand.

"What are you asking for?" Marcus said.

Navarro glanced at Kim. She opened her satchel and pulled out a folded map, hand-drawn, showing Firefly operations across the western United States. Safe houses, supply caches, allied settlements. It was impressively detailed. And impressively trusting. Showing your network to a stranger was either a sign of confidence or desperation.

"Three things," Navarro said. "First, trade. You have medical supplies and agricultural surplus. We have intelligence, salvage from FEDRA armories, and communication networks. Fair exchange."

"Second?"

"Recruits. Not your people. We're not asking anyone to leave. But if you encounter survivors who want to join the fight, we'd like to be a referral option."

"And third?"

"Recognition. An alliance, even a loose one. When Marlene talks to other settlements about building a coalition, she wants to point at Crystal Falls and say, 'They're with us.' That carries weight."

Marcus let the silence sit for a moment. Joel shifted by the door. Tommy's chair creaked.

"Trade is reasonable," Marcus said. "We trade with FEDRA already. Fair terms, no tribute, no strings."

"We wouldn't ask for tribute."

"You're asking for our name on your banner. That's tribute of a different kind."

Navarro paused. The calm teacher's voice recalibrated. "I understand the concern. Marlene's position is that independent settlements are the future. She's not looking to absorb anyone. She's looking for partners."

"Partners in what, exactly?"

"In building something that outlasts the infection. In finding a cure. In making sure the next generation doesn't inherit a world that's already over."

Marcus looked at the pendant on the table. A small silver insect. When you're lost in the darkness, look for the light. The motto was everywhere in the games. On walls, on pendants, on the lips of people who believed it. He'd always found it moving. Hopeful. The kind of slogan that made you want to believe.

He didn't believe it anymore.

Not because the Fireflies were evil. They weren't. Navarro was sincere. Kim was a medic who'd probably saved real lives. Marlene genuinely wanted to save the world. The problem wasn't their intentions. The problem was what those intentions would lead to. A girl on a table. A surgeon with a knife. The math of sacrificing one life for many, done without asking the one life if she agreed.

Thirteen years. That was all the distance between this polite conversation in a pine-paneled room and a decision that would define everything.

"I appreciate the offer," Marcus said. "Give me a day. I want to discuss it with my council."

Navarro nodded. "Of course. We expected that."

"Helen will show you to the guest cabin. Hot water, clean beds. We'll feed you dinner. We're not savages."

Kim smiled. The first genuine expression she'd shown. "Hot water?"

"Solar-heated. Tommy's design."

"I haven't had a hot shower in eight months."

Marcus stood. "Enjoy it. We'll talk tomorrow."

Joel held the door. Navarro and Kim walked out with Helen, who was already explaining the meal schedule and the hot water rationing policy. Helen could turn any human interaction into a logistics briefing.

Joel closed the door.

"I don't like 'em," he said.

"You don't like anyone."

"I don't like people with pendants and slogans and a leader who sends scouts to settlements she's never seen." Joel moved to the table and sat in the chair Navarro had vacated. He picked up the pendant Navarro had left behind, either as a gesture of trust or a calculated move. "This is how it starts. First it's trade. Then it's joint operations. Then it's 'we need your militia for a mission.' Then you're takin' orders from someone in a city you've never visited."

Tommy leaned back in his chair. "He's not wrong. But they're also not wrong about the infection accelerating. The Shamblers I saw in Aspen, the Enhanced Runners. Something's changing."

"Somethin's always changin'," Joel said. "Doesn't mean we need to join a club to deal with it."

"We should hear them out properly," Tommy said. "A trade relationship with Firefly safe houses across the west? That's useful. Intel on FEDRA movements, settlement locations, infection patterns? That's valuable."

"And the price?" Joel set the pendant down. "There's always a price."

The door opened. Sarah came in, clipboard under her arm, pencil still behind her ear.

"I heard we have guests," she said.

"Fireflies," Marcus said. "Two of them. Lieutenant and a medic. Marlene sent them."

Sarah sat down. She looked at the pendant on the table. Picked it up. Turned it over. Read the serial number on the back.

"What do they want?"

Marcus told her. Trade, recruits, recognition. The three asks. Sarah listened the way she listened to everything, which was completely, with her eyes on whoever was talking and her mind already three steps ahead.

"The trade makes sense," she said. "Intelligence is the one thing we can't produce ourselves. We don't know what's happening east of Kansas or south of New Mexico. If the Fireflies have a network, that information is worth food and medical supplies."

"Agreed," Tommy said.

"The recruits ask is harmless if we keep it voluntary. Anyone who wants to leave and fight can leave and fight. We're not a prison."

"Also agreed," Tommy said.

"The recognition." Sarah set the pendant down. "That's the problem. If we put our name next to theirs, we inherit their enemies. Every QZ commander who hates the Fireflies, every hunter group they've crossed, every settlement that turned them down. Our name on their banner makes us a target for people who've never heard of Crystal Falls."

Joel pointed at Sarah. "What she said."

Marcus looked at the pendant. "There's something else."

The table waited. Joel, Tommy, Sarah. The people he trusted most in a world that made trust expensive.

"I don't trust Marlene," Marcus said. "I can't tell you exactly why. Call it a feeling. She believes she's building something good, and she probably is, right now. But people who believe they're saving the world are the most dangerous kind. Because when the hard choice comes, they'll sacrifice anything and call it necessary."

Joel's eyes sharpened. "You know somethin' about her?"

"I know the type. I've seen what happens when 'the greater good' becomes the only thing that matters. Individual people become expendable. Settlements become resources. Allies become tools."

The room was quiet. The turbine hummed through the walls.

"I'm not saying they're enemies," Marcus said. "I'm saying they're not friends. Not yet. Maybe not ever. We trade with them the same way we trade with FEDRA. Fair terms, clear boundaries, no allegiance."

Sarah nodded slowly. "I agree. Trade yes, alliance no."

Tommy tipped his chair forward. "Recruits?"

"If someone wants to join the Fireflies, that's their choice. We don't stop people from leaving and we don't advertise for them either."

"Recognition?"

"No. Crystal Falls is Crystal Falls. We don't fly anyone else's banner."

Joel picked up the pendant one more time. Held it up to the light. The tarnished silver caught the bulb's glow, and for a second the little insect almost looked alive.

"When you're lost in the darkness," Joel said, reading the inscription on the back that Marcus couldn't see from his angle. "Look for the light." He set it down. "I'll take the dark and a good lock on my door."

## — Marcus —

Marcus told Navarro the next morning.

They met on the dam walkway, breath fogging in the December cold, the reservoir frozen at the edges. Kim stood a few feet back, hands in her jacket pockets, watching the ice with the expression of someone who'd forgotten that water did that.

"We'll trade," Marcus said. "Medical supplies, food surplus, agricultural knowledge. In exchange for intelligence, communication access, and FEDRA salvage. Fair terms, negotiated case by case. No standing obligations on either side."

Navarro nodded. He'd expected this. "And the rest?"

"We won't send recruits. If individuals choose to seek you out on their own, that's their business. But Crystal Falls doesn't recruit for the Fireflies."

"Understood."

"And we won't fly your banner. No alliance, no recognition, no endorsement. Crystal Falls is independent. Has been since we built it. Will be as long as I'm standing here."

Navarro was quiet for a moment. He looked out over the reservoir, the frozen edges, the pines heavy with frost on the far shore. A beautiful, empty landscape that had been crawling with infected eighteen months ago. Crystal Falls had made it safe. That was the argument Navarro couldn't make and Marcus couldn't answer. You did this without us. Imagine what we could do together.

"Marlene will be disappointed," Navarro said.

"Marlene will understand. She's pragmatic."

"She is." Navarro turned back to Marcus. "Can I ask you something? Off the record."

"Sure."

"How did you build this? Three hundred and forty people, electricity, agriculture, a council. Seven years after the world ended. Every other settlement I've visited is either a prison or a campfire. This place works. How?"

Marcus thought about it. The honest answer involved a Gamer System, a Mall that sold solar panels and medical equipment for points earned by killing infected, and meta-knowledge from a video game franchise that hadn't been made yet in this world's timeline. The honest answer would get him locked in a room.

"Good people," Marcus said. "Joel, Tommy, Sarah. The council. Everyone who showed up and chose to stay and build instead of run. I can't take credit for what three hundred people decided to do with their hands."

Navarro smiled. Thin, tired, genuine. "That's a politician's answer."

"I'm not a politician."

"No. You're something I haven't figured out yet." Navarro extended his hand. "Trade partners, then. I'll send a runner with our first offer within the month. Medical intelligence, infection spread patterns, and whatever FEDRA salvage we can route through the Rockies."

Marcus shook his hand. "We'll have seeds and antibiotics ready. And a standing offer of hot showers for any Firefly who passes through."

Kim looked up at that. "I'm holding you to that."

They left an hour later. Marcus watched them walk south down the valley road until they were past the creek crossing and out of sight. Donna's watch tracked them for another mile before they disappeared into the tree line.

Joel appeared beside him. "They'll be back."

"I know."

"With more people. More asks. More slogans."

"I know."

Joel was quiet for a moment. "You said you don't trust Marlene. That it's a feelin'."

"Yeah."

"It ain't just a feelin', is it?"

Marcus looked at Joel. The man who'd walked eight hundred miles with him, fought beside him in dark hallways and abandoned buildings, built a town from a broken dam, walked his daughter down an aisle under string lights. Joel didn't ask questions he didn't already know the answer to. He just wanted to see if you'd lie.

"No," Marcus said. "It's not just a feeling."

Joel nodded. He didn't push. He never pushed. He filed things away and waited for them to matter, and when they mattered, he acted. It was the most dangerous quality Joel Miller had, and it was also the reason Marcus trusted him with everything except the full truth.

"Whatever it is," Joel said, "you'll tell me when it's time?"

"When it's time. Yes."

"That's good enough."

Joel walked back toward the compound. Marcus stayed on the walkway, hands on the railing, looking south at the empty road. The cold was sharp and clean. The reservoir ice cracked softly in the mid-morning sun. The turbine hummed beneath his feet, 168 kilowatts of steady power that lit up the valley every night and drew people to it like a beacon.

Like a light in the darkness.

Somewhere out there, in a Boston QZ that was slowly rotting from the inside, a woman named Marlene was taking care of a friend's baby. A girl named Ellie. A year old, maybe less. Immune to a fungus that had killed millions and would kill millions more. The most important person in the world, and she didn't know it yet. Wouldn't know it for twelve more years.

And when she did, Marlene would put her on a table and let a surgeon cut into her brain, because the greater good demanded it. Because one life was a small price for a cure. Because people who believed they were saving the world could justify anything.

Marcus had thirteen years to make sure that never happened.

He pushed off the railing and walked home.

## — Sarah —

The house was warm. Joel had insulated the north wall of Noah's room in October, and the woodstove in the main room kept the residence comfortable even on cold nights. The smell of pine and woodsmoke and the rosemary soap Dr. Reyes made in batches. Home smells. The kind you stopped noticing after a while, which was how you knew they were real.

Noah was on the floor in front of the woodstove, drawing. Captain was beside him, chin on the boy's knee, watching the pencil move with the intense focus of a dog who believed paper was edible and was waiting for his chance.

Sarah sat in the chair by the window with the supply ledger open in her lap, but she wasn't reading it. She was watching Noah draw. He was working on something more complex than usual. Not a dog. A building. Rectangular, with a triangular roof and small squares for windows. Smoke coming from a chimney.

A house.

Not the residence. Not any building in Crystal Falls. A different house. The kind a five-year-old remembers from before. A normal house on a normal street with a normal chimney and normal windows. The house he'd lived in before Kansas City, before the road, before the campground, before the crawl space.

Noah finished the chimney smoke. Looked at the drawing. Added a stick figure next to the house. A woman, taller than the house, with long hair drawn as a single curved line.

He put the pencil down.

"Is that your mama?" Sarah asked.

Noah nodded. He didn't look sad. He didn't look happy either. He looked like a kid who was drawing something he remembered because drawing was the only way to hold onto it.

"She had pretty hair," Sarah said.

"It was brown."

"Like yours."

Noah touched his own hair, as if checking. Then he picked up the pencil and drew another figure next to the first one. Smaller. A boy.

"That's you," Sarah said.

He nodded. Then he drew a third figure on the other side of the house. A man, taller than the woman, with no distinguishing features except a line across the chest that might have been a jacket. He drew a fourth figure next to the man. Another woman, smaller than the first, with a circle that might have been a ponytail.

"Who are they?" Sarah asked.

Noah pointed at the man. "Marcus."

He pointed at the fourth figure. "You."

Sarah put the ledger down. She looked at the drawing. A house with four people and a chimney. The dead mother on one side. The living family on the other. A five-year-old's theology, worked out in pencil on the back of a requisition form. You keep the ones you lost on the left. You put the ones you found on the right. The house stands in the middle because the house is the thing that holds it all.

"That's a good drawing," Sarah said. Her voice was steady. Her eyes were not.

Noah pulled the drawing free from the table and held it up. "For the wall?"

The kitchen wall. Where Mia's crayon dam had been pinned for five years. Where the patrol schedule and the meal rotation and the inventory charts all lived. The public record of Crystal Falls. Everything that mattered, tacked to drywall with pushpins.

"Yeah," Sarah said. "For the wall."

She took the drawing and walked to the kitchen. Found a pushpin. Put it up next to Mia's dam. Two children's drawings, five years apart, both of them saying the same thing in different handwriting.

This is my home.

Marcus came in from the cold. Stomped his boots on the mat. Hung his jacket on the hook Joel had installed by the door, the one that was exactly the right height because Joel didn't build things at the wrong height.

He saw the drawing on the wall. Looked at it. Looked at Sarah.

"Noah's?" he asked.

"Noah's."

Marcus studied it. The house, the figures, the chimney smoke drawn in careful pencil loops. His mouth moved, not quite a smile, not quite anything else.

"He drew us," Marcus said.

"He drew us."

Marcus walked to the main room. Noah was still on the floor, working on a new drawing. Captain had given up on eating the paper and was asleep. Marcus sat on the floor next to them, cross-legged, the way he'd sat on the pine planks of a campground lodge three months ago, waiting for a terrified boy to crawl out of a crawl space.

Noah held up the new drawing. Another dog. This one had two wings and a tail that was longer than its body.

"Captain?" Marcus asked.

"Captain."

"Still can't fly."

"He's practicing."

Marcus looked at the drawing. Looked at the boy. Looked at the woodstove throwing orange light across the floor, and the dog sleeping in it, and Sarah's pencil moving in the kitchen, and Joel's saw starting up in the workshop across the courtyard. The sounds of an ordinary evening in an extraordinary world.

Thirteen years. A long time. Long enough to build a city, raise a family, train an army. Long enough to figure out how to save a girl who hadn't been born yet from people who thought they were saving everyone.

Long enough, if he didn't waste it.

He leaned back against the chair and watched the fire. Noah drew flying dogs. Captain snored. The turbine hummed. The saw buzzed. Sarah's pencil scratched. The world kept ending, slowly and everywhere, and Crystal Falls kept going anyway.

A good day. Quiet. The kind worth protecting.
