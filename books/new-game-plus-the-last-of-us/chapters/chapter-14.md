# Chapter 14: Strays

## — Marcus —

Days blurred.

Not in a dramatic way. Not the kind of blur where time lost meaning and existential dread crept in and someone stared at a sunset and thought deep thoughts about the nature of human perception. Just the regular kind. The kind where you walked fifteen miles and slept in a barn and walked fifteen more and slept in a truck stop and the road looked the same and the sky looked the same and your boots wore down at the same rate and one Tuesday felt exactly like the Wednesday before it.

They'd left Buckner four days ago. The landscape had changed, gradually, the way landscapes do when you cross them on foot instead of in a car. Hill Country was flattening out. The live oaks and cedar were thinning, replaced by mesquite and dry grass that went yellow at the tips. The air smelled different. Drier. Less green.

Marcus checked the map twice a day. Their route was a lazy line trending northwest, following county roads that avoided the larger towns. They'd passed through three more dead settlements since Buckner. Same story each time. Empty streets, closed doors, newspapers from late September sitting in racks nobody would ever read. One place had a banner stretched across the main street: "BUCKHOLTS FALL FESTIVAL — OCT 12." That festival wasn't going to happen.

The group had its rhythm now. Joel on point, Tommy behind him, Sarah in the middle, Marcus at the back. They walked from dawn until the light started going orange, then found shelter, ate whatever Marcus pulled from the Mall, and slept. Joel took first watch. Tommy took second. Marcus took third. Sarah slept through the night because Joel insisted on it and nobody argued with Joel about Sarah.

820 points. The number sat in the corner of Marcus's vision like a bank balance he kept checking even though he wasn't planning to spend anything. The settlement kit cost 735. He had 85 to spare. For the first time since Outbreak Day, the math was ahead of the problem.

It felt strange. Good, but strange. Like finishing an exam early and sitting there wondering if you missed a page.

"There's something ahead," he said.

They were on a two-lane road that cut through open ranchland. A fence line ran along both sides, the wire sagging between cedar posts. A dead cow lay in the ditch, bloated and covered in flies. The smell was bad but they'd stopped noticing bad smells a week ago.

"Infected?" Joel asked, not turning around.

"No. The map shows..." He paused. The map function had improved with the 25-mile upgrade. It showed terrain, structures, infected clusters. It also showed, in faint blue outlines, other things. Moving things. Living things that weren't infected.

He'd never seen blue dots before. Red for infected, grey for structures. Blue was new.

"People," Marcus said. "Six of them. About two miles ahead, stationary. Looks like a building on the road."

Joel stopped walking. Tommy stopped behind him. Sarah bumped into Tommy's pack and didn't complain about it.

"Six people," Joel repeated.

"That's what the map shows."

"Armed?"

"I can't tell that from the map. Just positions."

Joel looked at Tommy. The look was quick but loaded. Two brothers who'd spent enough time in bad situations to communicate in glances. Tommy's expression said "your call." Joel's expression said "I know."

"We go around," Joel said.

"The road bends north in half a mile," Tommy said. "We'd lose three hours going overland to avoid them. The terrain west is open. No cover."

"Then we go slow and we look first."

"Agreed." Marcus hesitated. "For what it's worth, the map shows them all in one spot. Not spread out like a patrol or an ambush. They're clustered, inside a building. Could be survivors sheltering."

"Could be bait," Joel said.

"Could be."

Joel started walking again. Slower now. His hand moved to the SIG on his hip, not drawing it, just touching it. Making sure it was there. The way you check your wallet when you walk through a crowd.

Sarah had gone quiet. Not scared quiet. Paying attention quiet.

They covered the two miles in forty minutes.

---

The building was a gas station. One of the old rural kind, a single pump island under a flat metal canopy, a small convenience store attached to a cinder block garage bay. A sign on the roof said "HUTTO'S GAS & BAIT" in faded red letters. The fuel pump handles were still in their cradles. A bait cooler sat by the door, lid open, empty.

Joel stopped the group two hundred yards out, behind a cedar brake that gave them a view of the station without being visible from it. Tommy pulled the Remington and used the scope.

"I see a car," Tommy said. "Sedan. Blue Honda. Trunk open. Bags inside. Not military, not a truck. Family car."

"People?"

"Blinds are down on the store windows. Can't see inside. Wait." He adjusted the scope. "Door's got a chair wedged under the handle. From the inside. That's defensive, not aggressive."

"Doesn't mean anything," Joel said.

"Means they're scared enough to barricade."

Marcus checked the map again. Six blue dots, all inside the store. None moving. One of the dots was small. Smaller than the others. Either the map scaled by body mass or Marcus was reading too much into pixel size, but two of the six dots were noticeably smaller than the rest.

"I think two of them are kids," he said.

Joel looked at him.

"The map shows two contacts that are smaller. Significantly smaller. Could be children."

Joel's jaw moved. Not a clench. Just the muscles working while his brain ran calculations. Kids changed the math. Everyone knew it. Kids meant a family, and a family barricaded in a gas station two weeks into the apocalypse meant people who were lost and scared and running out of options. Not a threat. A problem, but a different kind of problem.

"Tommy stays here with Sarah," Joel said. "You and me walk up. Hands visible. If it goes bad, Tommy covers us and we pull back."

"What about Sarah?" Sarah asked.

"You stay with your uncle."

"Obviously. I meant what do I do?"

"Watch the road behind us. Make sure nobody comes up on Tommy while he's watching us."

She nodded. No argument. She'd figured out that the fastest way to be given real responsibilities was to do the small ones without complaining.

Marcus and Joel walked toward the gas station. Open ground. No cover. If someone inside had a rifle and bad intentions, they'd be dead before they heard the shot. Joel knew this. Marcus knew this. They walked anyway, because standing behind a cedar brake debating risk assessments wasn't going to feed those blue dots or get them back on the road before dark.

Joel knocked on the glass door of the convenience store. Three times. Firm. The knock of a man who'd been knocking on doors his whole life, whether to give a renovation estimate or to find out who was hiding behind one.

"Hey," Joel called. "We're passin' through. We ain't lookin' for trouble. Saw your car."

Silence. Then a scraping sound. The chair moving.

The door opened three inches. A face appeared in the gap. Male. Maybe mid-thirties. Hispanic. Dark eyes, three days of stubble, a cut on his forehead that had scabbed over brown. He was holding something behind the door. From the angle, Marcus couldn't tell if it was a weapon or a child.

"Who are you?" the man said.

"Joel. This is Marcus. We got two more up the road. We're headin' northwest."

"How many infected you seen today?"

"None in the last six miles. Had a cluster two days ago in a town called Buckner. We cleared it."

The man's eyes moved from Joel to Marcus and back. He was doing the calculation everyone did now. Three weeks into the end of the world and every stranger was a math problem. Helpful or dangerous. Worth the risk or not.

"Pete," the man said. "Pete Vasquez. I got my wife, two kids, and an older couple in here."

"We're not gonna hurt your family, Pete."

Pete looked at Joel for a long moment. Then he opened the door.

## — Joel —

The gas station was small. Maybe eight hundred square feet. Shelves that used to hold chips and jerky and motor oil, half of them picked clean. The coolers along the back wall were dark and the glass was fogged with condensation from the heat. A propane lantern sat on the checkout counter, unlit. The place smelled like sweat and canned food and the specific staleness of a room with too many people and not enough ventilation.

Pete Vasquez's wife was sitting on the floor behind the counter with two kids. The boy was maybe nine, dark-haired, wearing a San Antonio Spurs jersey that was too big for him. He had a Game Boy in his hands, turned off, and was pressing the buttons anyway. Force of habit. The girl was younger, five or six, asleep with her head in her mother's lap. The mother's hand was on the girl's hair, smoothing it in slow automatic strokes, the kind of motion that kept going even when the person doing it had stopped thinking about it.

The older couple was sitting on lawn chairs near the garage door. A man in his late sixties, thin, wearing a pearl-snap shirt and a feed cap. The woman next to him was about the same age, heavyset, with reading glasses on a chain around her neck and a look on her face that said she'd been through worse than this and was reserving judgment on the current situation.

"Earl and Dot Hutchins," Pete said, pointing. "They were at the next ranch over when it started. We've been together since Day Three."

"Where you headed?" Joel asked.

"San Angelo. Linda's sister lives there. Or lived there." Pete glanced at his wife. "We haven't been able to reach anyone since the phones went down."

"San Angelo's southwest," Joel said. "You're goin' the wrong direction."

"We tried southwest. The highway was blocked. Military vehicles, burned out, all across the road. Infected everywhere. We turned around. Tried to find another way. Got lost." Pete's voice was steady but thin, like a rope pulled tight. "We've been driving back roads for a week and a half. Ran out of gas yesterday. The pump here is dead."

"Power's out everywhere," Joel said. "Pumps won't work without it."

"I figured that out."

Joel looked around the gas station. Chips bags, empty water bottles, a stack of canned goods on the counter that Pete had probably pulled from the shelf. Pinto beans, creamed corn, tomato soup. Not much. Maybe three days' worth for six people if they stretched it.

"How long you been here?"

"Since yesterday morning. We walked from where the car died, about two miles south. The kids couldn't walk much further." Pete looked at his son. The boy was still pressing buttons on the dead Game Boy, his face set in the concentrated blankness of a kid who was coping by pretending nothing had changed. "Diego hasn't slept in three days."

"The girl?"

"Lily. She sleeps fine. She thinks it's camping."

Joel didn't smile. But something moved behind his eyes. He knew that trick. Sarah had done it too, in the first days. The mind's ability to reframe horror as adventure, available only to people young enough to not know the difference.

Earl Hutchins stood up from his lawn chair. He moved slow, the way men his age moved, careful with his knees. He extended a hand to Joel.

"Army?" Earl asked.

"No sir."

"You carry yourself like you been in it."

"Just been walkin' a while."

"We've been walking too." Earl's handshake was firm and dry. "Three weeks of it. We're not fighters, Mr. Joel. I was a math teacher for thirty-one years. Dot was a librarian. Pete here sells insurance. We are exactly the kind of people who are not equipped for this."

"Nobody's equipped for this."

"Some more than others." Earl looked at Marcus, then at Joel's SIG, then at the hunting knife on Marcus's belt. "You two look like you've been managing."

Joel didn't answer that. He turned to Marcus, who was standing by the door, doing the eye thing. Staring at nothing. Running numbers or checking maps or whatever it was he did when his eyes went blank and his face went still.

"Marcus."

"Yeah."

"What are you thinkin'?"

Marcus blinked. Came back. "I'm thinking they're out of food and they don't know where they're going."

"That's their problem."

"Is it?"

Joel held his gaze. The question sat between them like something physical, taking up space in the small gas station. Pete was watching. Linda was watching. Earl was watching. The boy with the dead Game Boy was watching, though he probably didn't understand why two strangers were having a conversation with their eyes instead of their mouths.

"We've got food," Marcus said. Simply. Not arguing. Just stating a fact.

Joel knew what came next. Marcus would open the invisible store and produce canned goods and water bottles and whatever else six hungry people needed, and it would cost him points, and he'd do it without hesitation because that was who Marcus was. The kid had a system that let him buy things from thin air and his first instinct, every single time, was to spend it on other people.

It was admirable. It was also going to be a problem.

"Fine," Joel said. "Feed 'em. But we keep movin' in the morning."

## — Sarah —

The Vasquez kids were weird. Not bad weird. Just the kind of weird that happened when regular kids got dropped into the end of the world and didn't have the context to process it.

Diego, the nine-year-old, hadn't said a word since they'd arrived. He sat on the floor of the gas station with his Game Boy and pressed buttons on a screen that had been dark for two weeks. A, B, start, select. The same pattern, over and over, like a ritual. Sarah recognized it. Not the Game Boy specifically. The behavior. The doing-a-thing-that-used-to-be-normal-because-normal-was-the-only-thing-holding-you-together thing.

She'd done it with the Jolly Ranchers. Same idea, different wrapper.

Lily was the opposite. Five years old, blond, missing a front tooth, and apparently fine. She'd introduced herself to Tommy by grabbing his hand and showing him a rock she'd found outside. "It's sparkly," she'd said, and Tommy had looked at the rock with the seriousness of a jeweler evaluating a diamond and said, "That's a good one."

Lily had nodded like this confirmed everything she'd suspected about the rock.

Now the two groups were mixed together in the gas station, eating dinner. Marcus had produced enough food for ten people without anyone asking how. Canned chili, crackers, peanut butter, bottled water, and a bag of dried apricots that nobody wanted but everyone ate because fruit was fruit. Linda Vasquez had heated the chili on the propane stove she'd brought from the car. She was the kind of person who heated canned chili properly, in a pot, stirring it, instead of eating it cold from the can. Sarah respected that.

"Your dad's intense," Diego said.

Sarah looked up. It was the first thing the boy had said in hours. He was sitting across from her, a paper plate of chili balanced on one knee, the Game Boy finally set aside.

"He's from Texas," Sarah said, as if that explained it.

"We're from Texas."

"He's from the intense part."

Diego almost smiled. It was a flicker, there and gone, like a light with a bad connection.

"How long have you guys been walking?" he asked.

"About two and a half weeks."

"Is it bad? Out there?"

She thought about how to answer. She thought about the Runners and the crossroads ambush and the child in pink pajamas that Marcus didn't talk about and the Clicker that screamed like metal tearing and her dad on the warehouse floor with a thing's hands around a pipe inches from his face.

"It depends on the day," she said.

"My dad says we just need to find Aunt Rosa and everything will be okay."

"Maybe it will."

Diego looked at his chili. Stirred it with a plastic spoon. The beans made slow circles. "He keeps saying it. Every morning. 'We'll find Rosa, everything will be okay.' Like it's the weather report."

Sarah didn't have a response for that. She knew what it felt like when adults repeated things. Her dad had done it too, in the first days. "We'll find somewhere safe." Over and over. Not because he believed it, but because saying it was all he had.

"Your dad's trying," she said.

"I know."

Lily appeared between them, holding a cracker with an improbable amount of peanut butter on it. "Diego, look. I made a mountain."

"That's too much peanut butter."

"It's a mountain."

"You're gonna choke."

"Mountains don't choke." She bit into the cracker. Peanut butter got on her nose. She didn't care.

Sarah watched her and felt something she couldn't name. Not sadness. Not envy. Something adjacent to both. A five-year-old who thought the apocalypse was camping and peanut butter was a mountain and rocks were sparkly. How long did that last? How many days until the world corrected her?

She pulled her journal from her jacket. Flipped to a blank page. Wrote: _Day 18. Met new people. A family and an old couple. The little girl thinks peanut butter is a mountain. I don't know if that's the saddest thing I've ever seen or the best._

## — Tommy —

They traveled together.

Not because Joel wanted to. Joel had made that clear with a look and a jaw set at the angle that Tommy had been reading since they were kids. The Joel angle for "I don't like this." But Joel also hadn't said no when Marcus suggested the combined group head northwest together, at least until they hit a junction where San Angelo was plausible again, and Joel not saying no was Joel's version of "fine."

Three days. That's how long they walked together. Eight people instead of four. The formation changed. Joel and Marcus still took point, but Tommy dropped back to walk with Earl, who moved slow and needed someone to keep pace with him without making it obvious. Pete walked with Linda and the kids in the middle. Sarah drifted between groups, spending time with Diego and carrying Lily on her back when the five-year-old's legs gave out, which happened every two miles like clockwork.

Tommy noticed things.

He noticed that Pete Vasquez asked Joel which road to take at every fork. Not Marcus, not Tommy. Joel. Pete had been navigating his own family for two weeks, making every decision, choosing every road, finding every shelter. And the moment someone who looked like they knew what they were doing showed up, Pete stopped deciding. Not out of laziness. Out of relief. The relief of a man who'd been pretending to be the person in charge and finally met someone who actually was.

He noticed that Linda Vasquez asked Marcus about food. Not "do you have food" but "what should we eat tonight" and "should we save the canned goods." She wasn't asking for resources. She was asking for a plan. Marcus gave her one every time, easy and casual, listing what he had and suggesting portions, and Linda followed the plan without question because someone had finally given her structure.

He noticed that Earl Hutchins fell in beside whoever was walking with the most confidence and asked smart questions. "How far today?" "What's the water situation?" "That hill up ahead, do we go over or around?" Earl wasn't leading. Earl was supporting. The man had spent thirty-one years in a classroom, reading the room, adjusting to whoever had authority. He'd found his role instantly. The role was: useful follower.

Tommy noticed all of this because he'd seen it before. Not in the apocalypse. In the Army.

A squad of strangers becomes a unit when someone takes charge. It doesn't matter if it's official. Doesn't matter if anyone voted. What matters is that one person starts making decisions and everyone else starts following them. It happens naturally. Like water finding a channel. The person who knows the most, or seems the most capable, or just talks with the most conviction, becomes the center. Everyone else orbits.

Joel was the center for security. Which road, which shelter, when to stop, when to move. His voice, his body language, his twenty years of don't-mess-with-me energy that came off him like heat from asphalt. People deferred to Joel on anything that involved danger because Joel looked like the kind of man who'd already survived the worst of it. Which he had.

Marcus was the center for everything else. Food, water, medical supplies, planning, direction. The guy who always had what you needed and never ran out. Pete and Linda didn't know where the supplies came from and they'd stopped asking after the first day. They just knew that Marcus said "I've got enough for everyone" and it was true, every time.

Tommy was the center for the gaps. The questions Joel was too terse to answer and Marcus was too focused to notice. "How's your knee, Earl?" "Diego, you need a break?" "Dot, I can carry your bag for a while." The connective tissue. The NCO, if this were a unit. The guy who made sure the privates had socks and the lieutenant knew the terrain.

On the second night, Tommy sat with Marcus on a hill overlooking the camp. They'd found a ranch house with a intact roof and a working well. Below them, Joel was showing Pete how to check a room before entering it. Open the door, wait, listen, then move in along the wall. Pete was nodding like he was taking notes for an exam.

"He doesn't know he's doing it," Tommy said.

Marcus was eating an apple. Where the apple came from was the usual mystery. "Doing what?"

"Teaching. Joel. He's teaching Pete how to clear a room. He didn't decide to do that. Pete asked a question and Joel answered it and now it's a class."

Marcus looked down the hill. Joel was demonstrating the door technique again. Pete tried it. Got it mostly right. Joel corrected his foot placement without making it feel like a correction.

"Huh," Marcus said. "Yeah. I see it."

"You do it too."

"Do what?"

"Lead. You just do it different. Joel leads by being Joel. People follow him because he's the strongest person in the room and he doesn't hesitate. You lead by solving problems. Every time someone has a question or a need, you've already got the answer. You produce food before anyone's hungry. You pick the route before anyone asks."

"I've got a map."

"It's not the map, Marcus. It's the instinct. You look at eight people and you think 'what do they need and how do I provide it.' That's leadership. The map is just the tool."

Marcus took another bite of the apple. Chewed. Thought about it.

"I'm not a leader," he said. "I'm a guy with a weird... resource advantage. If Pete had what I have, he'd be doing the same thing."

"No he wouldn't. Pete would be hoarding. Not because he's selfish. Because he's got two kids and a wife and hoarding is the rational move when you don't have enough. You've got more than enough and your first instinct is to share. That's not resources. That's character."

"Or stupidity."

"In the Army, we called it 'officer material.'"

Marcus laughed. A real one. Short, surprised, the kind that escaped before you could stop it. "Officer material. I'm a twenty-something who got dropped into the end of the world and doesn't know what he's doing."

"That describes every second lieutenant I ever served under. And some of them turned out fine."

They sat on the hill. Below, Joel had moved on from room-clearing to fire-building. Pete was arranging kindling the way Joel showed him. Linda was watching from the doorway, holding Lily. Diego was sitting near Sarah, not talking, just sitting. Being near someone his own age, roughly. Needing that. Earl and Dot were on the porch. Earl had a pencil and was marking something on Marcus's atlas, making notes in the margins. Trail distances, maybe. Water sources. The habits of a man who'd spent his life organizing information.

"You've been in this situation before," Marcus said. "In the Army. A group of strangers, no structure, someone has to step up."

"Fallujah. My first deployment. The platoon sergeant got hit on Day Four. The staff sergeant was running three squads and couldn't be everywhere. I was a corporal. Twenty-two years old. I had twelve guys looking at me for every decision from what to eat to where to sleep to when to shoot."

"And you figured it out?"

"I figured out that it doesn't matter if you know what you're doing. What matters is that someone is doing it. People can handle almost anything if they're not handling it alone." Tommy picked a piece of grass and folded it between his fingers. "Pete's been handling it alone for two weeks. That's why he looks like he hasn't slept. Not because the road is hard. Because every decision is on him and he doesn't trust his own judgment anymore."

"So what happens when we split up? When they go to San Angelo and we go to Colorado?"

Tommy didn't answer right away. He threw the grass away and picked another piece.

"That's the question, isn't it," he said.

Below them, Lily laughed at something. The sound carried up the hill, bright and clean, the laugh of a kid who still thought peanut butter was a mountain and rocks were sparkly and the world was an adventure she hadn't finished yet.

"It is," Marcus said.

"I don't have an answer. But I'll tell you what my old sergeant used to say. 'You don't find people. People find you. And once they find you, they're your problem.'"

"Encouraging."

"Wasn't meant to be."

They sat on the hill and watched the camp settle in for the night. Eight people. Four of them strangers from three days ago. All of them looking at the same three men for every decision, every meal, every answer.

Marcus finished his apple. Tossed the core into the brush. Somewhere below, Joel's voice carried, low and patient, explaining to Pete that you always sleep with your boots on during the first month.

"Tommy."

"Yeah."

"When we build the dam. When we get the settlement started. More people are going to show up, aren't they?"

"Guaranteed."

"And they're going to need the same thing. Someone to make decisions. Someone to solve problems. Someone to clear rooms and build fires and tell them it's going to be okay."

"That's how it works."

"I didn't sign up for that."

Tommy looked at him. In the fading light, Marcus looked young. He was young. Whatever impossible things he could do, whatever invisible system he carried, he was a guy in his twenties sitting on a hill in the dark watching strangers depend on him and wondering how he got here.

"Nobody does," Tommy said. "That's the whole point."

## — Marcus —

On the morning of the fourth day, they reached a crossroads.

Highway 83 ran north-south. Their county road intersected it at a T-junction with a stop sign that nobody would ever need again. South led toward San Angelo, eventually. North led toward Abilene, then the Panhandle, then New Mexico, then Colorado. Two different directions. Two different futures.

Pete stood at the junction with Linda and the kids. Earl and Dot stood behind them. Everyone had their packs. Marcus had restocked their food that morning, pulling two days' worth of canned goods and water bottles from the Mall while Pete and Linda were packing. He'd added a first aid kit and a flashlight. Thirty-two points. He was at 788.

"Straight south on 83, you should hit Brady in about forty miles," Marcus said, pointing down the highway. "Two days' walk if you keep a steady pace. From Brady, pick up Highway 87 southwest. That'll take you to San Angelo. Maybe five or six days total."

Pete nodded. He was holding Lily on his hip. The girl had her sparkly rock in one hand and a cracker in the other. Diego stood next to Linda, his Game Boy in his jacket pocket, where it had stayed since the second day. He'd stopped pressing the buttons.

"You sure you won't come with us?" Linda asked. She was looking at Joel. Everyone looked at Joel when they wanted reassurance.

"We're headed the other direction," Joel said. "Colorado."

"Colorado's a long way."

"Yes ma'am."

Earl stepped forward. He put out his hand and Joel shook it. Then he shook Marcus's hand, and then Tommy's.

"I taught algebra for thirty-one years," Earl said. "Thirty-one years, I never found a practical application for it. Now I know one. The number of calories per mile per person in a group of eight. I've been calculating it on your atlas."

"I noticed the notes," Marcus said.

"I hope you don't mind."

"I don't mind."

"Mr. Marcus." Earl held his hand a moment longer. "I've known you for three days. That's not long enough to understand a person. But it's long enough to see what they do. And what you do is take care of people you have no reason to take care of."

"We were going the same direction."

"That's geography. I'm talking about character."

Dot Hutchins didn't shake hands. She hugged Sarah, which surprised Sarah, which surprised nobody who'd watched Dot adopt Sarah as a surrogate granddaughter over the last three days, knitting conversations out of nothing during the long afternoon walks. What books Sarah liked. What music she remembered. Whether she'd ever tried pecan pie. Sarah tolerated the hug with the stiff patience of a twelve-year-old who wasn't used to grandmothers but understood the gesture.

"You take care of that hip," Dot said.

"Yes ma'am."

"And eat more. You're too thin."

"Yes ma'am."

Pete walked over to Joel. They stood a few feet apart, two men who'd met by accident and spent three days in the same orbit.

"Thank you," Pete said. "For showing me... the door thing. And the fire."

"Just basics," Joel said.

"They weren't basic to me." Pete paused. "I sell auto insurance. Sold. I've never fired a gun. I've never built a fire outside a Weber grill. My wife is smarter than me, my kids are tougher than me, and the only reason we're alive is because an old math teacher figured out how to ration canned goods." He looked at Earl. "Now you. Three days, and I've learned more about surviving than I figured out in two weeks on my own."

"You were doin' fine," Joel said, and it was the kindest lie Joel had told since Austin.

Pete knew it. He nodded anyway.

"San Angelo," Pete said. "If we get there, if Linda's sister is alive, if there's something there... we'll tell people about the direction you're heading. Colorado, right?"

"Yeah," Joel said, and then stopped. Looked at Marcus. Back at Pete. "Don't do that."

"Why?"

Joel didn't answer. Marcus understood why. Joel didn't want strangers showing up at the dam because Pete Vasquez told them about the group heading northwest. Joel wanted four people and a quiet dam and walls and safety and no complications.

Marcus understood it. He also knew it wasn't going to work that way.

"If you find other people," Marcus said, "and they need somewhere to go. Tell them to look for lights. In Colorado. Near a dam. If we get it running, there'll be lights."

Joel looked at him. The look lasted three seconds. Then Joel turned and started walking north.

Tommy followed. Sarah followed Tommy.

Marcus waved at the Vasquez family. Lily waved back with the hand holding the cracker. Diego raised one hand, brief, almost reluctant, like he wasn't sure he'd earned the right to wave goodbye to people he'd known for three days.

Then Marcus turned north and followed his group.

Four people on a road. 788 points. Six hundred and fifty miles to Colorado.

Behind him, six people walked south. Toward a city that might be empty, a sister who might be dead, a future that depended on canned goods and an old math teacher's calorie calculations and the door-clearing technique Joel had taught a man who sold auto insurance.

Marcus didn't look back. He kept walking.

But he thought about what Tommy had said. About people finding you. About them becoming your problem. About how nobody signs up for it and that's the whole point.

He checked the map. The road ahead was clear. No red dots. No blue dots. Just empty road and dry grass and a long walk north.

It wouldn't stay empty. He knew that. The blue dots would come back. More of them. Families and loners and broken people looking for somewhere to go, looking for someone who had answers, looking for lights in the dark.

And when they came, he'd have to be ready.

He wasn't ready yet. But 788 points was a start. A dam in Colorado was a start. And the four people walking in front of him, each one carrying something the others needed, Joel's strength, Tommy's steadiness, Sarah's clarity, that was more than a start. That was a foundation.

The road went north. The sun went west. Marcus followed both.
