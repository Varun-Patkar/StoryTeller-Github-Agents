# Chapter 48: What Stays

## — Marcus —

He went to the barricade at dawn.

The sun wasn't up yet. Just the grey light that came before it, turning the valley into shapes without color. The road south was empty. Briggs and his soldiers were twelve hours gone, somewhere on Highway 133, walking back to a QZ that had just learned it couldn't take what it wanted anymore.

Marcus stood at the left section of the barricade. The gap was still there. A twelve-inch space between two railroad ties where the steel plate didn't reach. Joel's report from last month was still on the council table, buried under three other priority requests. Irrigation pipe for Lena's south field. A replacement bearing for the sawmill. Glass for the schoolhouse windows.

The steel plate had been item four on the list.

He touched the railroad tie. The wood was chewed up by the M2 rounds, splintered in long pale strips that stuck out like broken fingers. A dark stain on the ground behind the gap, already turning brown in the dry mountain air. Dale's blood. Nobody had cleaned it up yet. There hadn't been time, or maybe nobody wanted to be the one who did it.

The captured Humvees sat where they'd died. Two disabled, the third driven to the compound by Tommy after the surrender. The one Marcus had hit was listing to the left, its front axle bent at an angle that said it would never drive straight again. The M2 mount was twisted sideways, the barrel warped where Marcus had grabbed it. The metal showed the impression of his fingers. Five dents in the steel, each one about three-quarters of an inch deep. Like someone had pressed their thumb into clay.

He looked at his hands. The burns from the gun barrel had healed overnight. Fresh pink skin, smooth and new. No scars.

The system had logged everything. He'd been ignoring the notification since last night, the little blue indicator pulsing in the corner of his vision like a voicemail he didn't want to check. He opened it now.

**[Battle Summary: Crystal Falls Defense]**
**[Duration: 38 minutes]**
**[Enemies Engaged: 197 (human — non-hostile classification)]**
**[Vehicles Disabled: 2]**
**[Personal Damage Sustained: 39% health reduction]**
**[Current Health: 94% (Accelerated Healing active)]**
**[Points Earned: 0]**
**[Note: Human combatants do not generate system points.]**

Zero points. Eight years and the system still wouldn't give him a single point for fighting people. Runners, Clickers, Bloaters, Shamblers, Beacons. The system rewarded all of it. But two hundred FEDRA soldiers with guns and vehicles and a colonel who'd rather start a war than accept a trade deal? Nothing.

He closed the notification. Picked up a fragment of steel plate from the ground. It was the size of his palm, sheared off by the M2 fire, edges sharp enough to cut. He set it on top of the barricade wall where the gap was.

It didn't cover anything. But he left it there.

...

Tommy found him twenty minutes later. He had coffee. Real coffee, from the last of a bag Helen had been rationing since March. The fact that she'd broken it out meant something.

"Council meeting at nine," Tommy said. "Sarah's handling logistics. Keiko's writing the after-action report."

"I'll be there."

Tommy handed him a mug. Stood beside him and looked at the road.

"Basalt's asking about the vehicles. Kate wants to know if we're keeping all three."

"We're keeping all three."

"That's what I told her."

They drank coffee. A chipmunk ran along the top of the barricade, stopped to look at them, and kept going. The sky was turning pink at the eastern ridgeline.

"You talk to Lucia yet?" Marcus asked.

Tommy shook his head. Lucia Molina. Hector's wife. Seven months pregnant.

"I'll go with you," Marcus said.

"Sarah's already been. Went at first light. She's sitting with her now."

Of course she had.

## — Joel —

They buried them at ten in the morning, on the slope behind the schoolhouse where the wildflowers grew in June.

Two graves. Two pine boxes that Frank Garvey had built before sunrise, working alone in the carpentry shop with the door closed. He'd made them out of the same wood he used for the council table and the mess hall benches. Clean-grained ponderosa pine, sanded smooth, no nails showing. The lids fit flush.

The whole settlement came. Three hundred and forty-seven people standing on a hillside in the morning sun. The children were in front, sitting cross-legged on the grass because Sarah had told the teachers that was the right thing to do. Kids should see this. Not be shielded from it.

Joel stood beside Dale's grave.

He'd thought about what to say for an hour. Then he'd thrown all of it out and decided to just talk. Dale deserved words that came from somewhere real, not from rehearsal.

"Dale Briggs joined the militia on Day 103," Joel said. "First patrol exercise, we ran the south creek route. Everybody was still figuring out which end of the rifle to hold. Halfway through, Dale stopped the whole line because he'd spotted fungal trace on a spruce tree. A little patch of white on the bark, chest height. Nobody else saw it. Most of 'em didn't know what they were lookin' at when Dale pointed it out."

Joel paused. Cleared his throat.

"He made it real for them. That's what Dale did. He paid attention to the small things because the small things are what keep you alive. He spotted the trace. He walked fifty miles with Marcus and me to scout a Beacon fortress and didn't flinch when the Sentries came. He sat at the same table in the mess hall every morning by the window and ate whatever was put in front of him."

Joel looked at the box.

"He was at the left end of the barricade yesterday. Left end's where the wall's thinnest. He knew that. He'd filed two reports about it. He stayed there anyway because somebody had to."

Joel took the cracked binoculars from his jacket pocket. One lens had a clean line through the center of the glass. He set them on top of the coffin lid.

"I'll miss him," Joel said. "That's all I know how to say."

He stepped back. Sarah squeezed his arm as he passed.

Marcus went to Hector's grave. He stood there for a moment, looking at the box, then at the crowd. Lucia Molina was in the front row, sitting in a chair that Dr. Reyes had insisted on. She had a blanket over her legs despite the warm morning. Her face was composed in the way that people's faces get when they've already done their crying somewhere private and don't have any left for public.

"I didn't know Hector well," Marcus said. "That's the truth and I think he'd prefer honesty over a speech that pretends otherwise."

The crowd shifted. People looked at each other. Marcus kept going.

"He joined the militia four months ago. He told Keiko during his intake interview that he wanted to learn to defend the place where his kid was going to be born. That was his reason. Not ideology. Not because he was brave, though he was. Because he was going to be a father, and fathers protect things."

Marcus looked at Lucia.

"I'm sorry. I'm sorry I built a wall that wasn't thick enough and started a fight that came to your door. I'm sorry your husband won't hold your baby."

He stopped. The hillside was quiet. A breeze moved through the wildflowers. Somewhere below, the dam hummed.

"Crystal Falls will take care of you and your child. That's not a promise from me. That's a promise from everyone standing here. Your kid will know who their father was and what he did."

Marcus stepped back. He didn't look at anyone.

They lowered the boxes. Frank Garvey and Carson filled the graves with shovels while Tommy played "Amazing Grace" on a harmonica because Joel's hands wouldn't stop shaking long enough for guitar. The notes drifted across the hillside and down to the river and the dam and the valley beyond, and when Tommy finished, nobody moved for a long time.

Keiko was the first to leave. She walked straight to the barricade to relieve the watch. Helen followed. Then the militia members, one by one, back to their posts. The civilians lingered. Some talked quietly. Some just stood.

Joel walked to the edge of the slope and sat down in the grass with his back to the graves. He looked at the valley. The river. The compound. The lights that had been on every night for eight years because Tommy had fixed a turbine and Marcus had bought the wire.

Sarah sat beside him.

"You okay?" she asked.

"No."

"Good."

They sat together. Joel didn't say anything else and Sarah didn't ask him to.

## — Marcus —

The council meeting at two PM was all logistics.

Keiko's after-action report: thirty-eight minutes of engagement, twelve militia positions held, two vehicles disabled, one captured. Coalition response time: twenty-nine minutes from Basalt, effective, decisive. Ammunition expenditure: 340 rounds of rifle ammunition, acceptable given the outcome. Barricade damage: significant but repairable. The left section needed a complete rebuild. Joel's request for the steel plate was moved to the top of every list.

Sarah's civilian report: non-combatant evacuation worked. Mine tunnel shelter held all 280 non-combatants. Martha Cope's headcount matched perfectly. Dr. Reyes treated three wounded militia, all stable. Lucia Molina was given priority housing in the main lodge, close to the clinic. Food reserves were stable. The captured FEDRA supplies included sixty MREs, medical kits, and ammunition.

Helen's coalition update: Basalt wanted a formal after-action debrief. Glenwood Springs sent a radio message from Diane Pryor saying she was "watching with interest." Willow Creek's Harlan Cope was furious about FEDRA's attack and wanted to accelerate the mutual defense pact timeline.

Tommy's infrastructure report: power stable, water stable, south perimeter tripwire system needed repair from vehicle damage. The captured Humvees could be made operational within two weeks if Marcus could source replacement parts from the Mall.

Standard council business. The machine running. Sarah took notes in the leather journal she'd carried since Day 54. Joel leaned against the wall with his arms crossed, listening to everything, saying nothing unless it was about security. Tommy reported his numbers. Keiko gave recommendations. Helen tracked the data.

Marcus sat at the head of the table Frank had built and gave approvals. Steel plate for the barricade. Parts for the Humvees. Additional medical supplies for Dr. Reyes. Food supplements for the Denver refugees who were still below weight.

He spent 800 points. Didn't think about it.

The meeting ended at 3:15. Everyone filed out except Sarah, who stayed to update the labor schedule. Marcus told her he was going for a walk. She looked at him the way she looked at him when she knew what the walk was actually for but didn't say it.

"Dinner's at six," she said. "Noah wants to show you a drawing."

"I'll be there."

## — Marcus —

He walked to the mine tunnel. Not the one they'd used for the evacuation shelter. The old one, seven miles northwest, where he'd killed his first Bloater three years ago. The entrance was still half-collapsed from the cave-in. The system had marked it as cleared.

Marcus sat on a flat rock outside the entrance and opened the system interface.

He scrolled past the battle summary. Past the point totals. Past the settlement management screen and the coalition map and the quest log. He went to his personal stats page.

**[Marcus Webb — Status]**
**[Level: N/A (Gamer System does not use traditional levels)]**
**[Age: 22 (biological) / Adult (mental)]**
**[Health: 97%]**
**[Points: 6,592]**

**[Enhancements Active:]**
**[Enhanced Senses — Tier 4 (Sight, hearing, smell, touch at 8x human baseline)]**
**[Accelerated Healing — Tier 4 (Regeneration rate: ~3% health per minute)]**
**[Enhanced Durability — Tier 4 (Bullet-resistant dermal layer, impact absorption)]**
**[Enhanced Speed — Tier 4 (Sprint: ~45 mph, reaction time: ~3x human)]**
**[Enhanced Strength — Tier 4 (Estimated lift capacity: ~2,200 lbs)]**

**[Tier 5 Progress: 76%]**
**[Requirements: 8/8 enemy classes ✓ | 500+ population (349/500) | [REDACTED]]**

He stared at the numbers.

Two thousand two hundred pounds. He could deadlift a compact car. He could outrun a horse at full gallop. He could take a fifty-caliber round to the chest and walk away with a bruise.

Yesterday, he had torn the barrel off a mounted machine gun with his bare hands. He'd ripped the wiring harness out of a military vehicle's engine block like pulling weeds from a garden. He'd charged through grenade shrapnel and gotten up. He'd covered sixty yards in under four seconds to reach a vehicle that was killing his people.

The FEDRA soldiers had seen all of it.

Not the ones on the flanks. Not the ones behind the transports. But the gunners. The drivers. The men and women close enough to see a human being do things that human beings could not do. They'd watched Marcus absorb bullets and keep moving. They'd watched him bend steel. They'd watched him rip apart their vehicles with the casual efficiency of a man opening a jar.

Some of them had stopped fighting after that. Not because they were outgunned. Because they were afraid. Because what they'd seen wasn't war. It was something else.

Marcus looked at his hands again. The same hands that held Noah at bedtime. That played Joel's guitar by the fireplace. That held Sarah's when they sat on the dam walkway.

The same hands that had five finger-shaped dents in an M2 gun barrel.

He pulled up the combat log.

**[Battle of Crystal Falls — Personal Actions:]**
**[Vehicles disabled: 2]**
**[Weapon systems destroyed: 2 (M2 mount, Mark 19 disabled)]**
**[Enemy combatants directly engaged: ~14]**
**[Enemy combatants incapacitated: 9]**
**[Injuries sustained: 7 (2 ballistic impacts, 1 grenade blast, 4 fragmentation wounds)]**
**[Time in active combat: 11 minutes]**
**[Recovery time to 90% health: 6 hours, 12 minutes]**

Fourteen people. He'd fought fourteen people in eleven minutes and beaten all of them. Torn apart their vehicles. Taken their weapons. Absorbed their ammunition.

A Bloater was a monster. A horde of Runners was a natural disaster. Those things made sense in the system's framework. The system gave points for infected kills because infected were the enemy. Clear, simple, clean.

But yesterday he'd used the same abilities against people. People with names and families and reasons for being there, even if those reasons were wrong. He'd picked a gunner out of a turret and set him on the road like a misbehaving child. He'd crippled two military vehicles that represented years of FEDRA's maintenance and resources.

The system gave zero points for any of it. Because the system knew the difference, even if Marcus wasn't sure he did anymore.

"What am I?" he said out loud.

The mine tunnel didn't answer. The mountain didn't answer. A jay landed on a branch overhead and looked at him sideways and flew away.

He sat there until the shadows got long.

## — Noah —

"Dad."

Marcus was sitting on the front step of their cabin, elbows on his knees, looking at the compound. The mess hall lights were on. People were filing in for dinner.

Noah stood in the doorway holding a piece of paper.

"Hey, buddy."

"I drew something."

Noah held out the paper. It was a crayon drawing, the kind six-year-olds make where the proportions are wrong and the colors are chosen by feeling rather than accuracy. A brown rectangle (the barricade). Green triangles (trees). Blue at the bottom (the river). And in front of the brown rectangle, a figure. Tall. With lines on its arms that might have been cuts or might have been muscles. Standing between the rectangle and a row of grey squares (the Humvees).

The figure was alone.

"That's you," Noah said.

Marcus looked at the drawing. At the figure standing between the wall and the vehicles with nothing around it. Alone in the open. Small details: the figure's arms were spread wide, like it was trying to block the whole road by itself.

"You were really brave," Noah said.

Marcus took the drawing. "Thank you, Noah."

"Mom said two people died."

"Yeah."

"Were they your friends?"

Marcus thought about Dale sitting by the mess hall window every morning. About Hector telling Keiko he wanted to learn to defend the place where his kid would be born.

"Yeah. They were."

Noah sat down beside him on the step. He was wearing the too-big flannel shirt Sarah had hemmed for him and boots that Donna had found in a scavenged donation box. He smelled like crayons and the pine soap Lena made for the compound.

"I'm sorry your friends died," Noah said.

"Me too."

They sat together for a minute. Then Noah leaned against Marcus's arm and said, "Can we have dinner now?"

"Yeah. Let's go."

## — Marcus —

Sarah found him after dinner.

He was on the dam walkway again. Their spot. The concrete railing where they'd sat a hundred times, looking at the river, talking about nothing or everything. The walkway was cold in the evenings. The spray from the overflow channel put a fine mist in the air that settled on everything.

She sat down beside him. Close. Shoulder to shoulder. She didn't say anything at first. Just sat with him and watched the water.

"Tommy says you went to the mine," she said.

"Went for a walk."

"A seven-mile walk to the place where you killed your first Bloater. Some walk."

He didn't answer.

Sarah waited. She was better at waiting than he was. Eight years of sitting in council meetings and mediating between Carson and Gene and handling supply disputes had taught her that the person who talks first usually says the wrong thing. She let silence do the work.

"I looked at the combat log," Marcus said.

"Okay."

"Fourteen soldiers. I fought fourteen of them in eleven minutes. Disabled two armored vehicles. Took seven hits including a grenade blast. And I was fine. My health was back to ninety-four percent by this morning."

"I know."

"I ripped a gun barrel off a military vehicle, Sarah. With my hands. I pulled a man out of a turret and set him on the ground. He weighed two hundred pounds in his gear and I moved him like he was a bag of groceries."

"I know."

"The system doesn't give points for human kills. It never has. Not once. From the very first day, it's been clear: infected are enemies, humans are not. But yesterday I used every enhancement the system ever gave me against people. The same speed I use to kill Runners. The same durability. The same strength."

He looked at his hands. Turned them over. The palms were smooth. No calluses anymore. The enhanced healing cleared those too.

"The system turned me into a weapon," he said. "And yesterday I pointed that weapon at human beings."

Sarah was quiet for a while. The river ran below them. An owl called from somewhere up the valley, low and patient.

"What did you do to the gunner?" she asked. "The one in the turret."

"I pulled him out and set him on the road."

"Did you kill him?"

"No."

"What about the drivers?"

"They ran. I let them go."

"And the soldiers on the road. The fourteen you fought."

"Nine incapacitated. Five ran. I didn't chase them."

"How many people did you kill yesterday, Marcus?"

He stopped. Thought about it. Actually counted.

"None," he said. "I didn't kill anyone."

"Joel's flankers killed FEDRA soldiers. Keiko's line killed soldiers. Kate Schultz's people from Basalt killed soldiers. You tore apart two vehicles and fought fourteen men and you didn't kill a single one of them."

Marcus blinked. He'd been so focused on the damage he'd done, the metal he'd bent, the power he'd used, that he'd skipped the one fact that should have been obvious.

"A weapon doesn't do that," Sarah said. "A weapon kills. That's what weapons are for. You had the strength to tear those vehicles apart. You could have torn those soldiers apart just as easily. And you didn't."

"I could have."

"I know you could have. That's the point. You could have, and you chose not to."

She took his hand. His fingers, the ones that had left dents in an M2 barrel, curled around hers.

"You're not a weapon, Marcus. Weapons don't cry at funerals."

Something in his chest unlocked. Not a dramatic release. Not a flood of emotion. Just the feeling of a knot loosening. Like he'd been holding his breath since yesterday morning and hadn't realized it.

"Dale is still dead."

"Dale is still dead. And you're going to carry that. You should carry it. That's what leaders do. But you're going to carry it as a person, not as a weapon. Because weapons don't care about steel plates on barricade walls. Weapons don't build clinics and buy blood pressure medication for old men and carry orphans out of crawl spaces."

She leaned against him. He put his arm around her. The mist from the overflow channel settled on their hair and their shoulders.

"The system gave you power," Sarah said. "What you do with it is yours. It's always been yours."

They sat on the dam. Below them, the turbine hummed 168 kilowatts of power into a town of three hundred and forty-seven people. The lights were on in the mess hall and the clinic and the schoolhouse. Somewhere, Keiko was walking the barricade with a flashlight, checking the gaps Joel had marked for repair. Somewhere, Lucia Molina was lying in the main lodge with her hand on her belly, listening to the sounds of a settlement that was still standing because her husband had stood on a wall.

"I need to fix that gap tomorrow," Marcus said.

"First thing. I already put it on the schedule."

"Of course you did."

She smiled. The first smile he'd seen from her since before the bell rang yesterday morning.

## — Joel —

He found them on the dam walkway at ten. Sat down on the railing across from them, legs hanging over the edge on the opposite side. Three people on a dam in the dark.

Joel had a tin cup. Not bourbon this time. Tea. Chamomile, from Lena's herb garden. He'd never been a tea man, but Lena had pressed a cup into his hands after dinner and he didn't have the energy to argue with a woman who grew all the food.

"Basalt's sending a delegation day after tomorrow," Joel said. "Kate wants a joint patrol schedule for the south corridor."

"Good," Marcus said. "We'll need it."

"Helen's drafting a shared communication protocol. Three-frequency rotation, daily check-ins, emergency codes."

"Good."

Joel sipped his tea. Looked at the river. The same river he'd looked at a thousand times from a hundred different angles.

"I talked to Keiko. She wants to expand the militia. Add twenty. Screen from the Denver group and the Willow Creek transfers. She thinks another squad-sized element on the eastern ridge would've ended that fight in fifteen minutes instead of thirty-eight."

"She's right."

"She usually is."

Quiet. The river. The lights. A dog barking somewhere near the main lodge. Captain, the old mutt who'd been with the settlement since Year 2, announcing something that only mattered to him.

"You did good yesterday," Joel said.

"Two people died, Joel."

"And three hundred and forty-seven didn't. I know you don't like that math. I don't either. But it's the math."

Joel set his cup on the railing. Folded his hands.

"I used to think about this. Back when it was just the four of us on the road, and you'd come back from a clearing run with blood on your shirt and a thousand yards in your eyes. I'd think, what happens when this kid stops bein' a kid and starts bein' something else?"

"And?"

"And then I watched you carry a five-year-old boy out of a crawl space and feed him applesauce with a plastic spoon. And I stopped worryin' about it."

Joel picked up his cup. Stood.

"Get some sleep. Both of you. Tomorrow we fix the wall."

He walked back toward the compound. His boots crunched on the gravel path. At the bend, he stopped and looked back.

"Marcus."

"Yeah."

"That steel plate request. The one that was item four on the list."

"Yeah."

"Make it item one."

"It already is."

Joel nodded. Kept walking. The dark swallowed him up, and then it was just Marcus and Sarah on the dam with the river running below and the lights of Crystal Falls behind them and the quiet that came after the hardest kind of day.

Sarah stood up. Held out her hand.

"Come on. Noah's asleep. I want to check on Lucia before bed."

Marcus took her hand. They walked back together.

The lights were still on.
