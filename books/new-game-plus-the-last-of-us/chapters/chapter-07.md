# Chapter 7: Hunting Party

## — Marcus —

The map woke him up before the bird did.

Not literally. The system didn't have an alarm clock, which was a design flaw Marcus planned to bring up with nobody, since the system never answered questions and didn't appear to have a complaints department. But the notification was waiting for him when he opened his eyes, glowing faintly in his peripheral vision like a text message he'd forgotten to check.

**[AREA ALERT — UPDATE]**
**[3 Runners: Now 7.4 miles SE. Bearing 142°. Moving NW at ~0.3 mph.]**
**[Estimated arrival at farmhouse: 22-26 hours.]**

He sat up on the bedroom floor and rubbed his face. The Louis L'Amour books watched him from the shelf with their cracked spines and faded cowboys. Outside, the sky was pink and grey, early enough that even Joel might still be asleep.

Might.

Marcus pulled on his shoes and opened the map in full. Three red dots, clustered tight, inching northwest through empty country. They'd covered about five miles since the alert last night, which meant they weren't sprinting. They were drifting. Runners did that. When they didn't have a target, they wandered in loose patterns, following sound or movement or whatever broken signal the fungus was feeding into what was left of their brains. But the drift had a direction, and the direction was here.

Seven miles. He could cover that in two hours on foot if he pushed it. Find them, kill them, be back before lunch. Three Runners at maybe eight to twelve points each, plus the streak bonus if he got all three in one outing. That was forty, fifty points minimum. Maybe more if the achievement kicked in.

More importantly, three Runners wouldn't reach the farmhouse. Sarah wouldn't have to hear them scratching at the plywood Joel had nailed over the windows. Joel wouldn't have to decide between fighting and running with a daughter who couldn't walk without help.

Marcus checked his status.

**[Points: 488]**
**[Health: 88%]**

The health was climbing. Rest and calories did that. He opened the Mall and bought thirty rounds of 9mm ammunition. Fourteen points with the Efficient Provider discount. Practical. He already had the SIG's twin, the one he'd kept for himself. Joel had the other one.

**[Purchase: 9mm Ammunition x30 — 14 Points]**
**[Remaining Points: 474]**

He loaded the magazine, chambered a round, and tucked the gun into his waistband. The hunting knife went on his belt. He filled a water bottle from the jug in the kitchen and ate a handful of saltines standing at the counter, chewing quietly in the grey light.

Tommy was on watch in the front room. Marcus could see the top of his head over the armchair, the handgun resting on the armrest. He was awake but still. Conserving energy the way soldiers learned to.

"Hey," Marcus said quietly, stepping into the doorway. "I need to talk to you and Joel."

Tommy turned his head. "About what?"

"Something coming. Not urgent yet. But I want to deal with it before it is."

Tommy studied him for a second. Whatever he saw in Marcus's face was enough. He nodded and got up.

...

Joel was in the master bedroom, sitting on the edge of the bed with his boots already on. Because of course he was. The man slept with his boots on, or close enough that the distinction didn't matter. He was looking out the window at the gravel drive, the same view he watched from every window in every room, like the drive was going to produce a threat if he blinked.

"We got a problem?" Joel asked before Tommy and Marcus were fully through the door.

"Not yet," Marcus said. "Three infected headed this direction. They're about seven miles southeast. Moving slow, but they'll be here by tomorrow night if nobody stops them."

Joel's eyes sharpened. "How do you know that?"

There it was. The question Marcus couldn't answer honestly and couldn't dodge forever. He'd rehearsed a version of this conversation in his head during the sleepless stretches of the last two nights.

"I've been keeping track of the area," he said. Technically true. "Watching for movement. I spotted signs yesterday." Less true. "Three of them, clustered together, working their way through the fields."

"You spotted signs from the porch?" Joel's voice was flat.

"I took a walk yesterday evening. Scouted the south perimeter." A lie. He'd been sitting on the porch steps staring at a glowing blue map that nobody else could see. But it was a lie that fit the shape of truth, and that was the best he could manage.

Tommy leaned against the doorframe. "Three Runners. We can handle three."

"We can," Marcus said. "But I'd rather handle them out there than in here. Sarah can't run. If they show up at the house, our only option is to fight with her ten feet away. I want to go find them and take them out before they get close."

Joel didn't respond immediately. He looked at Tommy. Tommy looked back. One of those brother conversations that happened without words, the kind built over decades of knowing someone well enough that a raised eyebrow carried a paragraph of meaning.

"I'll go with you," Joel said.

"You don't have to. I can handle three Runners."

"I said I'll go."

It wasn't a question or an offer. Joel stood up and picked the SIG off the nightstand and checked the magazine with the automatic efficiency of a man who'd been handling weapons since before Marcus was born. Which, depending on how you counted timelines, might actually be accurate.

"Tommy stays here with Sarah," Joel said. "We go out, find 'em, put 'em down, come back. Simple."

Tommy nodded. "Take the road south for about a mile, then cut east through the fields. If they're coming from the southeast, you'll hit 'em somewhere around that tree line past the neighbor's place."

"How long?" Joel asked, looking at Marcus.

"Two hours out, maybe less. Depends on how fast we move. Two hours back. Call it half a day."

Joel tucked the gun into his belt. "Then let's go before it gets hot."

Marcus had wanted to go alone. He'd planned to go alone. The system map gave him an advantage that was hard to use with someone watching over his shoulder. But Joel had a look on his face that Marcus recognized from the games, from the cutscenes, from every encounter where Joel had decided something and the decision was final. You didn't argue with that look. You just adjusted.

"Alright," Marcus said. "Let me grab my pack."

---

## — Joel —

They left at first light.

Tommy walked them to the cattle gate at the end of the gravel drive. Sarah was still sleeping on the couch, her breathing steady through the window screen. Joel had checked on her twice before leaving. The bandage was clean. The fever was gone. She was healing. She was fine.

He kept telling himself she was fine.

"Be careful," Tommy said. Low, just between the two of them while Marcus adjusted his backpack straps a few yards ahead. "And watch him."

"I know."

"I mean really watch him, Joel. How he moves. What he looks at. When he looks at nothin'."

Joel clapped Tommy's shoulder once and walked through the gate.

Marcus set the pace. Faster than Joel expected. The kid moved well for someone who claimed to be a desk jockey. Not military well, not the way Tommy moved, but with a loose efficiency that didn't waste energy. He kept to the road's gravel shoulder for the first half mile, then cut left across a cattle fence and into the open pasture without hesitation, like he knew exactly where he was going.

Which was the problem. He did know exactly where he was going. And Joel had no idea how.

The morning air was cool, the first hint that September was thinking about becoming October. The grass was knee-high and brown, crackling underfoot. Grasshoppers popped up in small explosions as they walked, bouncing off their shins and vanishing into the weeds. A hawk circled overhead in a wide, lazy orbit, riding the thermals, watching the fields for rabbits and mice. The world smelled like dust and dry cedar and something faintly chemical that Joel didn't want to think about, blowing up from the south.

They walked in silence for twenty minutes. Joel let the silence sit. He'd learned a long time ago that silence was a tool. People filled it. They said things they hadn't planned to say because the quiet made them uncomfortable. Marcus didn't seem uncomfortable, though. He walked and scanned the terrain and checked his bearings against something Joel couldn't see, and kept his mouth shut.

Fine. Joel could play that game longer than anyone alive.

At the forty-minute mark, Marcus slowed down. They'd crossed two fences and a dry creek bed and were approaching a cluster of buildings that sat in a shallow depression between two low hills. A house, a barn, a metal equipment shed with a rust-streaked roof. All abandoned. The house's front door was open and hanging crooked on one hinge.

"They're close," Marcus said.

Joel stopped. He listened. Wind through the grass. The distant ticking of a metal roof expanding in the early sun. Nothing else.

"How close?"

"Half a mile. Maybe less. In those buildings or near them."

Joel looked at the buildings. Looked at Marcus. "I don't hear anything."

"They're not always loud. Not when they're idle."

That was true. Joel had seen it in the first hours of the outbreak. Runners standing still in dark rooms, twitching, waiting for a sound or a vibration to set them off. Dormant wasn't the right word. Idling, like an engine left running in a parking lot.

"We go slow," Joel said. "Clear the barn first. Then the house. Shed last. We don't split up."

Marcus nodded. He pulled the hunting knife. Not the gun. Joel noted that.

"Why the knife?"

"Gunshots carry. If there's more infected in the area, I'd rather not ring the dinner bell."

Smart. Joel pulled his own knife, the one Marcus had given him on Outbreak Night, the one with the heavy blade and the textured grip that fit his hand like it was made for it. He left the SIG holstered. For now.

They moved toward the barn.

---

## — Marcus —

The map showed all three inside the house. Ground floor. Clustered in what was probably the living room, based on the building's layout. The red dots weren't moving, which meant they were in that idle state, standing in the dark, fungal neurons firing just enough to keep them upright and waiting.

Marcus couldn't share any of that with Joel. So he improvised.

"Barn first," he agreed, because the barn was empty and clearing it would look like proper procedure, not because he actually expected anything in there.

The barn was empty. Hay bales, an old John Deere with flat tires, a workbench with rusted tools. A pigeon exploded out of the rafters when they pushed the door open and Joel's hand went to his holster and came back empty in a single smooth motion. Fast. Joel was fast.

"Clear," Joel said.

"Clear," Marcus confirmed.

They crossed the yard to the house. The front door swayed in the breeze, its bottom hinge snapped, the wood around the latch splintered like something had hit it. Or pushed through it.

Marcus stopped at the porch steps. He could feel them now. Not through the system, through his body. A tightness in his chest, a prickle at the back of his neck. The system called it a proximity alert, a faint orange pulse at the edge of his vision that said "hostiles within 30 meters." He didn't know if it was the system enhancing his natural instincts or replacing them with something artificial, and right now he didn't care.

"Inside," he said quietly. "I can hear them."

Joel tilted his head. Listened. After a moment, he nodded once. He'd heard something too, or he was trusting Marcus. Joel would never admit which.

They went in through the front door, single file. Joel first, because Joel had positioned himself in front and Marcus didn't have time to argue about it. The entry hallway was narrow and dark, wallpaper peeling, family photos on the wall in frames that were starting to tilt. A coat rack with a denim jacket still hanging from it. Boots by the door. Someone's home. Someone's everything.

The smell hit at the hallway's midpoint. Wet rot and something sour underneath it, biological and wrong. Joel's nostrils flared. He knew the smell. Marcus knew it too, from descriptions in wikis and forums and a hundred Reddit posts about the game's audio design, but knowing it intellectually and smelling it in a dead stranger's hallway were different things. His stomach turned.

The living room was at the end of the hall. Through a wide archway with no door. Marcus could see the edge of a sofa, a coffee table with a mug on it, a window with closed curtains that let in thin bars of grey light.

And the shapes.

Three of them. Two standing near the far wall, swaying slightly, heads down, arms loose at their sides. The third was on the floor, sitting against the base of the sofa with its legs splayed out, twitching in irregular jerks that traveled up through its torso and into its neck. All three were dressed in civilian clothes. Jeans, t-shirts, sneakers. One was wearing a Texas Longhorns baseball cap. They'd been people four days ago. Students, maybe. Or workers from one of the ranches. Young. Early twenties at the oldest.

The fungal growth was early-stage. Veins of grey-white mycelia spreading up their necks and along their jawlines, threading into the skin like roots into soil. Their eyes were bloodshot, pupils blown wide, not blind yet but not seeing normally either. Runners. Stage one. Aggressive, fast, and still coordinated enough to be dangerous.

The system tagged them automatically.

**[Runner — Level 1]**
**[Runner — Level 1]**
**[Runner — Level 2]**

Level 2 was new. Marcus didn't know what the difference was, and this wasn't the time to read tooltips.

Joel had seen them. He held up three fingers without turning around. Marcus nodded, though Joel couldn't see it.

Joel pointed to the two standing by the wall. Then to himself. Then pointed to the one on the floor. Then to Marcus.

Marcus understood. Two for Joel. One for Marcus. He wanted to argue the split. Joel was giving himself the harder job with only a knife and no system enhancements. But Joel was already moving, placing his boots with a carpenter's precision, weight shifting from heel to ball in a silent roll that didn't disturb a single floorboard.

Marcus sidestepped left, circling toward the seated Runner. The floor creaked.

Every head snapped up.

The Runner by the sofa launched off the ground with a scream that didn't sound human and didn't sound animal. It sounded like a throat being used for something throats weren't designed for. Vocal cords shredded by fungal tissue, producing a wet, tearing shriek that hit Marcus in the chest like a physical force.

He sidestepped. Not gracefully. He threw himself left and the Runner's outstretched hands raked the air where his neck had been. Its momentum carried it past him, stumbling into the hallway arch. Marcus spun and drove the hunting knife into the back of its neck, below the skull, the way he'd done it on Outbreak Night. The blade went in hard. The Runner dropped.

**[Runner Killed — 10 Points]**

He pulled the knife free and turned.

Joel had the first Runner on the ground. His left hand was on its face, pressing its head into the carpet, and his right hand was driving the knife into its temple with a single controlled thrust. The Runner spasmed and went still. Joel was already standing, already turning toward the third.

The third Runner was faster than the others. The Level 2. It had come off the wall like a sprinter out of blocks, not stumbling, not flailing, running with purpose and coordination that the other two hadn't shown. It hit Joel in the chest before he could set his feet and they both went down, the Runner on top, fingers clawing at Joel's face, teeth snapping inches from his jaw.

Marcus didn't think. He crossed the room in three steps and grabbed the Runner by the back of its Longhorns cap, wrenching its head backward. It twisted in his grip with a strength that surprised him, fungal-fueled muscles operating past any limit a normal body would allow. Its hand caught his forearm and the fingers dug in, nails tearing through his sleeve and into skin.

Joel drove his knife up into the Runner's chest from below. The blade punched through the sternum. The Runner shuddered. Marcus pulled its head further back, exposing the throat, and Joel withdrew the knife and put it through the neck. Arterial blood, dark and wrong-colored, sprayed across the carpet in a wide arc.

The Runner went limp.

Marcus dropped it and stepped back, breathing hard. His forearm was bleeding. Four parallel scratches, not deep but angry and red, already starting to sting.

Joel was on his feet. Blood on his hands, his shirt, a smear across his cheek. He was breathing hard too. His eyes were wide and bright and very, very alert, the way a person's eyes look when the adrenaline is still running and the brain hasn't caught up to the fact that the danger is over.

"You good?" Marcus asked.

Joel looked at the three bodies on the floor. Looked at the blood on his hands. Looked at Marcus.

"Yeah," he said. "I'm good."

**[Runner Killed — 12 Points]**
**[Runner Killed (Assist) — 6 Points]**
**[ACHIEVEMENT: Streak 3 — Kill 3 infected in a single outing. Reward: 25 Points]**
**[Total Earned This Outing: 53 Points]**
**[Current Points: 527]**

The notifications stacked in Marcus's peripheral vision like receipts from a cash register. Fifty-three points. Not bad for ten minutes of terror. The streak bonus was the real prize, twenty-five points for doing something he needed to do anyway.

He dismissed the notifications and checked his forearm. The scratches were shallow. Nails, not teeth. Nails didn't carry the infection. He was pretty sure about that. Ninety percent sure. Eighty-five.

"Let me see that," Joel said.

Marcus held out his arm. Joel took it, turned it, examined the scratches with the matter-of-fact attention of a man who'd dealt with injuries on job sites for thirty years. Checked the edges. Checked for discoloration. Checked Marcus's eyes, which was a strange thing to do until Marcus realized Joel was looking for pupil dilation. Early signs of infection.

"Nails," Joel said. "Not a bite."

"I know."

"Clean it anyway. You got alcohol in that backpack?"

Marcus opened the Mall and bought a small bottle of isopropyl alcohol. Two points. He turned slightly away from Joel, palmed the bottle as it materialized in his hand, and held it up like he'd pulled it from a pocket. "Here."

Joel took it. Looked at it. Looked at Marcus's backpack, which was still zipped and on his back. Looked at the bottle again.

He didn't say anything. He poured alcohol over Marcus's forearm and Marcus hissed through his teeth and watched Joel watch him with an expression that said "I saw that, and we're going to talk about it, but not right now."

Fair enough.

---

## — Joel —

They checked the rest of the house. Bedrooms clear. Bathroom clear. Kitchen clear except for a pot on the stove that had something growing in it that Joel chose not to examine. The back door was locked from inside, which meant the Runners had come in through the front.

The equipment shed was empty. Just a riding mower and gas cans and a shelf of motor oil. No infected. No bodies.

Joel washed his hands at an outdoor spigot that still had water pressure. The blood came off in brown swirls that soaked into the dry dirt. He scrubbed his fingers, his nails, the creases of his knuckles. He'd killed men before. He'd killed two Runners before, on Outbreak Night, in the dark, when the world was still in the process of ending and killing things that were trying to eat his daughter felt like the most natural thing in the world.

This was different. This was planned. This was walking into a room and putting a knife into something's brain because it existed in the wrong place. He filed that away. Adjusted. The old Joel, the Joel from last week, would have been sick. The new Joel, the one who was already forming in the wreckage of the old one, just washed his hands and moved on.

Marcus was sitting on the porch steps of the dead family's house, wrapping his forearm with gauze from the backpack. The backpack that produced things.

Joel sat down next to him. Not close. A body's width apart.

"You knew they were here," Joel said.

"I told you. I scouted south yesterday."

"You told me they were seven miles out. We walked four. Maybe four and a half." Joel had been counting strides. Old habit from construction sites, where measuring distances by foot was a skill you used every day. "That means they moved two and a half miles overnight. Toward us. And you knew exactly where to find 'em."

Marcus tied off the gauze. "I estimated."

"You didn't estimate. You walked straight here. Didn't check the compass, didn't look at landmarks, didn't hesitate at a single fence crossing. You walked four miles across open country like you had GPS."

Marcus was quiet for a while. He looked at his wrapped forearm. At the dead house behind them. At the sky, which was turning blue as the morning warmed up.

"You're right," Marcus said. "I didn't estimate."

Joel waited.

"I can't explain it to you, Joel. I know that's not what you want to hear. But I can do things that don't make sense, and the more I try to explain them, the crazier it sounds. So I'm asking you to look at what just happened. Three infected that were going to reach the farmhouse. We found them. We killed them. Nobody got hurt except some scratches." He held up his forearm. "Sarah's safe."

"That's what you always say. Sarah's safe. Sarah's alive. Like that's supposed to answer everythin'."

"It answers the only thing that matters."

Joel stared at him. Marcus stared back. The kid had guts, Joel would give him that. Most people couldn't hold Joel's stare. Grown men on construction crews looked away. Foremen backed down. Marcus just sat there, bloody gauze on his arm, knife on his belt, and met Joel's eyes with an expression that wasn't defiant or challenging but simply honest. A person who was telling all the truth he could and knowing it wasn't enough.

Joel looked south.

From the slight rise where the house sat, you could see for miles. The fields ran out in rolling waves of brown and gold, interrupted by fence lines and the occasional dark smudge of a tree cluster. Beyond that, on the horizon, the sky was wrong. Not hazy like the last two days. Darker. A band of brown-grey smoke was climbing into the atmosphere, spreading out at altitude into a flat ceiling that caught the morning light in ugly yellows and oranges.

"That's new," Joel said.

Marcus stood up and looked south. His face changed. Not panic. Something quieter. Recognition.

"They're still bombing," Marcus said. "That's a second wave. They're trying to contain the infection by leveling everything south of the river."

"How do you know it's a second wave?"

"Because the smoke from the first round was clearing. This is fresh. And it's further south than before. They're working their way outward."

Joel watched the smoke. Thought about the house on Mockingbird Lane. The kitchen where Sarah ate breakfast. The garage where his truck sat with the bad starter and the cracked windshield. The watch Sarah had given him for his birthday, the one with the broken face, the one he'd left on his nightstand because he was going to get it fixed next week and next week would never come.

All of it was ash now. Or would be by tonight.

He didn't say anything about it. There was nothing to say. You couldn't grieve for a house when the world was ending. You couldn't mourn a kitchen when your daughter was alive four miles north, sleeping on a stranger's couch with a bullet wound in her hip. Priorities. Joel understood priorities.

"Let's get back," he said.

---

## — Marcus —

They walked north. The sun climbed. The smoke on the southern horizon grew taller and wider, a column that leaned east in the upper winds and spread out like a bruise on the sky. Neither of them talked about it. Joel walked with his jaw set and his eyes forward and his hand near the gun. Marcus walked beside him and tried not to think about what was under that smoke. Neighborhoods. Schools. The coffee shop on South Congress where Marcus Webb had apparently gotten his morning coffee before Marcus the transmigrant had taken over his body and his life.

People. Under that smoke were people who hadn't gotten out.

A notification blinked.

**[AREA MAP UPDATE]**
**[Local area clear. No infected within 5-mile radius.]**
**[Nearest cluster: 14 miles south. Stationary.]**

Clean. The immediate area was clean. The three Runners had been the vanguard, and now the vanguard was dead on a stranger's living room carpet. The farmhouse had bought itself another few days. Maybe a week. Maybe more, depending on how fast the infection spread through rural Texas.

Marcus dismissed the map and glanced at Joel. The man was walking with the same relentless pace he'd set on Outbreak Night, when he'd carried Sarah for four hours without stopping. A machine. A machine made of stubbornness and dad energy and the kind of physical toughness that came from decades of swinging hammers in Texas heat.

"You fight pretty well for a contractor," Marcus said.

Joel didn't look at him. "You fight pretty well for a tech support guy."

"Touché."

They crossed the dry creek bed. Joel went over the fence first, holding the top wire down for Marcus. A small courtesy. Joel wouldn't have done that two days ago.

"Three of them came from the southeast," Joel said. "That means there's more where they came from."

"There are. Further south. A lot further."

"How many?"

Marcus considered lying. Considered downplaying. Looked at Joel's face, at the set of his jaw and the clarity in his eyes, and decided that Joel Miller deserved the truth when the truth was relevant.

"Hundreds. Maybe thousands. The cities are gone, Joel. Austin, San Antonio, everything in the metro corridor. The infection has those areas. What we're seeing up here is the spillover. Stragglers that wander out of the population centers."

Joel was quiet for a few strides. "So it's gonna keep happening. More of 'em driftin' up."

"Yeah. Less and less as we get further from the cities. But yeah."

"Then we can't stay at the farmhouse forever."

"No. But we can stay long enough for Sarah to heal. A few more weeks. I'll keep the perimeter clear."

"We'll keep the perimeter clear," Joel corrected.

Marcus looked at him. Joel wasn't looking back. He was watching the horizon, the line of fence posts marching north toward the farmhouse, the windmill turning in the late morning breeze. But the correction was deliberate. We. Not you.

"Okay," Marcus said. "We."

...

They came over the last rise around noon. The farmhouse sat in its shallow valley, white walls and red barn, the windmill spinning, the plywood patches on the side windows looking like band-aids on a face. Tommy was on the front porch with the handgun on his knee. He stood up when he saw them.

"Three down," Joel said as they reached the gate. "Southeast. About four miles out. House and property, cleared."

Tommy looked at Joel. Looked at the blood on Joel's shirt, the smear still on his cheek that he hadn't fully cleaned. Looked at Marcus's wrapped forearm.

"You alright?"

"Scratches," Marcus said. "Not a bite. Already cleaned."

Tommy nodded. Something in his posture loosened. Not much. Enough.

"Sarah's been up since nine," Tommy said. "She ate breakfast. She's been asking where you went."

Joel went inside without another word. The screen door banged shut behind him. Marcus could hear Sarah's voice, small and sharp, asking questions before Joel made it across the living room.

Tommy stayed on the porch. He looked at Marcus with an expression that Marcus was starting to recognize. The Tommy expression. Part curiosity, part caution, part something that might eventually become trust if Marcus didn't do anything catastrophic between now and then.

"How'd he do?" Tommy asked.

"Joel?" Marcus sat down on the porch steps. His legs were tired. Four miles out, four miles back, plus the fight. Not much by objective standards, but his body was a 24-year-old office worker's body with only three days of apocalypse conditioning. "He did great. Took two of them down while I was still dealing with one. He's scary with a knife."

Tommy almost smiled. "He's been scary with a knife since we were kids. Used to whittle these little animals out of scrap wood on the porch. Horses, dogs, birds. Hands like a surgeon. Then you put somethin' dangerous in those hands and..." He trailed off. Shrugged. "He's Joel."

"Yeah," Marcus said. "He is."

He leaned back against the porch post and opened his status. Quietly. Just a glance.

**[Name: Marcus Webb]**
**[Points: 527]**
**[Health: 84%]**
**[Achievements Updated: Streak 3 — Complete]**
**[Next Achievement: Streak 5 — Kill 5 infected in a single outing]**

Five hundred and twenty-seven points. Climbing. Slowly, but climbing. Another outing like this every few days and he'd be in the six hundreds by the time Sarah could walk. By the time they left the farmhouse, he might have enough for something real. A water filtration system. A generator. Building blocks for whatever came next.

The screen door opened. Sarah was in the doorway, leaning on the frame, her weight on her left leg. She was wearing one of the dead homeowner's t-shirts, a faded blue thing that said FREDERICKSBURG PEACH FESTIVAL 2011 and hung on her like a dress. Her hair was pulled back in a ponytail. She looked better. Color in her face. Steadier on her feet.

"You went out to kill those things," she said. Not a question.

"Yeah."

"Without telling me."

"You were asleep."

"I was asleep because it was six in the morning. Normal people are asleep at six in the morning."

"I'll send a memo next time."

Sarah's mouth twitched. The almost-smile. The Joel twitch.

"Dad says you knew exactly where they were." Her eyes were doing the thing again. The cataloging thing. Adding another data point to her growing file on Marcus Webb, the man who didn't add up. "He says you walked right to them like you had a map."

Marcus looked at the porch boards between his feet. A line of ants was marching across a crack in the wood, carrying something white and crumbling toward a gap in the foundation. Busy. Purposeful. Completely indifferent to the end of the world.

"I'm observant," he said.

"You're something," Sarah said. "I haven't figured out what yet."

She turned and shuffled back inside. The screen door swung shut. Marcus watched the ants and listened to the windmill and thought about the plan. The dam. Colorado. The years ahead. The things he could build if the points kept coming. The people he could save if he played it right.

The smoke on the southern horizon was thinning now, spreading into the upper atmosphere and mixing with ordinary clouds until you couldn't tell which was smoke and which was weather. Austin was down there. What was left of it. A crater where a city used to be, where a man named Marcus Webb used to drink coffee and go to work and live a life that the real Marcus would never know.

He closed the system and went inside.

Joel was sitting at the kitchen table with Sarah, his arm around her shoulders, Tommy across from them eating cold chili straight from the can. The afternoon light came through the front window in long gold bars. The SIG was on the table next to Joel's elbow. Close enough to grab.

"Pull up a chair," Tommy said. "There's more chili."

Marcus pulled up a chair. He ate cold chili from a can and listened to Sarah tell Joel about a book she'd found on the bedroom shelf, a Louis L'Amour western about a man who builds a ranch in hostile territory and defends it against all comers.

"It's pretty good," she said. "The guy's kind of like you, Dad. Stubborn. Won't leave even when everyone says he should."

"Sounds like a smart man," Joel said.

"I said stubborn, not smart."

Tommy snorted. Joel's mouth did the thing where it wasn't quite a smile. Marcus ate his chili and let the moment sit, small and warm and ordinary, the four of them at a table in a dead stranger's kitchen, eating cold food and talking about books while the world burned to the south.

It wasn't much. But it was enough. For now, it was enough.
