# Chapter 1: New Game Plus

The ceiling fan was broken.

That was the first thing he noticed. A cheap white fan with faux-wood blades, hanging still and crooked from a popcorn ceiling. One blade had a crack running through it. The light fixture in the center was missing its glass cover, bare bulb exposed.

He didn't own a ceiling fan.

He sat up too fast and the world tilted. The room smelled like stale cigarettes and old carpet. A TV stand with no TV. A couch he was lying on, brown corduroy, lumpy in the middle. Beer cans on a coffee table. A window with bent blinds letting in the orange glow of a streetlight.

This was not his house.

"What the..." He swung his legs off the couch and his feet hit carpet that was slightly damp. His hands were wrong. Too rough, too tan. He turned them over and stared at knuckles that weren't his. Short nails, a scar across the left thumb, calluses on the palms.

He stood up and nearly fell. His center of gravity was off. He was taller than he should have been, and heavier, and his legs didn't move the way he expected. He caught himself on the arm of the couch and just stood there, breathing, trying to make sense of something that didn't make any sense at all.

A bathroom. He needed a mirror.

He found one down a short hallway, past a bedroom with an unmade bed and clothes on the floor. The bathroom was small and yellow-tiled and the mirror above the sink had a crack in the upper corner. He flipped the light switch and a fluorescent tube buzzed to life.

A stranger looked back at him.

A teenager. Fourteen, maybe fifteen. Brown hair, a little long, pushed to one side. A face that was average in every way. No scars except a small one near the right eyebrow. Brown eyes. Skinny in the way kids are before they finish growing. He was wearing a grey t-shirt and jeans that weren't his.

He gripped the edge of the sink and leaned in close. The reflection's mouth moved when his did. The reflection's eyes blinked when his blinked.

"Okay," he said. The voice was wrong too. Deeper. A slight rasp. "Okay. That's... that's not great."

Then the blue light appeared.

It flickered into existence about two feet in front of his face, hovering in the air like a projection with no projector. A translucent rectangle, glowing faintly, with clean white text:

**[SYSTEM INITIALIZING...]**

**[WELCOME, PLAYER.]**

**[YOU HAVE BEEN SELECTED FOR NEW GAME PLUS.]**

**[TUTORIAL WILL BEGIN IN 60 SECONDS.]**

He stared at it for a long time. Long enough that the number ticked down to 47 before he blinked.

"No way."

He reached out and touched the notification. His fingers passed through it, but the text pulsed brighter in response, like it registered the contact. A second later, a new line appeared:

**[PLEASE PROCEED TO A SAFE LOCATION FOR TUTORIAL.]**

"I'm in a bathroom," he said to the floating text. "How much safer do you want?"

The system didn't respond to that.

He walked back to the living room on shaky legs. The house was small, clearly lived in by whoever this body belonged to. A family's house, except it felt empty. He found a school ID on the kitchen counter next to a set of keys. Travis Heights Middle School. The name said Marcus Webb. Eighth grade. Date of birth: March 15, 1999.

Austin, Texas.

He set the wallet down slowly.

On the kitchen counter next to it was a newspaper. He almost missed it because who reads newspapers anymore, but this one was sitting right there, folded to the front page. The Austin American-Statesman. He picked it up and read the date in the upper right corner.

September 27, 2013.

The house went very quiet. Or maybe it had been quiet the whole time and he just now noticed it. The hum of the refrigerator. A clock ticking somewhere. And underneath it, far away, something that might have been a siren.

September 27, 2013. Austin, Texas.

"No," he said. "No, no, no."

He knew this date. Every fan of The Last of Us knew this date. It was Outbreak Day. The day the Cordyceps fungus jumped to humans, the day civilization started its twenty-year death spiral, the day a twelve-year-old girl named Sarah Miller got shot by a soldier and died in her father's arms.

He dropped the newspaper.

The system tutorial popped up in his vision, calm and clean and completely indifferent to his existential crisis:

**[TUTORIAL — THE GAMER SYSTEM]**

**[You have been granted the Gamer System as a transmigration benefit. Core features:]**

**[1. POINTS — Earned by eliminating Infected. Points scale with threat level.]**
**[2. THE MALL — A system shop where Points can be exchanged for items, equipment, and enhancements.]**
**[3. STATUS — View your current attributes and point balance at any time.]**

**[Say or think "Status" to open your character sheet.]**
**[Say or think "Mall" to open the shop.]**

**[Tutorial complete. Good luck, Player.]**

"That's the tutorial? That's the whole thing?" He stared at the fading text. "Three bullet points and a good luck?"

The system did not elaborate.

"Status," he said, feeling ridiculous.

A new panel appeared:

**[Name: Marcus Webb]**
**[Age: 14]**
**[Points: 0]**
**[Health: 100%]**
**[Attributes: Strength 6, Agility 5, Constitution 6, Perception 5, Intelligence 7, Willpower 6]**
**[Skills: None]**
**[Evaluation: Average civilian. No combat experience. Good luck.]**

"Did the system just roast me?"

He closed the panel with a thought. It dismissed instantly, like swiping away a phone notification. At least the interface was intuitive.

"Mall."

The shop opened up like a catalog. Categories floated in neat columns: Food & Water, Medicine, Tools, Weapons, Building Materials, Vehicles, Enhancements. He tapped on Weapons out of curiosity. A list of items appeared with prices. A basic kitchen knife: 3 points. A baseball bat: 5. A hunting rifle: 150. A handgun: 100.

He had zero points. He closed the Mall.

...

Outside, the sirens were getting louder. Not one siren. Several.

He went to the window and pulled the bent blinds apart with two fingers. The street was a typical Austin suburb. Ranch-style houses, big trees, a pickup truck parked on a lawn. Warm night, late September. A dog was barking somewhere down the block, frantic and continuous, the kind of barking that meant something was really wrong.

A car drove past too fast, headlights cutting across the houses. Then another one, going the same direction. A horn blared.

He let the blinds fall back.

"Okay. Think. Think."

September 27, 2013. Outbreak Night. The infection would have been spreading through contaminated food first, flour and grain products shipped from South American crops. People who ate the wrong bread, the wrong cereal, the wrong cake from the wrong bakery. They'd be turning now, or in the next few hours. The first Runners would be hitting the streets. Emergency services wouldn't know what they were dealing with. By morning the military would be shooting civilians. By next week, half the country would be gone.

And somewhere in this city, in a house maybe a few blocks or a few miles from here, a girl named Sarah Miller was about to die.

He turned away from the window and started moving through the house. He needed shoes. He found a pair of work boots by the front door, worn in but solid. They fit. He grabbed a hoodie from the bedroom closet and pulled it on. It was still warm outside but the hoodie had pockets and pockets meant you could carry things.

In the kitchen he found a flashlight in a junk drawer, half-charged. A folding knife, the cheap gas station kind. He shoved both in his pockets.

"This is insane," he muttered, lacing the boots. "This is actually insane. I'm in The Last of Us. I have a gamer system. The world is ending in about..." he checked the clock on the stove. 9:47 PM. "...the world is ending right now, actually."

He stood up and went back to the window. More cars now, moving fast. A house across the street had its lights on, and through their window he could see a family watching TV. They didn't know yet. Most of them wouldn't know until it was too late.

The question that mattered was simple: where was the Miller house from here?

He didn't know Austin. He'd never been to Texas. But the game started in a suburban neighborhood on the north side of the city, and this house was in a suburb, and the newspaper on the counter had a little weather box that said "North Austin, TX" at the top.

He needed a phone. Or a map. Or a phone book, because this was 2013 and people still had those.

He found Marcus Webb's phone on the kitchen counter, an iPhone 5 with 34% battery and no passcode. He opened Maps and typed "Miller" into the search bar, then stopped. Joel Miller wasn't going to show up on Google Maps. He didn't even know the street name. In the game it was never specified. Just a suburb. Trees and sidewalks and that one road where the car crashed.

He tried a different approach. The news. He opened Safari and searched for Austin news. The top results were normal. UT football. City council stuff. A restaurant review.

Then he scrolled down and found a breaking news alert from four minutes ago:

*"REPORTS OF DISTURBANCES IN SOUTH AND CENTRAL AUSTIN — MULTIPLE 911 CALLS — AUSTIN PD ADVISES RESIDENTS TO REMAIN INDOORS"*

Four minutes ago. It was starting.

His hands were shaking. Not a little tremble, but a visible shake, the phone screen wobbling in his grip. He set it down on the counter and pressed both palms flat on the surface and breathed.

He could stay here. Lock the doors, push the couch against the front one, fill the bathtub with water, and wait. Marcus Webb's house was small but solid. He could survive the night. Farm some Runners tomorrow when things calmed down. Build up points slowly and carefully.

Sarah Miller would die.

Joel would carry her body. He would feel her go still in his arms. And twenty years later, a broken man would lie to a girl named Ellie and set off a chain of violence that would end with a golf club in a ski lodge.

He picked the phone back up.

"Okay, Sarah," he said to nobody. "Where do you live?"

He didn't know the address. But he knew the neighborhood. Suburban, north Austin, houses with yards, close to a highway overpass. He pulled up the map and started looking at the neighborhoods around his current location. The blue dot put him on Rundberg Lane. The Millers lived somewhere in the sprawl of subdivisions between here and the highway.

A crash from outside. Glass breaking. He looked up from the phone and heard someone screaming. Not far. Maybe two blocks away. A woman's voice, high and ragged, the kind of scream that didn't stop for breath.

Then it cut off.

He shoved the phone in his pocket and grabbed the folding knife. It felt pathetic in his hand. Three inches of cheap steel against a world full of things that used to be people.

"This is the worst idea I've ever had," he said, and opened the front door.

____

The street outside was chaos. He locked the front door behind him and moved fast, cutting across the yard toward the sidewalk.

He could see the road clearly. More cars now, a steady stream heading north. A fire truck went past with its sirens screaming. Across the street, the family he'd seen through their window was now on their porch, the father shielding his eyes against a distant orange glow.

Something was burning. South of here, toward downtown, the sky had an ugly smudge of light against the clouds. Not a house fire. Bigger than that.

He pushed through the lobby door and stepped outside. The air hit him first. Warm, humid, that particular Texas heat that didn't quit even after dark. It smelled like car exhaust and something else underneath it, something sour and organic that he couldn't identify.

A man ran past on the sidewalk, barefoot, in pajama pants and nothing else. He didn't even look at Marcus. Just running.

"Hey!" Marcus called after him. "Hey, what's happening?"

The man didn't stop.

Three more cars blew through the intersection at the end of the block, one of them running the stop sign. He could hear more sirens now, layered on top of each other, coming from every direction. A helicopter thumped overhead, searchlight sweeping across the rooftops two streets over.

He started walking. Then jogging. The phone's map showed a maze of residential streets between here and the highway, and the Miller house could be in any one of those subdivisions. He didn't have time to search every block. He needed to narrow it down.

In the game, the Millers' street had a specific feel. A wide residential road. Houses with front lawns. A cul-de-sac nearby. Joel's neighbor's house was where things first went wrong, where the infected neighbor crashed through the glass door.

He ran south along Rundberg, past a gas station where every pump was occupied and people were shouting at each other. Past a strip mall with a Whataburger and a Check Into Cash. Past a church parking lot where a group of people stood in a circle, praying.

His phone buzzed. A text from someone named "Dave" in Marcus Webb's contacts:

*"dude are you seeing this?? they're saying it's some kind of disease. stay inside"*

He didn't reply.

Another notification, this time from the system:

**[QUEST RECEIVED]**

**[Save Sarah Miller]**
**[Sarah Miller will be fatally wounded tonight unless you intervene.]**
**[Reward: 500 Points, Trait — Guardian]**
**[Time Remaining: URGENT]**

He almost tripped over his own feet. The quest hung in his vision, glowing soft blue, and the word URGENT pulsed like a heartbeat.

"Where?" he asked the system. "Which direction?"

No response. No quest marker. No minimap with a golden arrow. Just the notification and the word URGENT and the sound of Austin tearing itself apart around him.

"Of course," he panted, still running. "Of course there's no quest marker. That would be too easy."

He cut through a neighborhood. Residential streets, houses with lights on, people on porches and in driveways. Some were packing cars. Some were arguing. A woman dragged a suitcase across her lawn while her kids stood by the car crying. Somebody's dog had gotten loose and was bolting down the middle of the road.

Then he heard it.

A sound that didn't belong in any neighborhood, in any city, in any world that still made sense. A wet, gurgling shriek, halfway between a scream and a cough, and it came from a house on the corner with its front door hanging open.

A Runner burst out of that open door.

It moved wrong. Everything about it was wrong. The way it ran, all jerky limbs and no coordination, like a marionette being yanked forward by invisible strings. It was a man, or it had been. Middle-aged, Hawaiian shirt, one shoe. His face was contorted, mouth open too wide, and there was blood on his chin and his hands and smeared down the front of his shirt.

The Runner saw Marcus from thirty yards away. Its head snapped toward him like a dog catching a scent.

He ran.

He ran harder than he'd ever run in his life, his original life, the life where the worst thing that could happen was a bad quarterly review. The Runner was fast. He could hear it behind him, bare feet slapping pavement, that horrible gurgling getting closer.

He vaulted a chain-link fence and the Runner hit it a second later, clawing at the metal. It didn't climb. It just threw itself at the fence, again and again, rattling the whole thing. He backed away, gasping, watching it through the links.

Its eyes were bloodshot and wrong. Not blank, not empty. Furious. Like something had scooped out the person and replaced them with nothing but rage.

After ten seconds it turned and ran toward the sound of a car horn, losing interest.

He bent over and put his hands on his knees and tried not to throw up.

"I'm in The Last of Us," he whispered. "I'm actually in The Last of Us."

The quest notification pulsed again. URGENT.

He straightened up and kept moving. Past backyards and fences and barking dogs and the sound of gunshots, closer now, maybe four or five blocks south. Past a house where every light was on and an old man stood on his porch with a shotgun. Past a playground where the swings moved in the breeze and nobody was playing.

And then, rounding a corner onto a wide residential street with big front lawns and mature oak trees, he saw it. A house with a warm yellow porch light. A truck in the driveway. A soccer ball on the front lawn.

He knew that truck. He knew that porch.

He knew this street.

The Miller house was dark inside now. The front door was open. Across the street, the neighbor's house had a hole where the sliding glass door used to be, glass scattered across the patio. There was blood on the sidewalk out front.

They were already gone. Joel, Tommy, and Sarah had already left. The neighbor had already turned. The clock was ticking.

He pulled out the phone and checked the time. 10:23 PM.

In the game, Sarah gets shot on the highway, running from FEDRA soldiers. Joel carries her. Tommy shoots the soldier, but it's too late.

He didn't know exactly when that happened. But it was tonight, and it was soon, and the highway was that direction, south of here, where the sirens were loudest and the sky glowed orange.

He started running again.

The quest pulsed: URGENT.

"I know," he gasped. "I know, I know, I know."

The streets blurred past. More Runners now, scattered, chasing screaming civilians or stumbling out of houses with that horrible jerking gait. He dodged them, cut through yards, jumped fences. His lungs burned. His legs burned. Marcus Webb's body was in okay shape but not built for sprinting a mile through an apocalypse.

Ahead, through the trees and past the rooftops, he could see the highway overpass. Headlights crawling bumper to bumper. And beyond it, the unmistakable strobe of military vehicles.

Sarah was out there somewhere. Bleeding. Running out of time.

He ran faster.
